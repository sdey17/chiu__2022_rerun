Abstract

   The biological half-life (t1/2) of perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) in male rats is 70 times
longer than that in female rats. The difference is mainly due to the difference in renal
clearance (CLR), which was significantly reduced by probenecid, suggesting that PFOA is
excreted by organic anion transporter(s). Castration of male rats caused a 14-fold increase in
the CLR of PFOA, which made it comparable with that of female rats. The elevated PFOA
CLR in castrated males was reduced by treating them with testosterone. Treatment of male
rats with estradiol increased the CLR of PFOA. In female rats, ovariectomy caused a
significant increase in CLR of PFOA, which was reduced by estradiol treatment. Treatments
of female rats with testosterone reduced the CLR of PFOA as observed in castrated male
rats. To identify the transporter molecules that are responsible for PFOA transport in rat
kidney, renal mRNA levels of organic anion transporter 1 (OAT1), OAT2, OAT3, organic
anion transporting polypeptide 1 (oatp1), oatp2 and kidney specific organic anion trans-
porter (OAT-K) were determined in male and female rats under various hormonal states and
compared with the CLR of PFOA. The level of OAT2 mRNA in male rats was only 13% that
in female rats. Castration or estradiol treatment increased the level of OAT2 mRNA whereas
treatment of castrated male rats with testosterone reduced it. In contrast to OAT2, mRNA
levels of both oatp1 and OAT-K were significantly higher in male rats compared with female

1. Introduction

   Perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) is a potent synthetic surfactant that is used in a
variety of industrial processes [1]. This chemical has been identified as a major
contributor to organic fluorine in human serum [2], and the subsequent studies have
revealed that serum levels of organic fluorine in plant workers occupationally
exposed to this chemical were 10- to 50-fold higher than that in the general public
[3]. The biological half-life (t1/2) in humans has been estimated to be 18–24 months
following analysis of the serum from a plant worker [3].
   The effects of PFOA on biological systems have been extensively studied in
rodents. PFOA causes peroxisome proliferation as well as induction of various
enzymes involved in lipid metabolism [4–8]. Toxic effects of PFOA have been
reported, such as induction of fatty liver [5] and uncoupling of the mitochondrial
respiratory chain [9]. PFOA also affects the serum levels of various hormones, i.e.
reducing thyroid hormone and testosterone, and increasing estradiol in rats [10,11].
Although these findings suggest that PFOA affects hormonal states and metabolism
of lipids in human, to date, no significant correlation has been observed between
serum levels of PFOA and any biological parameters in plant workers [12–14].
   PFOA, once absorbed by the body, is predominantly eliminated in urine and the
rate of fecal elimination is far less than that of urinary elimination in rats [15,16].
Urinary elimination, therefore, is critical for detoxification of PFOA. We and other
investigators have shown that urinary elimination of PFOA is markedly faster in
female rats compared with male rats [15–19]. There are also significant sex
differences in the metabolism of some chemicals and drugs [20–22]. Since PFOA is
metabolically inert [16,23,24], sex-related difference observed in the biological
effects of PFOA is not due to a difference in metabolism of PFOA. In the previous
study, we observed that perfluorocarboxylic acid (PFCA) with a shorter carbon
chain length was more rapidly eliminated in urine [15]. This suggests that PFCA are
recognized and transported into urine by certain biological systems, although little
is known about the molecular mechanism responsible for PFOA transport. Re-
cently, several transporters have been cloned, by which various organic anions are
transported across biological membranes, in various tissues including liver, kidney,
intestine and brain [25– 33]. These transporters have broad substrate specificities
and transport various drugs as well as endogenous substrates [34]. Regulation of
the expression of these transporters by sex hormones, however, has not been fully
investigated yet. We have hypothesized, therefore, that PFOA is transported into

urine by a transporter whose expression is regulated by sex hormones. In the
present study, we investigated in detail the activities of renal PFOA transport under
various hormonal states in male and female rats. Furthermore, the activities were
compared with renal levels of mRNA of various organic anion transporters to
identify potential candidates for PFOA transporters.


2. Materials and methods

2.1. Materials

   PFOA was purchased from Sigma Aldrich Japan (Tokyo, Japan). Inulin and
probenecid were purchased from Sigma Chemical Co. (St. Louis, MO). Testos-
terone propionate and b-estradiol-3-benzoate were purchased from Wako Pure
Chemical Ind. (Osaka, Japan). All other chemicals were of analytical grade.
3-Bromoacetyl-7-methoxycoumarin (BrAMC) was synthesized as described previ-
ously [35].

2.2. Animals

   Male and female Wistar rats were obtained from SLC (Hamamatsu, Japan) and
subjected to experiments when 9 weeks old (male rats, 240– 320 g; female rats,
190– 235 g) after acclimatization for at least 1 week. All animals were acclimatized
in a humidity- and temperature (239 2 °C)-controlled environment with a 12-h
light:12-h dark cycle. Animals had free access to water and laboratory chow (CE-2,
Clea Japan). Some male and female rats were castrated and ovariectomized,
respectively, at 6 weeks of age under sodium pentobarbital anesthesia (36 mg/kg
body weight). Two weeks after surgery, half of them received daily subcutaneous
administration of testosterone propionate (10 mg/kg body weight) for 5 days or
b-estradiol-3-benzoate (0.2 mg/kg body weight) for 7 days. These hormones were
dissolved in corn oil (1 ml/kg body weight). The day following the final injection,
these rats had their renal clearance (CLR) of PFOA determined or the mRNA levels
of various organic anion transporters were determined. For the extraction of RNA,
kidneys were quickly excised under light ether anesthesia, frozen in liquid nitrogen
and stored at − 80 °C until use.

2.3. Plasma concentration profile

   Male and female rats underwent surgical catheter (SP-45) implantation via the
femoral vein for drug administration and blood sampling under sodium pentobarbi-
tal anesthesia (36 mg/kg body weight). These rats were allowed to recover for 3
days after surgery. The catheter was flushed daily with 0.028% heparinized saline to
maintain patency. PFOA, dissolved in 0.9% NaCl after the addition of equimolar
NaOH, was injected into the femoral vein (48.63 mmol/kg body weight) via a
catheter, and then 0.2 ml of blood was withdrawn from the femoral vein catheter
304           N. Kudo et al. / Chemico-Biological Interactions 139 (2002) 301–316

at 5 min, 1, 6, 12 h, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10 and 12 days after injection. A
two-compartment open model was used for the toxicokinetic analysis of PFOA.
Toxicokinetic parameters (total clearance, t1/2, and volume of distribution) from
each rat were calculated using the curve-fitting program ‘MULTI’ [36].

2.4. Determination of renal clearance

   The CLR of PFOA was determined for four groups of male rats (control,
castrated, castrated+testosterone, estradiol) and four groups of female rats (con-
trol, castrated, castrated+estradiol, testosterone) with three to six animals in each
group. Rats underwent surgical catheter (SP-45) implantation via the femoral vein
under urethane anesthesia and then placed in a restrainer. Ten percent (w/v)
mannitol/0.9% (w/v) NaCl was infused at 0.1 ml/min throughout the experiment to
increase urination. Excretion of urine was increased by the infusion and reached a
constant rate of about 0.1 ml/min 1.5 h after starting the infusion. To determine
inulin clearance, inulin was also dissolved in mannitol/NaCl solution at the
concentration of 2 mg/ml. Two hours after the start of mannitol infusion, these rats
were injected with PFOA (48.63 mmol/kg body weight) via the femoral vein, and
blood samples were withdrawn at 5, 15, 35, 70, 120, 200 and 300 min after injection.
Urine samples were collected into glass tubes through a funnel attached to the
restrainer. The tubes were changed at 2.5, 7.5, 22.5, 47.5, 92.5, 147.5 and 252.5 min
after injection. Blood-sampling points were the midpoints of the urine collection
periods. Plasma and urine samples were stored at −30 °C for the determination of
PFOA and inulin. Inulin concentrations in urine and plasma samples were deter-
mined by the method of Dische and Borenfreund [37]. To determine the effects of
probenecid on the CLR of PFOA, probenecid was intravenously injected (53
mmol/kg body weight) before starting the infusion of mannitol/NaCl solution.
Then, probenecid, dissolved in the mannitol/NaCl solution at a concentration of 5.3
mmol/ml, was infused throughout the experiment. The CLR of inulin and PFOA
was calculated by CLR (ml/min) = inulin or PFOA in urine 0 – 300 min/AUC0 – 300 min.
The AUC0 – 300 min was calculated by the trapezoidal rule.

2.5. Determination of PFOA

   PFOA concentrations in plasma and urine were determined as described previ-
ously [35]. In brief, PFOA was extracted from an aliquot of plasma or urine, with
ethyl acetate: hexane (1:1, v/v) as an ion-pair with tetrabuthylammonium in the
presence of NaCO3 buffer (pH 10) after addition of a known amount of perfuoro-
hexanoic acid as an internal standard. The extract was taken to dryness under
nitrogen and the residue was mixed with a 0.2% solution of 3-bromoacetyl-7-
methoxycoumarin in acetone to obtain 3-acetyl-7-methoxycoumarine derivatives of
PFOA and perfluorohexanoic acid. The mixture was heated at 70 °C for 25 min
followed by cooling at − 20 °C for 2 h. The reaction mixture was filtered, then
subjected to HPLC using a reverse-phase column (Wakosil-II 3C-18, 4.6 mm
ID ×50 mm, Wako Pure Chemicals, Osaka, Japan) and eluted with a mixture of
               N. Kudo et al. / Chemico-Biological Interactions 139 (2002) 301–316   305

acetonitrile: water (140:60, v/v) at 50 °C. The PFCA peaks were detected by a
fluorescent detector (Shimadzu, RF-10AXL) at 316 excitation and 419 nm emission.



2.6. Determination of renal mRNA le6els of organic anion transporters by
RT-PCR

   Total RNA was isolated from kidneys by the single-step guadinium thiocyanate
procedure using RNeasy RNA extraction kit (Qiagen, Germany). The concentra-
tion of RNA was estimated by measuring the UV absorption at 260 nm. Poly(A)+
RNA was purified by oligo d(T)-Latex (Oligotex™-dT30 B super\ , Takara Shuzo,
Japan). The reverse transcription-polymarase chain reaction (RT-PCR) was com-
pleted on poly(A)+ RNA using AMV reverse transcriptase XL (Takara Shuzo,
Japan). RT was performed using 0.25 U/ml RT enzyme, 1 mg poly(A)+ RNA
combined with 0.125 mM of oligo d(T) primer, 10 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.3), 50 mM
KCl, 5 mM MgCl2 and 1 mM deoxynucleotide triphosphates (dNTPs). Amplifica-
tion of the complementary DNA (cDNA) product was performed using gene-spe-
cific primers (100 nM each) in 8 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.3), 40 mM KCl, 1.5 mM
MgCl2, and 0.8 mM dNTPs. The sequences of gene-specific primers are shown in
Table 1. PCR was performed at 94 °C for 1 min, 63 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30
s with Taq polymerase (Takara Shuzo, Japan). PCR was performed for 19, 23, 21,
21, 31, 21 and 17 cycles on organic anion transporter 1 (OAT1), OAT2, OAT3,
organic anion transporting polypeptide 1 (oatp1), oatp2, OAT-K (OAT-K) and
glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), respectively. An aliquot of
the reaction mixture was subjected to 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. This primer
set gave single PCR products whose molecular size corresponded to the calculated
size. The sequences of amplified products, determined by DNA sequencer (ABI
PRISM 310, Applied Biosystems, USA), were consistent with those of their
respective cDNA [26,28– 33]. The PCR product was subjected to agarose gel
electrophoresis (1.5%), stained with ethidium bromide, and then analyzed by
gel-imaging system Epi-Light UV2000 with Luminous Imager software (Aisin
Cosmos, Japan). The intensity of the clone-specific PCR product was calculated
relative to that of GAPDH. Linear correlations were obtained between the loga-
rithm of relative intensities of each transporter and the amounts of poly(A+)RNA
ranging from 125 ng to 2 mg, except for OAT2 prepared from female rats. Linear
amplification was obtained for the above-mentioned cycles9 1 cycle. The results
are shown as the expression relative to control male rats.



2.7. Statistical analyses

  Analysis of variance was used to test the significance of the difference between
four experimental groups of male or female rats. When differences were significant,
the statistical significance between any two means was determined using Shèffe’s
306             N. Kudo et al. / Chemico-Biological Interactions 139 (2002) 301–316

multiple range test. Statistically significant differences between male and female rats
were analyzed by Student’s t-test or Welch’s test after F-test for two means.

3.4. Regulation by sex hormones of renal mRNA expression of organic anion
transporters

   The marked inhibition by probenecid of the CLR of PFOA suggests that PFOA
is secreted into urine via organic anion transporter(s) in the renal tubules. Recent
studies have revealed that there are two major groups of organic anion transporters
that transport wide variety of organic anions [25–33]: the organic anion transporter
(OAT) family and organic anion transporting polypeptide (oatp) family. The
former includes OAT1, OAT2 and OAT3 and the latter includes oatp1, oatp2,
OAT-K1 and OAT-K2 [34]. The transporters belonging to the same family show a
high degree of homology [34]. To identify the organic anion transporters responsi-
ble for PFOA excretion, the renal expression of mRNA of several organic anion
transporters was determined in rats under different hormonal states by RT-PCR
(Figs. 3 and 4). The levels of OAT1 mRNA were not different between four
experimental groups of male rats (Fig. 3A) while the level of renal OAT1 mRNA
in female rats was slightly lower than that in male rats. A significant sex-related
difference was observed in the level of OAT2 mRNA (Fig. 3B). In male rats, the
level of OAT2 mRNA, which was only 13% that in female rats, was increased by
castration or estradiol treatment. Treatment of castrated male rats with testosterone
reduced OAT2 mRNA to the level of control male rats. In contrast with male rats,
neither ovariectomy nor testosterone treatment affected the level of OAT2 mRNA
in female rats. It is noteworthy that ovariectomy of female rats increased the level
of OAT3 mRNA (Fig. 3C). Fig. 4 shows the mRNA levels of the oatp family
(oatp1, oatp2 and OAT-K) in rat kidney. The level of oatp1 mRNA was 23 times
higher in male rats compared with female rats (Fig. 4A). In estradiol-treated male
rats, ovariectomized female rats, and ovariectomized female rats treated with
estradiol, no mRNA of oatp1 was detected by PCR for 21 cycles. In these samples,
faint bands were detected by amplification for 27 cycles. Castration or estradiol
treatment significantly reduced the renal level of oatp1 mRNA in male rats. This
was increased by treating female rats or castrated male rats with testosterone. No
significant difference was observed in the level of oatp2 mRNA between the eight
experimental groups (Fig. 4B). In the case of OAT-K, the expression of mRNA in
male rats was 2.5 times higher than in female rats (Fig. 4C). In male rats, castration
or estradiol treatment reduced the level of OAT-K mRNA, as was observed with
oatp1, whereas neither ovariectomy nor hormone treatment altered the level of
OAT-K mRNA in female rats.

4. Discussion

   Recent studies have shown that PFOA accumulates to a high degree in the
human body and the t1/2 was estimated to be 18–24 months [3]. Since this chemical
is widely used in industrial applications, it is plausible that long-term exposure to
this chemical results in its accumulation in humans, which may lead to toxic effects
in biological systems. PFOA is not metabolized in biological systems and is
eliminated in urine and feces as the free carboxylic acid in experimental animals
[16,23,24]. Therefore, the elimination of PFOA is responsible for its detoxification.

In a previous study, we have demonstrated that the hepatic concentration of
PFOA is low when PFOA is rapidly eliminated in urine [15] and that there is a
close relationship between the concentrations of PFOA and the activity of perox-
isomal b-oxidation in rat liver [38]. Fig. 5 shows that the CLR of PFOA is
inversely correlated with the potency of induction of peroxisomal b-oxidation
(data on peroxisomal b-oxidation derived from Kudo et al., [38]) in rat liver.
This fact suggests that the CLR of PFOA is a key determinant of the biological
action of PFOA in rats. The present study has revealed that the CLR of PFOA
is markedly influenced by sex hormones. It is obvious that testosterone reduces
the CLR of PFOA in both male and female rats. In contrast, the effects of
estradiol were more complicated; estradiol treatment increased the CLR of PFOA
in male rats, whereas it reduced the elevated CLR of PFOA in ovariectomized
female rats. These results suggest that at least two systems are responsible for
the renal transport of PFOA and these systems are differently regulated by sex
hormones. Although a sex-related difference in urinary elimination has been
reported for several chemicals and drugs [20,39–45], this has not been character-
ized in any detail. It seems important, therefore, to investigate the relationship
between the renal expression of various transporters and urinary elimination
under various hormonal states in order to understand the mechanism by which
these drugs and chemicals are eliminated into urine.
   Recently, clones of organic anion transporters transporting a wide variety of
organic anions have been isolated in rodents, humans and other animals [25–33].
PFOA may be transported by organic anion transporters since the urinary excre-

tion of PFOA is inhibited by probencid, an inhibitor of organic anion trans-
porters (Table 3). We hypothesized that renal expression of PFOA transporter is
essentially regulated by sex hormones. Therefore, the levels of mRNA of several
organic anion transporters were compared with the CLR of PFOA to identify the
transporters responsible for the renal PFOA transport in the present study.
   Several interesting findings were obtained with regard to the hormonal control
of renal mRNA expression of organic anion transporters. A significant sex-re-
lated difference was observed in renal expression of OAT2 (Fig. 3) and OAT-K
(Fig. 4) in addition to oatp1, consistent with the finding of Lu et al. [46] and
Meria et al. [47]. OAT2 had been shown to be expressed predominantly in the
liver, but only to a slight degree in the kidney of male rats [33]. However, we
found that renal expression in female rats was 7.5 times higher compared with
male rats. There was a close correlation between the CLR of PFOA and the level
of OAT2 mRNA in male rats, while the level was unchanged by castration or
the treatment with sex hormones in female rats. As for OAT3, the renal mRNA
level in castrated female rats was almost twice as high as that in other experi-
mental groups. Following multiple regression analysis, OAT2 and OAT3 posi-
tively correlated with the CLR of PFOA. The contribution of OAT3 seems to be
greater than that of OAT2, since the correlation coefficient of OAT3 was much
higher than that of OAT2. Histochemical studies have revealed that three trans-
porters of the OAT family are expressed in basolateral membranes of tubular
cells and transport organic anions from the circulation to tubular cells [34].
Accordingly, it seems likely that both OAT2 and OAT3 are responsible for the
basolateral uptake of PFOA in renal tubular cells.
   In contrast to the OAT family, three transporters of the oatp family have
been shown to be expressed in brush border membrane [34,48]. So far, oatp1 has
been thought to play a role in the uptake of organic anions and, therefore, to be
responsible for the re-absorption of these anions from urine [34]. Recent studies,
however, have suggested the possibility that oatp1 excludes organic anions from
cells [49]. Since the present study shows that there is an inverse correlation
between the mRNA level of oatp1 and the CLR of PFOA in male rats, but not
in female rats, it remains unclear whether oatp1 is responsible for the re-absorp-
tion of PFOA. Noe et al. [49] reported that oatp2 is predominantly expressed in
the liver and brain in rats. No significant difference in the eight experimental
groups in the present study suggests that this transporter does not play a crucial
role in the renal transport of PFOA.
   In conclusion, we have demonstrated that the CLR of PFOA and the renal
mRNA levels of some organic anion transporters are markedly affected by sex
hormones. Our results suggest that both OAT2 and OAT3 are responsible for
the urinary elimination of PFOA. This observation will lead to a better under-
standing of sex-related differences in the toxicokinetics of this chemical. The
present study suggests that PFOA, after absorption, distribution and accumula-
tion in humans, affects the transport of endogenous substrate as well as exoge-
nous chemicals such as drugs, resulting in toxic effects.
