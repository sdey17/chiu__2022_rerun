Abstract

   Elimination in urine and feces was compared between four perfluorinated fatty acids
(PFCAs) with different carbon chain length. In male rats, perfluoroheptanoic acid (PFHA)
was rapidly eliminated in urine with the proportion of 92% of the dose being eliminated
within 120 h after an intraperitoneal injection. Perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), pe-
rfluorononanoic acid (PFNA) and perfluorodecanoic acid (PFDA) was eliminated in urine
with the proportions of 55, 2.0 and 0.2% of the dose, respectively. By contrast, four PFCAs
were eliminated in feces with the proportion of less than 5% of the dose within 120 h after
an injection. In female rats, the proportions of PFOA and PFNA eliminated in urine within
120 h were 80% and 51% of the dose, respectively, which were significantly higher compared
with those in male rats. There was the tendency that PFCA with longer carbon chain length
is less eliminated in urine in both male and female rats. Fecal elimination of PFCAs was not
different between PFCAs in female rats and comparable to those in male rats. The rates of
biliary excretion of PFCAs in male rats were slower than those in female rats. Sex-related
difference in urinary elimination of PFOA was abolished when male rats had been castrated.
On the contrary, treatment with testosterone suppressed the elimination of PFOA in urine in
both castrated male rats and female rats. The effect of testosterone was in a time- and
dose-dependent manner. These results suggest that PFCAs are distinguished by their carbon

1. Introduction

   Perfluorinated fatty acids (PFCAs) are fatty acid derivatives in which all hy-
drogen atoms attached to carbon atoms are substituted by fluorine. These chemi-
cals were widely utilized as anti-wetting agents, plasticizers, corrosion inhibitors
and fire extinguishers [1,2].
   Perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and perfluorodecanoic acid (PFDA) are known
as peroxisome proliferators that induce peroxisome proliferation with various
peroxisomal enzymes [3 – 6] as well as a series of mitochondrial, microsomal and
cytosolic enzymes and proteins involved in lipid metabolism in rodent [5–14]. In
addition, these PFCAs showed various toxic effects on biological systems; accu-
mulation of triglyceride in liver [15 –18], uncoupling of mitochondrial respiratory
chain [19] and reduction of the level of thyroid hormone in circulation [20,21].
There is a significant sex-related difference in the effects of PFOA; upon the
repeated administration, PFOA markedly induced peroxisomal b-oxidation [22],
1-acylglycerophosphocholine acyltransferase [22], acyl-CoA hydrolase [22],
stearoyl-CoA desaturase [23], and carnitine acetyltransferase [24] in the livers of
male rats, while little induction was observed in female rats. The striking differ-
ence was explained by rapid elimination of PFOA in urine in female rats com-
pared to male rats [25 – 27]. As for the other PFCAs such as PFDA, however,
sex-related difference had not been well characterized. In the previous study, we
have extensively compared the potency of the induction of peroxisomal b-oxida-
tion between various PFCAs having 7-10 carbon atoms and between male and
female rats [28]. The results demonstrated that PFCA with longer carbon chain
length was more potent inducer of peroxisomal b-oxidation [28]. Interestingly,
hepatic concentration of perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA) was significantly differ-
ent between male and female rats after repeated administration of PFNA, as
observed in PFOA [28]. There was a significant relationship between the activity
of peroxisomal b-oxidation and hepatic concentrations of PFCAs irrespective of
number of carbon atoms and sex of rats [28]. These facts raised two questions;
(1) what is a key determinant of hepatic concentrations of PFCAs; and (2) why
sex-related difference was not observed in all PFCAs. To answer these questions,
it is necessary to clarify elimination route and rate of various PFCAs in male
and female rats, which must lead to better understanding the toxicity of these
PFCAs. In the present study, we demonstrated that PFCAs having less carbon
atoms were more quickly excreted into urine, resulting in lower concentrations in
serum and liver. Moreover, sex-related difference was observed not only in
PFOA but also in other PFCAs.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Materials

  Perfluoroheptanoic acid (PFHA), PFOA and PFDA were purchased from
Aldrich Japan (Tokyo, Japan); PFNA was from Lancaster Synthesis (Lancashire,
UK). All reagents were of analytical grade. 3-Bromoacetyl-7-methoxycoumarin
(BrAMC) was synthesized as described previously [29].

2.2. Animals

   Male and female Wistar rats of 5 weeks old were purchased from SLC (Hama-
matsu, Japan). After acclimatization for 1 week, rats were intraperitoneally admin-
istered with PFHA, PFOA, PFNA or PFDA that were dissolved into propylene
glycol: water (1:1, v/v) at a dose of 20 mg/ml/kg body weight. Rats were individu-
ally placed in metabolic cages, and urine and feces were collected every 12 h up to
5 days.
   Some of male rats were castrated at 3 weeks old, and 2 weeks after the castration,
the castrated rats were subcutenously injected with testosterone propionate with
corn oil as a vehicle (1 ml/kg body weight) at doses as indicated once a day for 4
days. Female rats of 6 weeks old were treated with testosterone propionate as
described above. These rats were intraperitoneally administered with PFOA 24 h
after the final treatment of testosterone, and urine samples were collected for 24 h
in metabolic cages.
   For the collection of bile, the common bile duct was cannulated with PE10
tubing under anesthesia with Nembutal. Rats were then intravenously injected with
PFHA, PFOA, PHNA or PFDA that were dissolved in serum (2 ml/kg body
weight) obtained from rats of the same sex at a dose of 25 mg/kg body weight and
placed in Bollman gauges. Bile samples were collected every 30 min up to 5 h after
the injection, weighed, and the amounts of PFCA were determined.
   To compare the rate of urinary excretion rates of various PFCAs, rats were
anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 25% (w/v) solution of urethane, and
femoral vein was cannulated with SP45 tubing. Then rats were placed in Bollman
gauges and continuously infused with 10% (w/v) mannitol in 0.9% (w/v) NaCl at a
rate of 0.1 ml/min during experiments. Two hours after starting infusion, rats were
intreavenously injected with PFHA, PFOA, PHNA or PFDA dissolved in serum as
described above at a dose of 25 mg/kg body weight. Urine samples were collected
every 10 min (1.0 – 2.0 ml of urine).

2.3. Determination of PFCAs

  Feces were weighed and then homogenized with nine volumes of water. PFCA
was extracted with ethyl acetate: acetone (1:1, v/v) from an aliquot of fecal
homogenates, urine or serum samples as an ion pair with tetrabutylammonium as
described previously [29]. For the determination of PFNA and PFDA, a known
206            N. Kudo et al. / Chemico-Biological Interactions 134 (2001) 203–216


amount of PFHA was added as an internal standard, and the known amounts of
perfluorohexanoic acid and PFDA were added as internal standards for the
measurement of PFOA and PFHA, respectively. The extract was taken to dryness
with N2 stream and to the residue was added the 0.2% solution of BrAMC in
acetone to obtain 3-acetyl-7-methoxycoumarin derivative of PFCA. The mixture
was incubated at 70°C for 25 min followed by cooling at − 20°C for 2 h. The
reaction mixture was filtered through glass-wool filter and applied to HPLC with a
reverse phase column (Wakosil-II 3C18, 4.6 mm ID× 50 mm, Wako Pure Chemi-
cals, Osaka, Japan). The mixture of acetonitrile and water (150:50, v/v, for PFOA
and PFNA or 140:60, for PFHA and PFDA, respectively) was used for a mobile
phase. The peaks of PFCAs were detected by a fluorescent detector (Shimadzu,
RF-10AXL) at 316 nm of excitation and 419 nm of emission.

2.4. Statistical analyses

   Analysis of variance was used to test the significance of differences between four
PFCAs and between male, female, castrated male, castrated male treated with
testosterone and testosterone-treated female. Where differences were significant, the
statistical significance between any two means was determined using Shèffe’s
multiple range test. Statistical significance between male and female rats was
analyzed by Student’s t-test or Welch’s test after F-test for two means.



3. Results

3.1. Comparison between 6arious PFCAs of urinary and fecal elimination in male
and female rats

   The amounts of urinary elimination were compared between PFHA, PFOA,
PFNA and PFDA in male and female rats (Fig. 1(A –B)). In male rats, PFHA was
rapidly eliminated in urine, 92% of the dose being eliminated within 120 h (Fig.
1(A)). PFOA was moderately eliminated in urine with 55% of the dose being
eliminated in urine. In contrast to PFHA and PFOA, PFNA and PFDA were
hardly eliminated in urine with 2.0 and 0.2% of the dose being eliminated within
120 h after the administration, respectively. These results indicate that PFCA
having shorter carbon chain length is more quickly eliminated in urine. Although
similar tendency was observed in female rats (Fig. 1(B)), female rats eliminated
PFOA and PFNA in urine significantly faster than did male rats. In contrast to
urinary elimination, all PFCAs were slowly eliminated in feces (Fig. 1(C –D)).
There was a tendency that PFCAs with longer carbon chain length is more rapidly
eliminated in feces in both male and female rats, but the difference in the
elimination with regard to carbon chain length was not significant in female rats. It
is noteworthy that fecal elimination was a major route of the elimination of PFDA
in both male and female rats.

3.2. Concentrations of PFCAs in serum and li6er

   Fig. 2 demonstrates concentrations of PFCAs in serum and livers 5 days after
dosing. In male rats, significant differences were observed between PFCAs, with a
tendency that PFCA with longer carbon chain length is higher in serum (Fig. 2(A)).
In female rats, serum concentrations of PFOA and PFNA were significantly lower
than those in male rats (Fig. 2(B)). Such differences were also observed in hepatic
concentrations of PFCAs (Fig. 2(C –D)), suggesting that PFCA, which is hardly
eliminated in urine, highly remains in the liver and circulation. Assuming that
blood volume is 1/12 of the body weight of rat, total recovery of the PFCA in
blood, liver, urine and feces 5 days after an injection were calculated to be 93, 95,
64, 78, 74, 57, 76 and 78% in male and female rats for PFHA, PFOA, PFNA and
PFDA, respectively.

3.3. Comparison of the rates of urinary and biliary excretion of PFCAs

   To rule out the possibility that the different rates of absorption from peritoneal
cavity are responsible for the difference in urinary elimination between PFCAs,
PFCAs were intravenously injected to rats. In addition, as shown in Fig. 1(A –B),
urinary excretion rates of some PFCAs were too fast to estimate the difference in
excretion rates between PFCAs and between male and female rats by collecting
urine samples with 12 h interval. Urinary excretion rates, therefore, were compared
between PFCAs under the condition that urination was enhanced by mannitol
infusion (Fig. 3). Rats were continuously infused with 10% (w/v) mannitol/0.9%
(w/v) NaCl solution to collect appropriate amounts of urine during 10 min. Urinary
excretion rates of PFOA and PFNA were considerably higher in female rats than
those in male rats (Fig. 3(B – C)). Urinary excretion rate of PFHA was also higher
in female rats than in male rats (Fig. 3(A)). In contrast to other PFCA, sex-related
difference was not observed in urinary excretion rate of PFDA; the rates in both
sexes were extremely low (Fig. 3(D)). In female rats, PFHA was more rapidly
excreted than was PFOA (Fig. 3(A –B)).

   Next, the rates of biliary excretion of PFCAs were determined, since there is a
possibility that slow rates of fecal elimination of PFCAs are due to re-absorption of
biliary-excreted PFCAs in intestine. PFCA with longer carbon chain length was
excreted more rapidly in male rats, although the differences were not significant in
female rats (Fig. 4). Biliary excretion rates of all PFCAs were significantly faster in
female rats than in male rats.

3.4. Effects of castration and testosterone treatment on urinary elimination of
PFOA

   Male rats of 3 weeks old were castrated, and urinary elimination of PFOA was
determined at 6 weeks old. The suppression of urinary elimination of PFOA by
treatment of castrated male rats with testosterone was observed in a dose-depen-
dent manner (Fig. 5(A)). At least daily treatment for 3 days was required for a
significant suppression (Fig. 5(B)). Effects of testosterone were compared between
castrated male rats and female rats (Fig. 6). Castrated rats eliminated 72% of the
dosed PFOA within 24 h, the value being the same as that of female rats. By
contrast, treatment of castrated male rats with testosterone significantly decreased
the amount of PFOA excreted into urine; the level was significantly less than that
of normal male rats. Although the effect of testosterone was observed in female rats

4. Discussion

4.1. Difference in urinary excretion rates of 6arious PFCAs

   The present study demonstrated that PFCA having longer carbon chain length
was more slowly eliminated in urine. That is, PFNA was excreted into urine slower
than PFOA but faster than PFDA, while the rate of urinary excretion of PFHA
was faster than that of PFOA in male rats (Fig. 3). Our present results were in
accordance with the previous findings of Vanden Heuvel et al. [27,30] who showed
that urinary excretion of PFOA is higher than that of PFDA using radiolabeled
compound. The concentrations in serum and livers were lower where PFCA was
more rapidly excreted (Fig. 2). Accordingly, urinary elimination is thought to be a
key determinant for persistence of PFCA in the bodies of rats. Involvement of
metabolic changes of PFCAs in the different degree of accumulation between
PFCAs can be excluded, since PFCAs are thought to be metabolically inert
[3,27,30]. The percentages of the recovery in liver, serum, urine and feces were lower
in PFOA, PFNA and PFDA. It is plausible that these PFCAs distributed to other
                 N. Kudo et al. / Chemico-Biological Interactions 134 (2001) 203–216                 211


organs as well, although we did not determine PFCA concentrations in tissues other
than liver.

4.2. Fecal and biliary excretion of PFCAs

   The amounts of PFCA eliminated in feces within 120 h were 2–5% of the dose.
There was a tendency that PFCA with longer carbon chain length was more
eliminated in feces, which was observed with biliary excretion of PFCAs (Fig. 4),
although differences in the amounts in both feces and bile between PFCAs were not
significant in female rats. Feces must become a major route of elimination when
PFCA was hardly eliminated in urine as was observed with PFNA in male rats and
PFDA in both sexes. The rates of biliary excretion were not significantly different
from those of fecal elimination for all PFCAs, although these data cannot be
directly compared because the experimental conditions were not the same in these
experiments. These results, therefore, confirmed that the key determinant of the
hepatic and serum concentrations of PFCAs was urinary elimination. For longer
period after an administration, however, entero-hepatic circulation plays a crucial
role in the elimination of PFCA. Johnson et al. showed that feeding of cholestyra-
mine to male rats facilitated fecal elimination of PFOA [31], suggesting that
entero-hepatic circulation is responsible for rapid clearance of PFOA for longer
periods of experiment. Biliary excretion rates in female rats were significantly higher
than those in male rats (Fig. 4). It is of interest that female rats excrete PFOA faster

4.3. Sex-related difference in urinary excretion of PFCAs

   Sex-related difference in the effects of PFOA was reported with regard to the
induction of hepatic peroxisomal b-oxidation [22] and some enzymes involved in
lipid metabolism [22 – 24] in rat liver. The lack of these effects of PFOA in female
rats was explained by extremely low concentration of hepatic PFOA [27,28]. In
contrast to PFOA, no apparent difference between male and female rats was
observed in the induction of peroxisomal b-oxidation by other PFCAs [28]. Vanden
Heuvel et al. reported that urinary elimination of PFDA was not different between
male and female rats [30]. Therefore, it is of interest to know why sex-related
difference in urinary elimination is observed in PFOA but not in PFDA, although
these two compounds are structurally related. The present study suggests that
sex-related difference of urinary excretion is extensively observed in various PFCAs,
although it is not obvious in the case of PFDA, since PFDA is hardly eliminated
in urine.
              N. Kudo et al. / Chemico-Biological Interactions 134 (2001) 203–216   213


   Sex-related difference in urinary elimination rate of PFOA was controlled by
testosterone (Fig. 6; [32]). Our previous results suggest that urinary PFNA is also
regulated by testosterone, since hepatic concentration of PFNA was strikingly
changed by castration or testosterone treatment [28]. The effect of testosterone was
observed dose- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 5). The present study demon-
strated that suppression by testosterone of urinary excretion was also observed in
female rats (Fig. 6), while Vanden Heuvel reported that testosterone did not affect
in female rats [32]. The discrepancy is thought to be due to the difference in
experimental conditions, especially the protocols on the treatment with testos-
terone, because the conditions of testosterone treatment that were effective in
castrated male rats failed to suppress urinary elimination of PFOA in female rats,
and the treatments for longer periods suppressed it (Fig. 4). The effect of testos-
terone was not related to the stage of sexual maturation, as is the case for
Zenarestat, an aldolase inhibitor, reported by Tanaka et al. [33]. By contrast,
castration and testosterone were effective only at an early stage of development on
the activity of CYP 2C12 in the liver of rats, where growth hormone directly
regulated the expression [34]. Several studies demonstrated that estradiol regulated
urinary elimination of some drugs [35 –37]. Ylinen et al. reported that treatment of
estradiol effectively increased urinary elimination of PFOA [35]. In our recent
study, however, treatment of estradiol increased urinary elimination of PFOA not
so much as did castration (Kudo et al, unpublished data). Further studies are
required to solve the regulatory mechanism of urinary excretion of these chemicals
by sex hormones.

4.4. Toxicity of PFCA to rats

   PFOA and PFDA have been characterized as peroxisome proliferators that cause
an induction of peroxisomal, microsomal and cytosolic enzymes as well as perox-
isome proliferation in the liver of rodents [3–14]. However, LD50 of PFDA was 41
mg/kg body weight [38], which is lower than that of PFOA (189 mg/kg body
weight) in rats [38], suggesting that the effects of PFDA on biological systems are
different from those of PFOA. In fact, several biological or toxicological effects
were found in PFDA but not in PFOA, i.e. reduction of thyroid hormone level in
rats [20,21], uncoupling of electron transport in isolated mitochondria [19] and
inactivation of a channel for 2-aminopurine [39]. In the previous study, we have
shown that the potency of the induction of hepatic peroxisomal b-oxidation is
highly correlated with hepatic concentrations of PFCA irrespective of their carbon
chain length [28]. Among various PFCAs, PFCA having longer carbon chain length
was more accumulated in the liver of rats when PFCAs were intraperitoneally
administered [28]. These facts suggest that PFDA having more extensive toxicity
compared to PFOA is predominantly due to its higher accumulation in the target
organs. This idea is supported by the fact that the potency of the induction of
peroxisomal acyl-CoA oxidase was not different between various PFCAs in rat
hepatocytes [28]. Moreover, we have preliminary observed that the accumulation of
triglyceride positively correlated to the PFCA concentration in rat liver (Kudo et
214               N. Kudo et al. / Chemico-Biological Interactions 134 (2001) 203–216


al., unpublished data). The results obtained in this study, therefore, strongly
suggested that urinary excretion plays a crucial role for the expression of toxicity of
PFCAs.
   The mechanism by which PFCA is excreted into urine has not been elucidated
yet. A few studies showed that probenecid, an inhibitor of organic anion trans-
porter(s), inhibited urinary excretion of PFOA in rats [25,32]. It is plausible,
therefore, that PFCAs are excreted into urine by an organic anion transporter,
which distinguishes PFCAs by their carbon chain length. Further studies are now
in progress in our laboratory to fully elucidate the mechanisms involved in urinary
excretion of PFCAs and its regulation by sex hormones.
