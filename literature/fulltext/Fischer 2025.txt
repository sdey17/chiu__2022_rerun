Abstract

Page 2

Exposures to some per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), including perfluoroalkyl acids 
(PFAA), have been associated with diverse adverse health effects. Physicochemical properties of 
PFAA are known to influence their toxicokinetics in mammals, but mechanistic models capable 
of identifying the key drivers of absorption, distribution, and elimination are limited. Here, we 
develop and evaluate a physiologically based toxicokinetic (PBTK) model parameterized to an 
in vivo mouse model using data from in vitro studies. We simulated tissue concentrations of 
9 PFAA with perfluorinated carbon chains  ηpfc  ranging from 4 to 10 in wild-type mice, and 
for perfluorooctanesulfonic acid in knockout mice, following intravenous and oral exposures. 
The PBTK model quantifies blood flow, binding to tissue proteins and phospholipids, membrane 
permeability, hepatic and renal transporters, and fecal and urinary excretion. Model evaluation 
comparing experimental and simulated mice blood and tissue concentrations showed R2 values 
of ≥ 0.65 and relative root-mean-square errors of ≤ 122% for most PFAA. Model sensitivity 
analyses showed that permeability and phospholipid binding strongly influenced the elimination 
and distribution of long-chain PFAA  ηpfc ≥ 7 , while elimination for short-chain PFAA  ηpfc ≤ 6
was more sensitive to renal transporters and albumin binding. This study illustrates how 
integrating in vitro-derived parameters into PBTK models enables mechanistic evaluation of PFAS 
toxicokinetics across diverse compounds and physiological conditions.

adverse effects on human health such as immunotoxicity,5 increased cancer risk,6 and

exposures and internal concentrations at active sites where toxicity is elicited remain poorly 
understood.

Perfluoroalkyl acids (PFAA) are the most well-studied PFAS subclass. They 
include perfluorosulfonic acids (PFSA) such as perfluorooctanesulfonate (PFOS) and 
perfluorocarboxylic acids (PFCA) such as perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and their 
precursors. Some PFAA with perfluorinated carbon chain lengths  ηpfc  of 7 or more have 
been shown to accumulate in humans, as evidenced by elimination half-lives of 3.5 years 
for PFOA  ηpfc = 7  and 4.8 years for PFOS  ηpfc = 8 .8 In contrast, shorter chain PFAA 
ηpfc ≤ 6 , such as perfluorobutanesulfonate (PFBS, ηpfc = 4), have elimination half-lives in 
humans ranging from days to weeks,9 consistent with findings from animal studies.10,11 In 
addition to physicochemical properties of PFAS, variability in measured human elimination 
half-lives reflect interindividual physiological differences driven by disease and/or genetic 
factors that are difficult to isolate. In contrast, time-dependent concentrations of PFAA in 
blood, organs, and excreta can be measured in physiologically comparable rodents following 
controlled doses.

Rodents are widely used in pharmaceutical and medical research to study 
pharmacokinetics.12 Numerous studies have examined PFAA toxicokinetics in mice 
using single intravenous or oral doses to generate time dependent blood and tissue 
concentrations.13–17 Knockout mouse models, such as albumin- and fatty acid binding 
protein (FABP)-deficient C57BL mice, have been used to investigate specific toxicokinetic 
mechanisms affecting PFAA exposure. For example, albumin-deficient mice exhibited 
significantly elevated liver:blood concentration ratios,18 while FABP-deficient mice showed 
PFAA toxicokinetics similar to wild-type mice.19 Availability of physiological parameters 
for laboratory mouse strains, including organ volumes and blood flow rates,20,21 makes them 
a well-suited model species for mechanistic physiologically based toxicokinetic (PBTK) 
modeling.

Many important mechanisms affecting tissue PFAA distribution, elimination, and 
(re)absorption have been demonstrated in vitro. For example, PFAA with ηpfc ≥ 7 show 
stronger binding to blood proteins such as albumin,22 and slower membrane permeability 
than their ηpfc ≤ 6 counterparts.23 Binding to membrane transporters such as organic anion-
transporting polypeptides (Oatp) and organic anion transporters (Oat)23–26 is thought to 
drive PFAA uptake into liver and kidneys, respectively.27 Multiyear elimination half-lives 
of ηpfc ≥ 7 PFAA in humans have been attributed to enterohepatic recirculation and renal 
tubular reabsorption.28,29 PFAA with ηpfc ≥ 7 are substrates for bile acid transporters 
such as apical sodium-dependent bile acid transporters (Asbt) and sodium/taurocholate 
cotransporting polypeptides (Ntcp).30,31 These transport mechanisms occur concurrently 
and interact dynamically under in vivo conditions and their overall importance for PFAA 
toxicokinetics requires further evaluation.

The main objective of this study was to better understand mechanisms affecting PFAS 
toxicokinetics in mammals. To do this, we developed a PBTK modeling framework that 
integrates in vitro data on permeability, tissue binding, and membrane transporters with 
physiological data for two mouse strains previously used in PFAS toxicokinetic studies: 
C57BL/6J (an inbred strain with minimal genetic variability) and CD-1 (an outbred strain 
with greater genetic diversity). We used the model to evaluate mechanistic drivers for 
variability in PFAA absorption, distribution, and elimination in wild-type and knockout 
mice for nine PFAA with ηpfc = 4 − 10. We conducted sensitivity analyses to identify the 
relative contributions of individual mechanisms to PFAA accumulation and elimination. We 
use these findings aim to gain insights into processes that are most important for PFAS 
toxicokinetics and highlight key knowledge and data gaps for extending findings to human 
populations and emerging PFAS.

2.  MODEL THEORY AND PARAMETERIZATION

2.1.  General Model Structure.

We developed a five-compartment PBTK model that includes: (1) central blood, (2) gut, 
(3) liver, (4) kidneys, and (5) the rest of the body (Figure 1). The “rest” compartment 
includes all other organs not specified. The model includes two exposure routes: oral PFAA 
ingestion through the gut lumen, and intravenous PFAA administration into the central 
blood compartment. Cells and interstitial fluid are represented as single organs, separated 
from perfusing blood by bidirectionally permeable membranes. The model accounts for: 
(a) blood flow to and from organs; (b) binding to proteins and phospholipids within 
organs; (c) passive membrane diffusion; (d) active transport via membrane transporters; 
(e) enterohepatic recirculation, (f) renal tubular reabsorption; and (g) excretion through feces 
and urine. Chemical parameters were derived from in vitro studies, while physiological 
parameters for CD-1 and C57BL mice were obtained from literature.

2.2.  Description of the PBTK Model.

In mammals, physiological blood flow rates determine PFAA transport from central blood 
to organs. The model simulates temporal changes in blood and tissue concentrations 
dC /dt, mol mL−1 s−1  based on chemical mass flows, representing PFAA absorption, 
distribution to the systemic circulation, distribution to organs, and elimination. PFAA bind 
to serum albumin, globulins, structural proteins, FABPs, and phospholipids in blood and 
organs, while binding to storage lipids is considered negligible.32 The concentration of 
freely dissolved PFAA  Cfree, mol mL−1  in blood is considered available for transport among 
organs.33 PFAA bound to tissue components  Cbound, mol mL−1  are not available for inter-
organ transport and elimination. We simulated concentrations in whole blood rather than 
plasma to represent the full distribution of PFAS among plasma, cellular, and colloidal blood 
components.

We used desorption rate constants to account for PFAA dissociation from proteins and 
phospholipids.34 Bidirectional chemical transport was assumed to occur via permeability. 
We incorporated transporter-mediated uptake and efflux of PFAA for the liver, gut, and

2.4.  Chemical Parameters.

Major categories of parameters in the PBTK model are: (1) PFAA apparent membrane 
permeabilities  Papp ,23 (2) partition coefficients for proteins and phospholipids,32,46–49 and 
(3) membrane transporter affinities.23–27,30,31,50 Model algorithms represent mechanistic 
processes that could be parameterized using reliable data derived in in vitro systems 
with available scaling factors. Only pathways with compound-specific data were included, 
and transporter rates were set to zero for compounds with no measurable activity or 
missing data. We used Monte Carlo simulations (10,000 iterations) to quantify the 
influence of measured variability/uncertainty in chemical parameters based on a detailed 
literature review of published in vitro studies (Tables S7–S10). We assumed log-normal 
distributions for partition coefficients and calculated μ and σ for each PFAS from the 
means and standard deviations of reported log K values (Table S8). For permeabilities 
and transporter affinities, we used normal distributions for each PFAS calculated from the 
grand mean and pooled variance calculated from replicate measurements within two in 
vitro studies,23,31 as summarized in Tables S9 and S10. Albumin desorption rates were 
used to account for desorption kinetics (1/s).51 Partition coefficients for phospholipids,47,48 
serum albumin,46,49,52,53 and FABPs53–55 were obtained from multiple sources. Association 
and dissociation constants were transformed to partition coefficients following Endo et 
al.56 In vitro permeabilities and transporter affinities were scaled to in vivo conditions 
using relative activity factors specific to the cell lines employed.57 Section S3 of the 
Supporting Information provides additional information on chemical parameters, including 
their sources, variabilities, and methods for conversion and scaling.

2.5.  Model Evaluation and Sensitivity Analysis.

Single and repeated intravenous and oral dosing studies for mice were used to evaluate 
model performance. We report the arithmetic mean of Monte Carlo simulations, and 
confidence intervals spanning the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the ensemble for modeled 
time-dependent concentrations and elimination half-lives of each PFAA. Elimination half-
lives were calculated by log–linear regression of the terminal phase blood concentration 
time curves for each simulation. We compared measured mean blood and tissue PFAA 
concentrations to the model expected values to calculate R2 and relative root mean squared 
error (rRMSE). Specifically, we used data from three primary studies described below.

Modaresi et al.19 administered 0.1–5 mg/kgbw PFOS to 5–6-month-old wild-type C57BL/6J

3.  RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

3.1.  Model Evaluation.

We compared simulated plasma and blood concentrations of PFOS, PFOA, and PFHxS after 
single oral and intravenous (IV) doses to experimental data from C57BL/6J and CD-1 mice 
(Figure 2A–C). R2 values ranged from 0.73 to 0.87 and rRMSE between 18% and 38% 
between modeled and observed values16,19 plasma and blood PFOS concentrations after 
single oral doses of 0.1–5 mg/kgbw. Similarly, R2 values ranged from 0.79–0.92 and rRMSE 
between 35%–41% between modeled and observed16 PFOA plasma concentrations after oral 
doses of 1 and 5 mg/kgbw. We found lower R2 values (0.045 and 0.01) and higher rRMSE 
values (65% and 70%) between modeled and observed16 PFHxS plasma concentrations at 
the same oral doses, primarily due to underestimation at early time points (Figure S3). For 
IV dosing of PFHxS, the model better matched experimental data with R2 values of 0.71–
0.90 and rRMSE ranging from 31% to 89%. However, plasma concentrations of PFOS and 
PFOA were underestimated following IV administration of 1 mg/kgbw, with higher rRMSE 
values of 487% and 292% for PFOS and PFOA, respectively (Figures S1 and S2). Notably, 
in the study by Zhu et al.,16 maximum plasma concentrations of PFOS and PFOA at the high 
dose (5 mg/kgbw) were unexpectedly higher following oral compared to IV administration,

Modeled elimination half-lives  t1/2  for long-chain PFAA  ηpfc ≥ 7  were consistent with

(Figure 2 and Table 1). For PFHxS  ηpfc = 6 , modeled t1/2 was 28–34 days compared 
to experimental values of 25–54 days.16,17 In contrast, the model overestimated t1/2 for 
short-chain PFAS  ηpfc ≤ 5 . PFBS  ηpfc = 4  and perfluorohexanoic acid (PFHxA, ηpfc = 5) 
had modeled t1/2 values of 3.2 and 3.8 days, respectively, compared to experimental values 
of ~ 6 h. This discrepancy may reflect missing renal clearance mechanisms in the model. 
PFBS and PFHxA exhibit high affinity for OAT1 and OAT3,23 indicating efficient uptake 
into renal proximal tubule cells and suggesting they may also be substrates for apical efflux 
transporters such as multidrug resistance-associated proteins, for which in vitro data are 
currently lacking.

Modeled PFOS concentrations in liver, kidneys, and the rest of the body fell within a 
factor of 3 of experimental observations across all dosing regimens. These results included 
single oral doses ranging from 0.1 to 5 mg/kgbw (sampled after 65 days)19 and repeated 
daily oral doses of 0.5 or 10 mg/kgbw over 7 days (Figure 2D).18 Some deviations in 
gut concentrations were observed, likely due to the assumption of constant oral uptake in 
the model, whereas mice were fed at defined intervals in the experimental studies. Tissue 
concentration data were only available for PFOS, limiting model evaluation for other PFAA.

The PBTK model successfully captured differences in PFOS distribution and elimination 
between knockout and wild-type mice. Previous studies showed that PFOS blood 
concentrations were not significantly different in FABP knockout and wild-type mice 
exposed to the same doses.19 Model results similarly indicated that deletion of liver-FABP 
and intestinal-FABP did not influence PFOS blood concentrations (Figure S5). While 
FABPs have been hypothesized to facilitate hepatic PFAS uptake based on in vitro studies,53 
our findings suggest their contribution to PFOS toxicokinetics in vivo is minimal. In 
knockout mice with global genetic deletion of albumin (Alb−/−), observed plasma PFOS 
concentration were 52–65% lower and liver concentrations were 23–26% higher than 
wild-type mice.18 Modeled results predicted a similar 48% reduction in plasma PFOS 
concentrations, but lower increases compared to observed changes in liver concentrations 
(4%) (Figure S6). This discrepancy likely reflects the dominant role of phospholipid binding 
in hepatic PFOS retention,59 which may reduce the impact of albumin loss. Overall, we 
find the model reasonably reproduced observed PFOS distribution patterns in knockout mice 
with global albumin and FABP deletion.

3.2.  Simulating Absorption, Distribution, and Elimination for PFAA.

Model results illustrate a rapid increase in gut and liver concentrations of the nine PFAA 
examined in this study within the first 24 h following a single oral dose (Figures 3A and S7). 
Short-chain PFAA, such as PFBS  ηpfc = 4  and PFHxA  ηpfc = 5 , reach peak concentrations 
in systemic blood, kidneys, and the rest compartment within 24 h, and decline thereafter 
(Figure S7A–C). In contrast, maximum blood and kidney concentrations for ηpfc ≥ 7 PFAA 
exhibit a 2–3 day delay after dosing (Figure S7D–H). Accumulation of ηpfc ≥ 9 PFAA in the 
rest compartment continued throughout the 15-day simulation, underscoring their prolonged 
tissue retention (Figure S7G,H).

To simulate constant exposure conditions relevant to humans, we modeled repeated 
daily dosing at 100 ng over two years. Under these conditions, short-chain carboxylates 
ηpfc = 4 − 6 , including PFBS, PFHxA, and PFHpA, accumulated to significantly lower 
body concentrations than long-chain sulfonates and carboxylates (Figure 3C). For example, 
PFDA  ηpfc = 9  accumulated to levels approximately 100 times higher than PFBS  ηpfc = 4 . 
Tissue distribution simulations showed high relative concentrations of all PFAA in blood, 
liver, and kidneys (Figure 3C). Notably, PFHxS  ηpfc = 6  accumulated to higher tissue 
concentrations and masses than PFOA  ηpfc = 7 , highlighting the greater bioaccumulation 
potential of sulfonates compared to carboxylates. Longer chain length PFAA  ηpfc ≥ 7
exhibited higher concentrations in the liver compared to shorter chain length PFAA 
ηpfc ≤ 6 , which were more concentrated in the kidneys. Despite low concentrations, the 
rest compartment was the dominant sink for PFAA mass due to its large volume (24 mL) 
(Figure S9).

The fraction of PFAA absorbed was calculated with respect to the total amount of each 
compound in all compartments relative to the administered dose. Absorption across all 
PFAA was high, ranging from 76% to 90% following a single oral dose (Figure 3D). Shorter 
chain length PFAA exhibited slightly lower absorption  ηpfc ≤ 5 = 76 − 83 %  compared to 
longer chain length PFAA  ηpfc ≥ 6 = 84 − 90 % . Between 70–92% of the total administered 
dose over two years was eliminated through urinary excretion across all PFAA, making it 
the primary elimination pathway. Fecal excretion was most prominent for ηpfc = 5 − 7 PFAA, 
peaking at 30% for PFHxS. Notably, PFOS exhibited the highest urinary excretion (92%) 
and the lowest fecal elimination (8%). Reduced fecal excretion of ηpfc ≥ 7 PFAA such as 
PFOS (8%), PFDA (12%), and perfluoroundecanoic acid (PFUDA, 9%) is likely due to 
enterohepatic recirculation,28 where compounds excreted into the gut via biliary transport 
are reabsorbed in the gut lumen because of their high permeability.

phospholipids, with short-chain PFAA such as PFBS and PFHxA bound at ≥ 98% (Figures 
3B and S8). In the liver, 7 of the 9 PFAA were predominantly bound to phospholipids, with 
PFOS and PFDA showing over 80% phospholipid binding, while the remaining fraction 
was mainly protein-bound (Figure 3B). A similar binding pattern was observed in the 
kidney, gut, and rest compartments, where phospholipid binding accounted for ≥ 50% of

3.3.  Sensitivity of Toxicokinetic Parameters.

PBTK model sensitivity analyses showed that elimination half-lives of ηpfc ≥ 8 PFAA are 
strongly driven by phospholipid binding and permeability. For these compounds, normalized 
sensitivity coefficients – defined as the percent change in toxicokinetic outcome per percent 
change in model parameter – ranged from 47% to 83% for membrane lipids and −73% to 
−84% for permeability. In contrast, ηpfc ≤ 7 PFAA elimination showed greater sensitivity 
to renal transporter activity, particularly Oat3, with coefficients of −37% for PFBS and 
−35% for PFHpA. The pronounced sensitivity to phospholipid binding for ηpfc ≥ 7 PFAA 
is driven by their strong affinity for phospholipids  log KPL/w: 3.51 − 4.88
widespread presence of phospholipids in mouse tissues such as the liver (2.05%) and 
kidneys (2.06%).21,32 This agrees with intratissue simulations showing that most PFAA 
are primarily bound to phospholipids in tissues (Figure 3B).

Permeability acts as a rate-limiting factor for PFAS distribution from central blood to the

0.14% in the kidneys, therefore retaining PFAA in the blood and reducing distribution to 
peripheral tissues. Globulins, the second most abundant class of blood proteins (1.33% by 
volume)32 have a minor yet compound-specific impact on blood AUC. For ηpfc ≤ 9 PFAA, 
sensitivity coefficients for globulin binding remain below 7%, but globulins contribute more 
significantly to blood levels of PFUDA (ηpfc = 10, sensitivity: 26%), consistent with their 
reported affinity to bind ηpfc ≥ 10 PFAS.46 Phospholipids negatively influence blood AUC 
by promoting PFAA retention in tissues, with sensitivity coefficients of −25% for PFOS.

absorption within the simulated one elimination half-life. Conversely, for long-chain PFAA,

Oatps and Ntcp only minorly influence PFAA toxicokinetics across absorption, blood 
and tissue retention, and elimination, with sensitivity coefficients reaching up to 6% 
for absorption (Figure S11). These transporters are located in the liver, where they 
facilitate the active uptake of PFAA from the hepatic portal vein and hepatic artery into 
hepatocytes.24,26,50 In addition, Oatp2b1 is present on the apical membrane of enterocytes, 
facilitating PFAA influx from the gut lumen. These transport processes promote PFAA 
distribution into the systemic circulation, retention in the liver, and biliary transport, 
contributing to enterohepatic recirculation.28 Consequently, an increase in Oatp or Ntcp-
mediated transport enhances absorption, tissue retention (particularly in the liver), and 
elimination half-lives, while decreasing blood AUC.

Oat-mediated transport influenced PFAA blood levels and clearance, particularly for ηpfc ≤ 7
PFAA, with sensitivity coefficients for Oat1 and Oat3 ranging from −7.7% (Oat1) to 44% 
(Oat3) (Figure 4). By contrast, Oat2 had minimal impact on toxicokinetic outcomes for all 
PFAA, with sensitivity coefficients consistently below 1%. Located in the kidneys, Oat1–3 
transport PFAA from the blood into renal tissues, thereby promoting renal elimination. 
For ηpfc ≥ 8 PFAA, sensitivity coefficients for Oats ranged from −1.4% to −5%, indicating 
negligible to moderate importance of these transporters and highlighting the role of passive 
permeability for these highly permeable PFAA.23

3.4. 

Implications, Limitations, and Applicability to Humans.

Several PBPK models have been developed to describe PFAS kinetics in rodents and 
humans.60–62 These models rely on empirically fitted partition coefficients and clearance 
terms to simulate tissue distribution and elimination. In contrast, our model uses in vitro-
derived parameters, without calibration to in vivo data. This structure allows for mechanistic 
evaluation of individual toxicokinetic mechanisms and facilitates refinement where key 
pathways – such as transporter-mediated renal clearance of short-chain PFAS – remain 
poorly defined.

Our PBTK model accurately predicts toxicokinetic end points for most PFAA in mice across 
single and repeated oral and intravenous dosing regimens, with R2 values ≥ 0.65 and rRMSE 
≤ 122%. Exceptions included underestimation of early PFHxS plasma concentrations after 
oral dosing and overestimation of PFOS and PFOA plasma levels at high IV doses of 5

identify compound-specific mechanisms driving elimination half-lives and tissue distribution

Sensitivity analyses revealed that permeability and phospholipid binding were the most 
important model parameters for ηpfc ≥ 7 PFAA, while renal transporters, particularly Oat3, 
substantially impacted the clearance of ηpfc < 7 PFAA. Albumin and phospholipids were 
the primary determinants of PFAA retention in blood and tissues, respectively. Structural 
proteins played a minor role in tissue retention, while FABPs showed minimal sensitivity to 
tissue accumulation and elimination. Interestingly, PFAA absorption was the toxicokinetic 
outcome that was least influenced by shifts in model parameters (Figure S11). Alongside 
Oats and Oatps, transporters like the fatty acid translocase or platelet glycoprotein 4 (CD36) 
and the breast cancer resistance protein (Bcrp) may contribute to PFAS toxicokinetics,65,66 
but their roles in absorption, distribution, and elimination remain unclear due to limited 
quantitative data. Transporter data used in the current model version were from in vitro 
cells genetically modified to overexpress the transporter of interest and literature scaling 
factors.57 A source of uncertainty in the current model is the absence of experimentally 
determined Michaelis–Menten parameters for the included membrane transporters, including 
Km (affinity) and V max (maximum transport rate), which limits incorporation of transporter 
saturation kinetics into uptake and efflux processes. In addition, limited data on transporter 
expression in the in vitro cells used to generate these kinetics constrains accurate in vitro 
to in vivo scaling. Addressing these gaps through targeted experiments and quantitative 
proteomics could enhance model accuracy, especially for short-chain PFAS with transporter-
dependent kinetics.

Expanding the applicability domain of the model requires inclusion of additional exposure 
routes, including inhalation and dermal absorption. The ongoing shift from legacy to 
new PFAS with fewer fluorinated carbons and distinct functional groups emphasizes the 
need for kinetic and binding data on emerging PFAS measured in vitro. The current 
model was developed using uniform physiological parameters for laboratory mice. Future 
applications in humans should incorporate interindividual variability in transporter and 
protein expression, as well as physiological changes associated with genotype, age, or 
disease states. As shown in prior work, variability in blood protein concentrations alone 
can cause up to 3-fold differences in free PFAS fractions, potentially affecting blood 
elimination and tissue accumulation.46 Noninvasive techniques, such as liquid biopsies,67 
could provide personalized physiological data, enabling the development of individualized 
human PBTK models. Such models could be used to explore how disease and genetic
