Background: Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are persistent substances with surfactant and repellent 
properties. Municipal drinking water contaminated with PFAS had been distributed for decades to one third of 
households in Ronneby, Sweden. The source was firefighting foam used in a nearby airfield since the mid-1980s. 
Clean water was provided from December 16, 2013. 
Aims: The purpose was to estimate serum half-lives and their determinants in the study population for different 
PFAS. 
Methods: Up to ten blood samples were collected between 2014 and 2018 from 114 participants (age 4–84 years 
at entry, 53% female). 19 PFAS were analysed. Linear mixed models were used to estimate the half-lives. 
Results:  Eight  PFAS  were  increased  in  Ronneby:  perfluorooctanoic  acid  (PFOA),  perfluoropentane  sulfonate 
(PFPeS),  perfluorohexane  sulfonate  (PFHxS),  perfluoroheptane  sulfonate  (PFHpS),  linear  perfluorooctane  sul-
fonate (L-PFOS) and three branched perfluorooctane sulfonates (1 m-PFOS, 3/4/5m-PFOS and 2/6m-PFOS). The 
mean estimated half-lives (in years) were 0.94 (95 %CI 0.86–1.02) for PFPeS, 2.47 (2.27–2.7) for PFOA, 2.67 
(2.51–2.85) for 2/6m-PFOS, 2.73 (2.55–2.92) for L-PFOS, 3.43 (3.19–3.71) for 3/4/5m-PFOS, 4.52 (4.14–4.99) 
for PFHxS, 4.55 (4.14–5.06) for PFHpS, and 5.01 (4.56–5.55) for 1 m-PFOS. 
The most important determinants of a shorter half-life were young age, and better kidney function measured by 
estimated  glomerular filtration  rate  and ratio  of  paired  urine and  serum PFAS  levels,  followed  by female  sex 
during their fertile period aged 15–50. Markers of gut inflammation and reduced permeability i.e. zonulin and 
calprotectin  were  also  possibly  associated  with  shorter  half-life.  The  results  also  suggested  a  time-dependent 
PFAS elimination process, with more rapid elimination in the first year after the end of exposure. 
Conclusion: The half-life estimates are in line with past estimates for some PFAS such as PFOA, and the novel 
results for different PFOS isomers. These results provide observational support for elimination routes –  renal, 
fecal and maternal.   

1. Introduction 

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are persistent substances 
with  surfactant  and  repellent  properties.  The  production  and  use  of 
PFAS  has  led  to  global  environmental  contamination.  The  source  of 
exposure  to  PFAS  in  the  general  population  may  include  food,  house 
dust, drinking water, and, for infants, placental transfer and breast milk 
(Begley et al., 2005; Berger et al., 2009; Falandysz et al., 2006; Shoeib 

et  al.,  2005).  Locally,  drinking  water  contamination  is  a  significant 
exposure source, particularly at very high exposure levels identified in 
four  large  communities  in  the  mid-Ohio  Valley  US  (C8  population); 
Arnsberg,  Germany;  Veneto,  Italy  cohort  and  in  Ronneby,  Sweden 
(Brede et al., 2010; Frisbee et al., 2009; Pitter et al., 2020; Xu et al., 
2021) but also at lower general population levels (Glynn et al., 2020; Hu 
et al., 2019). A wide range of health effects of PFAS during development, 
as well as later in life, are suspected even at present background human

exposure (EFSA, 2018). 

It is imperative to establish the rate of elimination of PFAS from the 
body  after  cessation  of  exposure,  not  only  for  modelling  purpose  in 
epidemiological studies, but also for risk assessment and risk commu-
nication in affected areas worldwide. Various animal studies have esti-
mated serum half-lives (t1/2) for PFAS in different species, and reported 
half-lives  usually  in  hours  or  days  with  markedly  variation  between 
species (Lau et al., 2007). Human half-lives for the few PFAS studied are 
substantially longer than animals (Bartell et al., 2010; Li et al., 2018; 
Olsen et al., 2007; Xu et al., 2020a). Half-lives in human have been re-
ported for perfluorooctane sulfonic acid (PFOS) and perfluorooctanoic 
acid (PFOA) for between 2 and 4 years, and perfluorohexane sulfonic 
acid (PFHxS) for 5 up to 7, while some short-chain PFAS have consid-
erably shorter half-lives (Xu et al., 2020a). There are some differences in 
the average half-life reported between different human studies, and in 
addition  we  have  demonstrated  there  is  substantial  inter-individual 
variation for which there is very little explanation (Li et al., 2018). 

However,  these  studies  have  provided  very  few  insights  into  the 
determinants of PFAS elimination. For example, it is expected that fe-
males in fertile ages have a shorter PFAS half-life than males due to by 
menstrual  blood  losses,  pregnancies  and  breast-feeding  (Wong  et  al., 
2014).  For  PFOA,  dose-or  time-dependent  elimination  has  been  sug-
gested (Brede et al., 2010; Seals et al., 2011; Xu et al., 2020a). In a C8- 
study,  the  elimination  rate  was  related  to  single  nucleotide  poly-
morphisms (SNPs) in tubular transporter proteins (Fletcher et al., 2012), 
indicating  that  renal  elimination  phenotypes  may  be  important, 
although a smaller cross sectional analysis of PFOA levels in relation to 
one of these SNPs in an Italian study did not find any significant asso-
ciation (Ingelido et al., 2020). Therefore there remains a need for more 
detailed  information  on  half-life  for  more  PFAS  than  the  few  most- 
studied and the variation by age, sex, body mass index and other po-
tential determinants. Given that elimination can be by both renal and 
gut excretion, factors that affect these routes are of interest to explore 
whether they may be reflected in individual PFAS half-life. 

An  earlier  paper  on  this  same  population  derived  half-lives  for 
PFHxS, PFOA and PFOS (Li et al., 2018), and the current paper provides 
improved estimates for half-lives in this population with longer follow- 
up and an extended analysis of other PFAS compounds. 

The aims in this paper were to estimate serum half-lives of a range of 
perfluoroalkyl  substances  with  varying  carbon  chain-length  after  the 
abrupt cessation of long-term high exposure to contaminated drinking 
water, and to characterize the individual determinants of serum half- 
lives. 

2. Material and method 

2.1. Study group 

In  2013,  it  was  found  out  that  drinking  water  from  one  of  two 
municipal  waterworks  in  Ronneby,  a  municipality  with  28  000  in-
habitants in southern Sweden, was contaminated with very high levels 
of PFAS (Xu et al., 2021). This waterworks, which served approximately 
one third of the households in the municipality, was closed on 16 Dec 
2013  and  water  was  immediately  provided  from  the  other  non- 
contaminated waterworks. It was soon confirmed that use of aqueous 
film-forming foam (AFFF) for firefighting at the nearby military airport 
since the mid-1980s was the source of contamination. 

Extensive biomonitoring in the municipality started in June 2014, 
approximately 6 months after end of the exposure, by open invitation 
and free of cost. Subjects for a half-life study were recruited among the 
first 1000 participants, until the target of 100 subjects was reached with 
a wide range of serum levels and ages. These participants have donated 
so far serum samples in 10 rounds: in June 2014, October 2014, January 
2015, April 2015, September 2015, March 2016, September 2016, April 
2017, September 2017 and May 2018. Urine samples were collected in 
September 2015 (round 5), March 2016 (round 6) and May 2018 (round 

10). Fecal samples were collected in September 2015. Ongoing sampling 
is planned for several years to come. 

A reference group from Karlshamn, a nearby municipality without 
PFAS contamination in drinking water, was also examined in 2016 (Li 
et  al.,  2018;  Xu  et  al.,  2021).  68  of  the  participants  in  the  reference 
group, with age range 20–50 years, were randomly selected to estimate 
the  background  exposure  level  for  an  extended  range  of  PFAS 
compounds.

2.3. Determinants 

2.3.1. Age group 

The  participants  were  divided  into  three  age  groups  i.e.  ≤ 14, 
>14–50,  >50,  at  first  sampling,  broadly  reflecting  pre-and  early  pu-
berty, menstruating ages and postmenopausal ages. 

2.3.2. Body mass index (BMI) 

For  those  above  14  years,  we  have  investigated  the  possible  BMI 
effect on elimination. BMI was dichotomized at 25, i.e. normal weight vs 
overweight. There was no one in the study group above 14 years that 
was underweight i.e. BMI below 18.5. 

2.3.3. Kidney and gastrointestinal function 

For the kidney, the glomerular filtration rate is a marker of kidney 
function,  and  for  the  gut,  calprotectin  and  zonulin  are  markers  of 
inflammation and permeability, which may affect PFAS reabsorption. 

Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was used as indicator of 
kidney function and based on equations using serum cystatin C based on 
CAPA formula (Grubb  et al., 2014). eGFR was divided  into two cate-
gories above or below 90 ml/min/1.73 m2. 

For those having donated fecal samples (n = 57), measured zonulin 
and  calprotectin  levels  were  used  as  indicators  of  gastrointestinal 
permeability  and  inflammation  (Walsham  and  Sherwood,  2016; 
Malíˇckov´a  et  al.,  2017).  Zonulin  was  dichotomized  at  their  median 
levels.  A  conventional  cut-off  at  50  mg/kg  was  used  for  calprotectin 
(Konikoff and Denson, 2006). 

2.3.4. Urine/serum ratio 

62 participants had donated urine samples in rounds 5, 6 and round 
10. All of the 8 PFAS at higher concentrations with most above 1 ng/ml 
(PFOA, PFPeS, PFHxS, PFHpS, L-PFOS, 1 m-PFOS, 3/4/5m-PFOS, 2/6m- 
PFOS) were detected in the most of urine samples. Based on the paired 
urine  and  serum  concentration,  their  ratios  were  calculated.  The  de-
nominator is the serum concentration, a surrogate of the body burden of 
PFAS.  The  numerator  is  the  urine  concentration,  reflecting  the  renal 
excretion pathway. Based on the ratios, each participant could be clas-
sified  as  having  high or  low  renal excretion  depending on  the  ratio’s 
relation to the median of the specific round. Since there were up to three 
rounds of urine samples, a participant who consistently remained in the 
high ratio category in all the three rounds urine samples was classified as 
having a ‘high’ ratio. We used the urine values measured even if below 
the LOD. These were used in the numerator for the urine/serum ratio 
and then they were classified into high vs low ratios. Thus those below 
LOD were rightly placed in the low category.

cept αi, the subject-specific slope ki  and the random error term εij  were 
modeled  as  random  with  normal  distribution;  others  were  treated  as 
fixed effects. The slope ki  is the individual elimination rate and its mean 
estimated  from  model  (i.e.  population  average  elimination  rate)  was 
converted to half-life (ln2/mean(ki)) to derive the mean half-life. Indi-
vidual values of ki  were predicted using the “best linear unbiased pre-
diction” method, i.e. an individual elimination rate for each participant 
derived  from  the  model  via  command  ‘random  /solution’  in  the  pro-
cedure  ‘proc  mixed’  in  SAS.  Summary  half-life  values  have  been  pre-
sented as either a mean half-life (calculated from the mean elimination 
rate constant k: t1/2 = ln(2)/k) or as median half-life (the median value 
of the individual predicted half-life values). The 95% CI for mean (ki) 
from the regression was used to derive the CI for the half-life, by con-
verting as for the mean. 

We conducted additional analyses to determine whether elimination 
rates depended on certain covariates. In addition to model 1, we added 
interaction terms between age group (≤14, >14–50, >50) and elapsed 
time, interactions between age group, gender and elapsed time, and the 
interactions between initial total PFAS levels tertiles and elapsed time. 
Since age group had a strong effect on elimination, the interaction 
term  between  elapsed  time  and  age  groups  were  kept  in  following 
analysis.  For  a  subgroup  with  age  over  14,  we  adding  interaction  of 
dichotomous BMI variable (below or above 25) with elapsed time. 

We also evaluated the potential dependence of elimination rates on 
time elapsed after end of exposure by comparing the estimates based on 
the samples from round 1 to round 3, (6–13 months after end of drinking 
water exposure), round 3 to round 6 (13–25 months) and round 6 to 
round 10 (25 to 53 months). 

In  addition,  we  evaluated  whether  kidney  function  measured  by 
eGFR,  gut  integrity  measured  by  zonulin  and  calprotectin,  and  urine 
excretion  measured by  urine/serum ratio  have an  influence on elimi-
nation  rate  by  adding  interaction  terms  between  these  dichotomous 
variables and elapsed time. 

Finally,  we  calculated  partial  R2,  as  a  measure  of  the  relative 
importance of the determinants based on the linear model with the in-
dividual predicted half-life as outcome and the determinants as cova-
riates  i.e.  interaction  terms  between  age  and  sex,  categorical  eGFR, 
zonulin,  calprotectin  and  initial  total  PFAS  levels,  all  in  the  model 
together.

3.2. Serum PFAS levels 

In  total  19  PFAS  compounds  were  measured  in  the  panel  group 
(Table 2). At 1st sampling, PFHxS showed the highest serum concen-
tration,  with  geometric  mean  level  480  times  higher  than  the  level 
observed  in  the  reference  population.  PFOA  and  PFHpS  also  showed 
substantial higher levels. The levels of PFPeS>100 times higher than in 
the reference. The linear and branched PFOS substances (L-PFOS, 1 m- 
PFOS,  2/6m-PFOS  and  3/4/5m-PFOS)  were  substantially  higher  with 
50–150  times  higher  levels  in  the  panel  group  than  in  the  reference 
group.  We  focused  on  these  8  predominant  PFAS  compounds  in  the 
following analyses. 

3.3. Urinary PFAS levels 

Compared to serum PFAS levels, the urinary PFAS levels were low, 
with a median for PFHxS 0.13 ng/ml and for other PFAS medians were 
below 0.08 ng/ml (Table 3). At the group level, the urine/serum ratios 
for PFPeS were higher than the rest of PFAS compounds. 

For each PFAS, there was a substantial between-individual variation 
of the urine/serum ratio. The ratios were more consistent over time as 
the  median  of ratios  were similar across times. Over 60%  of the  par-
ticipants showed consistency in high or low urine/serum ratio over the 
first two rounds, i.e. a participant had relatively high urine/serum ratio

3.4. Half-lives of PFAS 

From  June  2014  to  May  2018,  a  four-year  period,  the  geometric 
mean serum level decreased 56% PFOA, 92% for PFPeS, 43% for PFHxS, 
38% for PFHpS, 60% for linear PFOS (L-PFOS), 40% for branched 1 m- 
PFOS, 54% for branched 3/4/5m-PFOS and 64% for branched 2/6m- 
PFOS. 

Table 4 shows the elimination constant and the corresponding half- 
lives  for  each  PFAS  compound.  PFPeS  showed  the  most  rapid  annual 
decrease with 52%, which corresponds to a mean half-life of 0.94 years. 
PFOA, L-PFOS and 2/6m-PFOS had half-lives around 2.5 to 3 years. 3/4/ 
5m-PFOS showed a half-life of 3.4 years. PFHxS, PFHpS and 1 m-PFOS 
showed relative longer half-lives than rest with 4.5 years, 4.6 years and 
5.0 years respectively.

3.5. Determinants of half-lives of PFAS 

We explored the possibility of a change in elimination rate over time 
since end of exposure (a combination of time- and dose-dependency). 
The  elimination  rates  based  on  the  samples  during  the  first  year  of 
observation after end of exposure were generally faster than the elimi-
nation  based  on  the  late  samples,  indicating  the  possibility  of  time- 
dependency  (Table  5).  A  time-dependency  of  elimination  was  not 
obvious for the branched PFOS, i.e. 1 m-PFOS, 3/4/5m-PFOS and 2/6m- 
PFOS. 

Initial PFAS levels were relevant as well, with positive associations 
between higher initial PFAS levels and shorter half-lives in some of the 
PFAS substances (Fig. 1, Table S4), especially in PFHxS and PFHpS. 

Age had a very strong effect on half-lives especially for the preteens, 
as  shown in  Fig. 2 (details  in Table  S5). In general, shorter half-lives 
were  associated  with  younger  ages.  The  preteens  showed  45%-60% 
shorter half-lives for different PFAS, comparing with those older than 
50, for all PFAS except PFPeS which showed the most rapid excretion in 
the mid age range. There was also some evidence for the shorter half- 
lives in adults aged below 50 than aged above 50. The scatter plot of 
individual  elimination  rate  vs  age  suggest  a  fairly  steady  decline  in 
excretion and consequent increase in half-life throughout the lifetime 
(Supplementary Figure S3). 

An effect of gender on half-life was only evident between 15 and 50 
years with female having shorter half-lives (5–22%) for all PFAS com-
pounds expect PFPeS (Fig. 3, details Table S6-8). There was no evidence 
of BMI having an effect on half-lives in adults (Table S9). 

It  is  evident  that  the  participants  with  lower  eGFR  have  12–27% 

longer half-lives for all the PFAS compounds (Fig. 4, Table S10). 

The participants with higher urine to serum ratio had short half-lives 

for all the PFAS (Fig. 5, Table S11).

ciations with half-lives, although the difference only reached statistical

4. Discussion 

We  estimated  the  half-lives  of  eight  PFAS  in  114  participants, 
recruited six months after long-term drinking water exposure stopped, 
who were followed during a four-year period with up to 10 repeated 
serum measures. The estimated half-lives varied from 0.94 to 5.01 years 
for  different  compounds.  The  inter-individual  variations  in  half-lives 
were  substantial  for  the  different  PFAS,  with  3  to  5  fold  differences 
between 5th and 95th percentiles. In general, young age, female fertile 

age,  normal  estimated  glomerular  filtration  rate,  and  possibly  gut 
inflammation and lower gut permeability were associated with shorter 
half-life. There was an evident trend that longer half-life was associated 
with longer time elapse since end of drinking water exposure. For PFHxS 
and PFHpS, a longer half-life was associated with higher initial PFHxS 
levels. 

The average half-life for PFHxS was 4.52 years in the current study, 
with a threefold difference between the 5th and 95th percentiles. To our 
knowledge, there are the only two other relatively small studies previ-
ously reporting half-life for PFHxS after end of exposure. Olsen (2007) 
reported an estimated half-life of PFHxS of geometric mean 7.3 years 
based on 24 retired fluorocarbon workers with five-year follow up. This 
is consistent with our findings as their study population aged 55 to 75 
and the half-life in the older 50 + subgroup in our study was 7.25 years. 
In contrast, Xu et al. (2020a) reported a mean half-life for PFHxS of 2.84 
years  (95%  CI  2.03,  4.43)  from  17  airport  workers,  observed  from  2 
weeks after end of exposure to 5 months later. Similarly, in the present 
study we found a shorter half-life in the first period after end of expo-
sure,  6–12  months  (3.9  years)  compared  to  the  later  stage,  so  still 
findings fairly consistent between studies. 

Our estimated half-life for PFOA with 2.47 years is in the range of 
values reported in six other studies, with estimates ranging from 1.48 to 
3.8  years  (Bartell  et  al.,  2010;  Brede  et  al.,  2010;  Olsen  et  al.,  2007; 
Worley et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2021). The half-life reported by Bartell 2.3 
years for the first year after end of exposure is close to our finding for the 
first reporting rounds. The estimate by Xu et al. was even shorter, 1.48 
years for the first 6 months after end of exposure. The longer half-life 
values are generally found in cohorts with a longer follow-up, reflect-
ing a possible time-dependent effect on excretion. The age differences 
across the cohorts, varied background exposure levels and adjustment 
methods may also contribute to the variation in estimated half-lives. 

The half-life of L-PFOS was 2.73 years, whereas for branched isomer 
1  m-PFOS,  3/4/5m-PFOS  and  2/6m-PFOS  were  5.01,  3.43  and  2.67 
years,  respectively.  1  m-PFOS  had  the  highest  half-live  estimate,  a 
finding consistent with result from a study in fish (Chen et al., 2015) and 
a human study with paired urine and serum sample (Zhang et al., 2013). 
However,  our  data  do  not  corroborate  previous  finding  of  a  higher 
excretion of branched PFOS, with exception of 1 m-PFOS, which pre-
viously has been reported (Xu et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2013). 

The estimated half-life for PFOA, PFHxS and linear PFOS were 2.47, 
4.52  and  2.73  years.  These  results  are  slightly  different  from  the  re-
ported value  based  on two-year  follow-up in the  same population (Li 
et al., 2018) due to three reasons. Firstly, in the previous publication, 
serum PFAS were analysed after each sampling round in order to provide 
rapid  feedback  to  each  participant.  However,  this  may  have  allowed 
some batch variation between laboratory analyses. In this paper, all the 
serum sample in all the ten rounds were analysed in the same batch, thus 
reducing laboratory variation. Secondly, branched and linear PFOS were 
identified separately in current study but not in the two-year follow-up. 
Thirdly, the half-lives presented in the current study are based on serum 
PFAS  levels  after  subtracting  background  levels,  and  the  background 
exposure were not considered in previous publication. Finally, we now 
have ten rounds providing more data and half-lives could be derived for 
PFPeS and PFHpS in addition. For these reasons the estimates here are 
considered more certain than those in the earlier paper. 

Our estimated half-life for PFPeS was a mean of 0.94 years (95% CI 
0.86–1.02) was slightly longer than the estimate from a smaller cohort 
with 17 airport workers with an average of 0.63 years (95% CI 0.46 to 
1.01 years). For PFHpS with a seven carbon chain length, the estimated 
half-life from our study (4.55 years, 95 %CI 4.14–5.06) was also much 
longer than from 17 airport workers (1.35 95% CI 0.77–5.71) (Xu et al., 
2021),  again  indicating  time-dependent  elimination.  However,  the 
confidence intervals of the two studies overlapped. 

Differences in elimination with elapsed time after end of exposure for 
most PFAS were observed, with a greater elimination at the start of the 
study  period  for  all  except  the  branched  PFOS.  Time-dependent

into the blood (Loccisano et al., 2011). Since the renal excretion is partly 

dependent on glomerular filtration, it is understandable that a higher 
eGFR contributes to a higher amount of urinary excretion and shorter 
half-life. Similarly, it is understandable that a higher urine/serum ratio 
is associated with a higher elimination rate, since a higher ratio means 
that a higher fraction of PFAS is excreted through the urine. Increased 
PFAS  levels  were  associated  with  decreased  urine  to  serum  ratio 
reflecting a capacity-limited of urinary elimination. However, the fact 
that urine/serum ratio is poorly correlated with eGFR strongly suggests 
that there are other mechanisms determining the urine/serum ratio. One 
such  mechanism  could  be  tubular  reabsorption  and  secretion,  which 
could  differ  between  individuals,  for  example  through  genetic  or 
epigenetic differences.

5. Conclusions 

The present study gives half-life estimates of eight PFAS, especially 
including  the  branched  PFOS-isomers.  We  found  that  the  most 

important determinants of a shorter half-life were young age and better 
kidney  function,  followed  by  female  sex  during  their  fertile  period. 
Markers of gut inflammation and reduced permeability were also asso-
ciated with shorter half-life, but should be investigated further. These 
results  provide  observational  support  for  elimination  routes  –  renal, 
fecal and maternal. 

The result also suggests a time-dependent PFAS elimination process, 
with more rapid elimination in the first year after the end of exposure, 
than later.
