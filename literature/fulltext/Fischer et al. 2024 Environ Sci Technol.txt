ABSTRACT: Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are a
diverse class of highly persistent anthropogenic chemicals that are
detectable in the serum of most humans. PFAS exposure has been
associated with many adverse effects on human health including
immunotoxicity, increased risk of certain cancers, and metabolic
disruption. PFAS binding to the most abundant blood serum
proteins (human serum albumin [HSA] and globulins) is thought
to affect transport to active sites, toxicity, and elimination half-lives.
However, few studies have investigated the competitive binding of
PFAS to these proteins in human serum. Here, we use C18 solid-
phase microextraction fibers to measure HSA−water and globulin−
water distribution coefficients (DHSA/w, Dglob/w) for PFAS with
carbon chains containing 4 to 13 perfluorinated carbons (ηpfc = 4−
13) and several functional head-groups. PFAS with ηpfc < 7 were highly bound to HSA relative to globulins, whereas PFAS with ηpfc
≥ 7 showed a greater propensity for binding to globulins. Experimentally measured DHSA/w and Dglob/w and concentrations of serum
proteins successfully predicted the variability in PFAS binding in human serum. We estimated that the unbound fraction of serum
PFAS varied by up to a factor of 2.5 among individuals participating in the 2017−2018 U.S. National Health and Nutrition
Examination Survey. These results suggest that serum HSA and globulins are important covariates for epidemiological studies aimed
at understanding the effects of PFAS exposure.
KEYWORDS: exposure, unbound fraction, interindividual variability, solid-phase microextraction, human serum albumin, globulins

1. INTRODUCTION

Exposures to per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), a
diverse class of highly fluorinated anthropogenic chemicals,
have been linked to many adverse health effects in humans.1−6
The extreme resistance of the perfluorinated carbon bond to
degradation and high production volumes used by industry
and in consumer products have resulted in their ubiquitous
presence in the environment and detection in the serum of
98−99% of United States (U.S.) individuals.7−9 PFAS binding
to serum proteins is known to affect
their accumulation,
circulatory distribution, and elimination in humans.10−15
Specifically, the unbound fraction of a toxicant in blood is
typically more available for transport to organs and more likely
to reach molecular targets that elicit toxic effects.15,16 PFAS
bound to proteins are thought
to be inaccessible to
biomolecules involved in transformation and elimination
processes and are recirculated in the bloodstream, thereby
extending elimination half-lives.17,18 Two of the most abundant
proteins in blood are human serum albumin (HSA) and
globulins. However, competitive binding of diverse PFAS to
for
these proteins

in human serum and implications

toxicokinetics in the human body are poorly understood and
are the focus of this work.

PFAS binding to serum proteins can be expressed by the
distribution coefficient (D), which encompasses the binding of
both charged and neutral PFAS to the protein.19 Past work has
reported distribution coefficients for human serum albumin
(HSA, i.e., DHSA/w).20 However, binding of PFAS to globulins,
the second most abundant class of blood proteins, is poorly
characterized (i.e., globulin−water distribution coefficients,
Dglob/w), as is competitive interactions between these two
proteins and PFAS. PFAS are strongly bound to
HSA10,12−15,21,22 and DHSA/w generally increases with greater
perfluorinated carbon-chain length (ηpfc) and hydrophobic-
ity.11,14,23,24 These properties may explain the long elimination
half-lives for longer chain PFAS (ηpfc ≥ 6) in humans.25−27

The unbound fraction of PFAS in blood (f unbound) can be
estimated from the distribution coefficients for HSA and
globulins and measured serum protein levels. Protein
concentrations measured in human serum (i.e., the liquid
component of blood after the blood has clotted) are known to
vary based on environmental and longitudinal factors28 and
disease status.29,30 Quantifying the unbound fraction of PFAS
in blood is useful for understanding their tissue distribution in
the human body31 and variability in elimination half-lives for
PFAS across individuals.27 Epidemiological studies investigat-
ing associations between PFAS serum concentrations and
health outcomes in humans may also benefit from accounting
for variability in blood protein concentrations and binding
affinities for different PFAS as additional covariates.

Distribution coefficients of chemicals to blood proteins and
other biomolecules can be measured using solid-phase
microextraction (SPME) techniques.32 The difference in
chemical mass sampled by an SPME fiber in the presence
and absence of biomolecules in solution represents the
chemical fraction bound to the biomolecules of interest. A
distribution coefficient can be derived from this fraction and
the mass of the biomolecule in solution. This approach avoids
extractions of chemicals from biomolecules, thereby minimiz-
ing laboratory work and matrix effects during chemical analysis.
The utility of C18 SPME fibers for measuring PFAS serum
protein binding has been demonstrated in past work that used
them to measure binding of
four PFAS to bovine serum
albumin (BSA), human plasma, and in vitro cell homoge-
nates.16

The main objectives of this work were to (1) investigate
competitive binding of PFAS to two major proteins (HSA and
γ-globulin) in human serum using SPME techniques and (2)
characterize variability in the unbound fraction of PFAS in the
human body. We focused on distribution of PFAS to γ-
globulin because it is the most abundant protein within the
class of globulins in human serum.33 We also compared
binding of PFAS to HSA and bovine serum albumin (BSA)
because it is often used as a proxy for HSA.12,16,21 To do this,
we adapted and modified a previously developed C18 SPME
fiber method17 to measure distribution coefficients of PFAS
with ηpfc = 4−13 and different
functional head groups
(carboxylic acids, sulfonic acids, and sulfonamides) to proteins
in human serum. We chose these PFAS because preliminary
studies of extractable organofluorine (EOF) as a proxy for total
PFAS suggest that they account for most PFAS in human
serum, despite production of diverse chemicals in this
class.34,35 We evaluated whether PFAS distribution to HSA
and globulins is a reasonable predictor for the unbound
fraction of PFAS in serum ( f unbound) by comparing
experimental results to measured serum−water distribution
coefficients (Dserum/w). To better understand potential
variability in the unbound fraction of PFAS in blood at the
population level, we examined variability in HSA and globulin
in the 2017−2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination
Survey (NHANES) data.9 We discuss how experimental
distribution coefficients for blood proteins and associated
proxies for the unbound fraction of PFAS in blood can be
leveraged to improve toxicokinetic (TK) models and to assess
susceptibility to PFAS exposures.

2. MATERIALS & METHODS

2.1. Materials and Chemicals. C18 SPME fibers with a
calculated coating volume of 520 nL were purchased from

Supelco (#57281-U).32 PFAS purchased from Wellington
Laboratories were tested as single chemicals or in batch
experiments as mixtures. A mixture of five PFAS was used for
method development, while a 32 PFAS mixture was used for
protein binding experiments. For batch experiments with 32
PFAS, the PFAC−24PAR mixture from Wellington Labo-
ratories was combined with additional PFAS that were not
included in the mixture. Mass-labeled mixtures of PFAS were
used as internal standards (Wellington Laboratories). Addi-
tional information on native and labeled PFAS standards is
provided in the Supporting Information (SI, Table S1).
Methanol (MeOH) and deionized water (DI) were used as
solvents. Phosphate buffered saline (PBS: 0.01 M, NaCl: 0.138
M; KCl: 0.0027 M) yielding a pH of 7.4 at 25 °C was
purchased from Sigma (#P3813).

Human serum albumin (Sigma, no. A1887) and γ-globulins
(Sigma, no. G4386) were used as reference serum proteins. A
serum standard from a human male residing in the U.S. was
used for experiments and contained a total protein
concentration of 40−90 g/L (Sigma, #H4522, see Supporting
Information (SI) Section S1 for product specifications).

2.2. Preparation and Storage of C18 Fibers. SPME
fibers were stored in 5 mL polypropylene (PP) tubes
containing 4 mL of a MeOH/DI mixture (50:50) until use
in experiments. During storage, preconditioning, and experi-
ments, the fiber holders were stabilized in prepunctured caps
to avoid damaging the fiber coating (see Section S2, Figures S1
and S2). SPME fibers were incubated in 100% MeOH and
100% DI at room temperature for ≥2 h and ≥10 min,
respectively, to precondition them for binding experiments
before transfer to 50 mL falcon tubes for binding experiments.
Rapid transfer of SPME fibers among storage, preconditioning,
and experimental tubes avoided drying of the C18 polymer
coating.

2.3. Setup of Binding Experiments. The molar ratio of
PFAS to proteins was kept below 0.004 (HSA, BSA) and 0.009
(γ-globulin) in all binding experiments (i.e., 261 and 111 mol
of protein per mol of PFAS, respectively), which is well below
where saturation of serum proteins was observed in previous
studies that measured binding isotherms for PFAS11,16,36−38
and other chemicals.19,24,32 For example, nonlinearity of BSA
binding has been observed for PFBA, PFOA, PFHxS, and
ratios of ≥0.39,16 emphasizing that our
PFOS at molar
experiments were conducted at concentrations within the
linear binding range. Low molar ratios were selected to mimic
binding in serum at PFAS concentrations relevant to human
exposure, based on ratios of ≤0.0005 that were estimated from
PFAS concentrations reported in human sera.9

The BSA, HSA, γ-globulins, and human serum were
dissolved in defined amounts (varied among experiments) in
40 mL of PBS in a 50 mL falcon tube and incubated in a
horizontal shaker at 37 °C and 100 rpm for ≥15 min. A 40 mL
PBS solution free of proteins/serum was prepared and treated
similarly as a control. Defined amounts of PFAS stock
solutions in MeOH were added to 50 mL falcon tubes and
evaporated to dryness to avoid MeOH contamination in PBS
samples. The PBS and protein solutions were added to the
falcon tubes and incubated on the horizontal shaker at 37 °C
and 100 rpm for ≥1 h. The solution was evenly distributed in 4
mL aliquots to PP tubes (n = 3 to 5 replicates). Preconditioned
SPME fibers were transferred to the tubes while ensuring that
the fiber coating was completely immersed in the liquid.

2.4. C18 Fiber Binding Experiments. We first performed
distribution experiments between the C18 fiber and PBS buffer
(i.e., protein-free water) using three perfluoroalkyl carboxylates
(PFCA: perfluoroheptanoate: PFHpA, perfluorooctanoate:
PFOA, and perfluorononanoate: PFNA), and two perfluor-
oalkyl sulfonates (PFSA: perfluorohexanesulfonate: PFHxS
and perfluorooctanesulfonate: PFOS) as the test chemicals. To
assess the reproducibility of distribution experiments, the C18-
water distribution coefficient (DC18/w) of PFOA was measured
after 24 h of fiber−water incubation at 37 °C and 100 rpm
horizontal agitation (n = 10). All subsequent experiments were
performed at 37 °C and 100 rpm. DC18/w for PFHpA, PFOA,
PFNA, PFHxS, and PFOS were determined after 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8,
24, and 48 h of incubation (n = 3 per time point) to determine
the time until fiber-water equilibrium was achieved. We tested
for variability in DC18/w across three log units in water
concentrations (0.2−42 μg L−1) of the five PFAS to assess if
saturation of the C18 fiber material occurred (n = 3 per
concentration, 48 h incubation). DC18/w were determined for
each PFAS in the 32 PFAS mixture after 48 and 96 h to
confirm that fiber−water equilibrium was achieved for all study

chemicals (n = 4 per time point). Recoveries were measured in
all experiments in vials containing PBS buffer only (n = 3 to 5)
to confirm that no significant binding or loss processes other
than to C18 fibers and dissolved proteins occurred.

2.5. Protein Binding Experiments. We compared
distribution coefficients for PFHpA, PFOA, PFNA, PFHxS,
and PFOS to bovine serum albumin (DBSA/w) after 48 h
incubations (n = 4) against previously measured literature
values11,16 to validate the methodology used in this study.
Fiber−water−protein equilibrium was assessed for the 32
PFAS by measuring DHSA/w after 48 and 96 h (n = 4 per time
point). Notably, the distribution coefficients reported in this
study were determined at molar ratios of PFAS to proteins
≤0.01, which is approximately 100 times lower than the molar
ratio at which saturation was observed for BSA.16 This should
be considered when applying these distribution coefficients to
systems with higher PFAS-to-protein ratios, such as in vitro
toxicity tests. Binding to proteins was not expected to be rate-
limiting based on previous work.39 However, binding proper-
ties of proteins may change as denaturation of proteins, such as
albumin and globulins occurs. Hence, our reported Dprotein/w
were not measured in test durations of >48 h. Following
method validation with BSA, distribution coefficients of the
PFAS mixture to HSA (DHSA/w), γ-globulins (Dglob/w), and
human serum (Dserum/w) were measured (n = 4 per type of
protein).

2.6. Chemical Analysis using HPLC-MS/MS. Targeted
PFAS in this study were measured using an online SPE method
on an Agilent (Santa Clara, CA) 6460 triple quadrupole liquid
chromatography−tandem mass
spectrometer at Harvard
University.40,41 Additional
information on chemical analysis
is summarized in Section S4, SI. Linear and branched isomers
were individually measured, and the reported distribution
coefficients integrate the sum of isomers in the mixture.

3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

3.1. Method Development and Validation. We
observed good reproducibility (relative standard deviation,
RSD = 1−11%) across replicate fiber−water distribution
experiments (Table S2; Figures S3 and S4). Recoveries of 90−
110% were observed for PFAS with ηpfc ≤ 9 and 75−110% for
PFAS with ηpfc > 9 (Figures S5 and S6). These results confirm
losses of PFAS due to binding or
negligible to small
denaturation processes during fiber−water distribution experi-
ments. Kinetic experiments with five PFAS showed equilibrium
in the C18 SPME fibers was achieved within 48 h (Figure 1A).
Rapid uptake of PFAS by the SPME fibers reduces the
likelihood that biological materials degrade over the duration
of the experiment.

DC18/w deviated by <30% between 48 and 96 h for all PFAS
(Table S3). DHSA/w decreased between 48 and 96 h,
particularly for ηpfc > 9 PFAS (Table S4). This may reflect
denaturation of the proteins and hence reduced sorption of

Linearity of sorption to fibers was observed for the five initial

3.2. Binding to C18 Fibers, Serum Albumin, Glob-
ulins, and Human Serum. Binding to C18 fibers (i.e., log
DC18/w) increased with increasing molecular weight (Mw) of
the PFAS up to ∼600 g/mol (Figure 2A and Table S2). No
differences were observed for PFAS with different functional
groups (carboxylates, sulfonates, sulfonamides) or carbon
chain structures (fluorotelomers) that had similar molecular
weight. These results suggest that the sorption affinity of PFAS
for the C18 polymer is predominantly size-dependent (i.e.,
dependent on the chain length of the perfluorinated tail and
the size of the functional group of the molecule).

Bovine serum albumin (BSA) has been previously used as a
surrogate for HSA in toxicokinetic models for PFAS.12,16,21
Our experiments reproduced literature values for DBSA/w within
0.5 log units (Figures S7 and S8). DHSA/w and DBSA/w agreed
within 0.25 log units for 23 out of 27 PFAS. However, we
found sorption to HSA was 0.5−1 log units higher than BSA
for perfluorohexanoate (PFHxA), 5:3 fluorotelomer carbox-
ylate (5:3 FTCA), perfluorobutanesulfonate (PFBS), and
perfluoroheptanesulfonate (PFHpS) (Figure S9). This may
reflect relatively more favorable electrostatic interactions with
binding sites on HSA compared to those on BSA for PFAS
with ηpfc = 3−7. These results suggest DBSA/w may not be a
good proxy for predicting the distribution to HSA for PFHxA,
5:3 FTCA, PFBS, and PFHpS.

PFAS binding to HSA (DHSA/w) generally increased with
higher Mw up until ∼400 g/mol, followed by a plateau above
this level (Figure 2B, Table S2). This result agrees with past
mechanistic studies that showed a strong increase in HSA
binding driven by electrostatic interactions (polar headgroup)
and hydrophobic effects (nonpolar C−F chain) with PFAS
carbon chain lengths up to ηpfc = 8.11,13 Both types of
interactions are accommodated in HSA cavities for ηpfc ≤ 8
PFAS. For ηpfc > 8 PFAS, reduced molecular flexibility limits
their accessibility to the HSA binding pockets.13 A relatively
higher DHSA/w for PFHpS compared to PFNA (Figure 2B)
could be explained by the additional hydrogen bond
established by the sulfonic headgroup.13

PFAS to γ-globulins differed from patterns observed for
HSA. PFAS with a Mw < 400 exhibit relatively weak binding
(Dglob/w < 100). Distribution coefficients for globulins (log
Dglob/w) increased linearly for Mw between 400−600 g/mol but
declined at Mw > 600 g/mol. γ-Globulins are larger than HSA
(Mw of γ-globulins = 1193 kDa; Mw of HSA = 67 kDa) and are
not known to bind with fatty acids. Binding of small molecules
(<1 kDa) to blood globulins is generally considered to be of
minor importance for the unbound fraction in human blood,43
with the exception of α1-acid glycoprotein binding of basic
drugs like propranolol.44 While γ-globulins possess binding
sites for endogenous macromolecules like antigens (Mw ≈ 150
kDa),45 binding of <1 kDa molecules is thought to be driven
by hydrophobic effects with the relatively large surface area of

3.3. Distribution of PFAS in Human Serum. Binding to
human serum measured using the C18 fiber method (Dserum/w)
increased linearly with Mw up to a peak of around 600 g/mol
(Figure 2D). This trend is similar to that observed for HSA
and γ-globulins (Figure 2B,C). Albumin appears to drive the
distribution of PFAS with a Mw < 500 in the serum as
illustrated by Dglob/w/DHSA/w ratios < 0.1 (Figure S10). The
importance of PFAS binding to globulins (and related
cotransport) increases with Mw until Dglob/w/DHSA/w ratios of
∼1 for PFAS with ηpfc = 12−13. Thus, globulins should be
considered when assessing bioaccumulation of PFAS with
Dglob/w/DHSA/w ratios of >0.1. HSA and globulins constitute the
majority of serum biomolecules47 but several other bio-
molecules, such as phospholipid-like fats, are present at lower
concentrations and could also act as binding substrates for
PFAS.48,49

Values for Dserum/w suggest that PFAS are generally strongly
bound to proteins in human serum (f unbound 0.01−1.2%). The
estimated unbound fraction of PFAS decreased with an
increasing molecular weight up to an inflection point at 600
g/mol, after which it
increased again (Figure 3A). The
unbound fraction of PFAS in serum was lower (0.035%) for
PFHpS (ηpfc = 7 sulfonate), compared to PFOA (ηpfc = 7
carboxylate = 0.061%), PFHxS (ηpfc = 6 sulfonate = 0.041%),
and PFOS (ηpfc = 8 sulfonate PFOS = 0.042%) (Figure 3A).
We attribute this to the stronger relative binding of PFHpS to
HSA (Figure 2B). Longer elimination half-lives in human
serum have been reported for the PFAS with the greatest
binding to serum proteins (lowest f unbound) reported in this
work (PFHpS ≈ PFHxS > PFOS > PFOA).26,50,51

that was directedly measured and that predicted from protein

concentrations for predicting their f unbound and Cunbound in
human populations.

binding to globulins is thus more important for individuals
with albumin deficiency and vice versa. Variability in blood

results suggest variability in f unbound may help to explain such
differences.53

3.5.

Implications for Toxicokinetic Modeling and
Epidemiological Studies. Results of this study show that
distribution coefficients for diverse PFAS to serum proteins
and other blood constituents can be measured using the C18
SPME fiber method. Further, PFAS binding in human serum
can be approximated by distribution coefficients (Dglob/w and
DHSA/w) and concentrations of globulins and HSA. Less than
1.2% of PFAS were estimated to be unbound in human serum
in this study because they are strongly bound to proteins. Mw <
500 PFAS mainly bind to albumin (fraction bound to albumin
>98%). Binding to globulin becomes more significant as Mw
increases, with approximately equal contributions of the two
proteins to the total binding expected for Mw > 600 PFAS.
Based on albumin and globulin concentrations of individuals,
we estimated that f unbound of PFAS varied by up to a factor of
2.5 among individuals participating in the 2017−18 NHANES
survey cycle.9 While f unbound of Mw < 500 PFAS is driven by the
presence of albumin, lower variability in f unbound of Mw > 500
PFAS is expected due to comparable protein binding strengths
and inversely correlated concentrations of albumin and
globulins in human serum.

The strong agreement between predicted and experimental
Dserum/w in this study suggests that our method is suitable for
predicting the unbound fraction of PFAS in the serum of
individuals. Given the simplicity and low costs of protein
assays, serum proteins could be more routinely measured
concurrently with PFAS exposures in human biomonitoring
studies to develop such estimates at an individual level. While
we compared predicted and experimental Dserum/w for serum of
a single donor, concurrent measurements of blood protein and
PFAS concentrations in human biomonitoring studies could
further evaluate the accuracy of our approach in predicting
Dserum/w and f unbound. We used γ-globulins as a reference protein
for the entire class of serum globulins. Future studies should
aim to investigate variances in PFAS binding to other blood
proteins to evaluate this assumption. Measuring additional
serum proteins and biomarkers (e.g., hepatic enzymes and
membrane transporters)54 may explain the differences in
predicted and experimental Dserum/w observed for 6:2 FtS, 5:3
FTCA, 7:3 FTCA, and PFHpS (Figure 3B).

Our results suggest the relationship between external PFAS
exposure and internal body burdens could be refined by
accounting for variabilities in blood protein concentrations
among individuals.55,56 Such refinements may also strengthen
relationships between PFAS exposure and adverse health
effects by improving estimates of internal dose and should be
investigated in future work. Binding data reported in this study
are useful for parametrizing physiologically based toxicokinetic
(PBTK) models that can help elucidate the mechanisms
underlying accumulation, elimination, and toxicity of PFAS. In
combination with PBTK models, analyses of individual Cunbound
could reveal the role of variable blood protein concentrations
attributed to genetic, environmental, and longitudinal factors28
to susceptibility to PFAS toxicity and elimination half-lives in
humans.56,57 Diseases that alter blood protein levels, such as
albumin deficiency in nonalcohol fatty liver disease,29 could
potentially trigger a feedback effect by allowing higher
bioactive concentrations to reach already impaired target
organs like a fatty liver and such hypotheses should be
examined in future work. The SPME fiber method developed
in this work could be used to investigate the binding of PFAS
to tissue homogenates derived from target organs, such as the
liver and kidneys. Resulting blood-tissue distribution coef-

ficients can be compared to organ concentrations in humans to
assess the relevance of toxicokinetic processes beyond tissue
binding such as enterohepatic recirculation and renal tubular
reabsorption.58,59

S-4. Summary of analytical methods.

SPME  fiber  extracts  and  PBS  samples  were  analyzed  for  32  PFAS  with  an  Agilent  (Santa 

Clara,  California,  U.S.)  6460 

triple  quadrupole 

liquid  chromatography-tandem  mass 

spectrometer equipped with an Agilent 1290 Infinity Flex Cube online SPE located at Harvard 

University.  PFAS concentrations were determined from a 12-point calibration curve ranging 

from 1 to 8,000 ng L-1. Instrumental blanks (to monitor cross-contamination and carry-over) 

and  the  calibration  curve  were  prepared  with  50:50  MeOH:DI  water  with  internal  standard 

concentrations matching the samples. All analytes and internal standards were monitored for 

in electrospray ionization (ESI) negative mode. The linear and branched isomers of PFHxS 

and  PFOS  were  quantified  separately  using  calibration  curves  for  each  isomer.  Initial 

conditions were 97% 2 mM ammonium acetate in water (A) and 3% 2 mM ammonium acetate 

in methanol (B). From 0.85 to 3.5 minutes the gradient was linearly increased to 54% B. From 

3.5 to 16 minutes the gradient was linearly increased to 85% B and then linearly increased to 

100% B until the end of the run (16.5 minutes).
