CITATION: Harada KH, Hashida S, Kaneko T, Takenaka K, Minata M, Inoue K, Saito N, Koizumi A.
  Biliary excretion and cerebrospinal fluid partition of perfluorooctanoate and
  perfluorooctane sulfonate in humans. Environ Toxicol Pharmacol 2007;24(2):134-139.
  PMID 21783801. doi:10.1016/j.etap.2007.04.003.

ABSTRACT (verbatim)
"Perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS) and perfluorooctanoate (PFOA) are detected in the
environment and, more specifically, in wildlife and humans. The large variation in the
reported biological half-lives for PFOA and PFOS has remained unexplored. In this study, we
aimed to evaluate their partition from serum to bile and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in
humans. Four pairs of serum and bile, and 7 pairs of serum and CSF were donated by patients.
In considering biliary excretion, the median concentrations of PFOA and PFOS in serum
samples were 3.8 and 23.2 ng/mL, respectively, whereas those in bile samples were 1.0 and
27.9 ng/mL, respectively. The median ratio of PFOS concentrations (bile/serum: 0.60) was
significantly higher than that for PFOA, 0.21 (p<0.01). Biliary excretion rates for PFOA and
PFOS in the present study subjects were estimated as 1.06 and 2.98 mL/kg/day, respectively,
which is significantly higher than serum clearances via urine in humans and might represent
a major excretion route. Biliary reabsorption rates of PFOA and PFOS were estimated to be
0.89 and 0.97, respectively. In considering partition into the cerebrospinal fluid, the
median concentrations of PFOA and PFOS in serum samples were 2.6 and 18.4 ng/mL,
respectively, whereas those in CSF samples were 0.06 and 0.10 ng/mL, respectively. The
median ratio of PFOS concentrations (CSF/serum: 9.1 (x10-3)) was comparable to that of PFOA,
17.6 (x10-3), suggesting that PFOA and PFOS cannot pass through the blood-brain barrier
freely. In conclusion, the biliary excretion of these compounds was comparable in both rats
and humans and the long half-lives in humans might be attributable to low levels of
excretion in urine and high biliary reabsorption rates."

VALUES
  n = 4 serum/bile pairs (patients with biliary drainage); n = 7 serum/CSF pairs.
  Bile/serum ratio: PFOS 0.60, PFOA 0.21 (p<0.01).
  BILIARY clearance: PFOA 1.06 mL/kg/day, PFOS 2.98 mL/kg/day.
  Biliary REABSORPTION fraction: PFOA 0.89, PFOS 0.97.
  CSF/serum ratio: PFOA 17.6 x 10^-3, PFOS 9.1 x 10^-3.
  Bile flow assumed 350 mL/day (per Andersson et al. 2025 sec 4, who re-derive the ratio
  using 604 mL/day from Boyer & Bloomer 1974 as a sensitivity check).

SCALE CHECK, done by Andersson et al. 2025 (Environ Res 285:122487) sec 4, quoted verbatim:
  "Dividing our fecal clearance of 0.017 mL/day/kg for PFOA and 0.046 mL/day/kg for PFOS ...
   with their respective biliary clearance yield ratios of 1.6 % for PFOA and 1.5 % for
   PFOS. However, Harada et al. assumed a conservative bile flow of 350 mL/day, while
   another study estimated it to be 604 mL/day. If a bile flow of 604 mL is instead assumed,
   the ratios of biliary/fecal clearance would be 0.9 % for both PFOA and PFOS. This
   suggests that only a small percentage of the PFOA and PFOS (and possibly other PFAS) that
   is excreted in bile ends up in feces, i.e., there is an enterohepatic recirculation of
   approximately 99 %."
=> Harada 2007's biliary clearance is a clearance into bile, NOT a net elimination; ~99% is
   reabsorbed in the gut. This is exactly the step that bile-acid sequestrants interrupt,
   and it is why cholestyramine raises fecal PFOS 23-fold (Andersson 2025) while probenecid
   (an OAT inhibitor acting on the renal route) does nothing.
