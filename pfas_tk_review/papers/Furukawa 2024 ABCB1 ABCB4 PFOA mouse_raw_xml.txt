The role of ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters in PFOA toxicokinetics is unknown.</p></sec><sec id="sec2" disp-level="2"><title>Methods</title><p>In this study, two ABC transporters, ABCB1 and ABCB4, were examined in mice with single intravenous PFOA administration (3.13 µmol/kg). To identify candidate renal PFOA transporters, we used a microarray approach to evaluate changes in gene expression of various kidney transporters in <italic>Abcb4</italic> null mice.</p></sec><sec id="sec3" disp-level="2"><title>Results</title><p>Biliary PFOA concentrations were lower in <italic>Abcb4</italic> null mice (mean ± standard deviation: 0.25 ± 0.12 µg/mL) than in wild-type mice (0.87 ± 0.02 µg/mL). Immunohistochemically, ABCB4 expression was confirmed at the apical region of hepatocytes. However, renal clearance of PFOA was higher in <italic>Abcb4</italic> null mice than in wild-type mice. Among 642 solute carrier and ABC transporters, 5 transporters showed significant differences in expression between wild-type and <italic>Abcb4</italic> null mice. These candidates included two major xenobiotic transporters, multidrug resistance 1 (<italic>Abcb1</italic>) and organic anion transporter 3 (<italic>Slc22a8</italic>). <italic>Abcb1</italic> mRNA levels were higher in <italic>Abcb4</italic> null mice than in wild-type mice in kidney. In <italic>Abcb4</italic> null mice, <italic>Abcb1b</italic> expression was enhanced in proximal tubules immunohistochemically, while that of <italic>Slc22a8</italic> was not. Finally, in <italic>Abcb1a/b</italic> null mice, there was a significant decrease in the renal clearance of PFOA (0.69 ± 0.21 vs 1.1 mL ± 0.37/72 h in wild-type mice). A homology search of ABCB1 showed that several amino acids are mutated in humans compared with those in rodents and monkeys.</p></sec><sec id="sec4" disp-level="2"><title>Conclusions</title><p>These findings suggest that, in the mouse, <italic>Abcb4</italic> and <italic>Abcb1</italic> are excretory transporters of PFOA into bile and urine, respectively.</p></sec><sec id="sec5" disp-level="2"><title>Supplementary information</title><p>The online version contains supplementary material available at <ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://doi.org/10.1265/ehpm.23-00284" ext-link-type="uri">https://doi.org/10.1265/ehpm.23-00284</ext-link>.</p></sec><sec id="kwd-group1" sec-type="kwd-group" disp-level="2"><p><bold>Keywords:</bold> Perfluorooctanoic acid, ABCB1, ABCB4, Renal clearance, Biliary excretion</p></sec></abstract><abstract id="abstract2" abstract-type="graphical"><title>Graphical Abstract</title>

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs) have been used since approximately the 1940s as water and oil repellents, aqueous film-forming foam as a fire extinguisher, and surfactants [<xref rid="r04" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>]. PFASs are man-made chemicals with a structure in which the hydrogen on the carbon chain is replaced by fluorine atoms. Therefore, many PFASs are chemically stable substances, and even if they are partially metabolized or decomposed, they become stable end products and are believed to remain in the environment for a long period. The Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development estimated that there are more than 4,730 chemicals that are in the PFAS category [<xref rid="r05" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>].</p><p>Among the PFASs, perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) is mainly used as a processing aid for fluoropolymer and surfactant production [<xref rid="r06" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>]. Contamination of PFOA around fluoropolymer manufacturing plants has been reported [<xref rid="r07" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>–<xref rid="r09" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>]. PFOA poses particular public health concerns because of its long-term persistence and bioaccumulation in the environment and in humans [<xref rid="r10" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>–<xref rid="r13" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>]. The production of PFOA is internationally regulated under the Stockholm Convention [<xref rid="r14" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>].</p><p>The effects of PFOA on health have been reported. The International Agency for Research on Cancer currently lists PFOA as Group 1 (carcinogenic to humans) [<xref rid="r15" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>]. Tumors of the liver, mammary gland, testis, and pancreas have been observed in rodents, and epidemiological studies have suggested an association between PFOA exposure and kidney and testicular cancers [<xref rid="r16" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>]. The C8 Science Panel, which is an independent panel of scientists, conducted a survey of 69,000 residents as a result of a settlement between DuPont and residents in Ohio and West Virginia, USA. The panel ultimately concluded that there are “probable links” between PFOA exposure and high cholesterol concentrations, kidney cancer, testicular cancer, thyroid disease, ulcerative colitis, and gestational hypertension [<xref rid="r16" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>]. The association between PFOA exposure and kidney cancer was confirmed independently in a nested case–control study from the Prostate, Liver, Colorectal, Ovarian (PLCO) Trial [<xref rid="r17" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>].</p><p>The cause of PFOA’s extremely long half-life in humans is not well understood. An epidemiological study of retired workers from a PFOA production plant operated by 3M showed that the plasma elimination half-life was 3.8 years [<xref rid="r18" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>]. In contrast, the plasma elimination half-life of PFOA is much shorter in mice (15–20 days) [<xref rid="r19" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>], rats (&lt;1–15 days) [<xref rid="r20" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>], and cynomolgus monkeys (20–35 days) than in humans [<xref rid="r21" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>]. The reason for the long half-life of PFOA in humans is because of the virtual absence of renal excretion [<xref rid="r22" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>], while biliary excretion of PFOA is high compared with renal excretion [<xref rid="r23" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>, <xref rid="r24" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>]. Several organic anion transporters (solute carrier family 22, member 6, [<italic>SLC22A6</italic>] and <italic>SLC22A8</italic>) transport PFOA in rats and humans, but cannot explain the low excretion rate in the kidney because there is no significant difference in their activities [<xref rid="r25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>]. <italic>SLC22A11</italic> encoding human OAT4 also transports PFOA <italic>in vitro</italic> [<xref rid="r26" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>], which may confer the placental barrier. Human intestinal Caco-2 cells show uptake of different PFASs, including PFOA, suggesting involvement of organic anion transporting polypeptides such as OATP2B1 [<xref rid="r27" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>, <xref rid="r28" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>]. In addition, Na<sup>+</sup>/taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide (SLC10A1) and apical sodium-dependent bile acid transporter (SLC10A2) mediate uptake of PFASs into hepatocytes [<xref rid="r29" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>]. In our previous study [<xref rid="r30" ref-type="bibr">30</xref>], biliary excretion of PFOA was associated with the expression of ATP-binding cassette (ABC) sub-family B, member 4 (<italic>Abcb4</italic>: <italic>Mdr2</italic>) in the liver of mice. However, there have been few investigations on active transporters of PFASs <italic>in vivo</italic>. In this study, we aimed to examine two ABC transporters, ABCB1 and ABCB4, for biliary and renal excretion of PFOA in knockout mice.</p></sec><sec id="sec7" disp-level="1"><title>Material and methods</title><sec id="sec8" disp-level="2"><title>Animals</title><p>Animal studies, including animal care and all experimental procedures, were in accordance with the Animal Welfare Guidelines of Kyoto University. All experimental procedures were reviewed and approved by the Kyoto University Animal Research Committee (approval number: MedKyo11069). Wild-type mice (FVB) and <italic>Abcb4</italic> null mice (FVB.129P2-<italic>Abcb4<sup>tm1Bor</sup></italic>/J, FVB <italic>Abcb4<sup>(−/−)</sup></italic>) were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). <italic>Abcb1a/1b</italic> null mice (FVB.129P2-<italic>Abcb1a<sup>tm1Bor</sup> Abcb1b<sup>tm1Bor</sup></italic>/N12, FVB <italic>Mdr1a/1b<sup>(−/−)</sup></italic>) were purchased from Taconic Farms (Germantown, NY, USA). The mice were housed in the Kyoto University Institute of Laboratory Animals. A standard commercial lab chow diet (F-2, 3.73 kcal/g; Funahashi Farm Corp., Chiba, Japan) was used. All mice were maintained at an ambient temperature of 24 °C ± 2 °C and 50% ± 10% humidity with a 12 h dark-light cycle (lights on at 7:00 a.m.).</p></sec><sec id="sec9" disp-level="2"><title>Experimental design</title><p>All experiments were performed with male mice aged 8–10 weeks (25–30 g). The number of mice in each group ranged from 3 (<italic>Abcb4</italic> KO/WT groups) to 5 (<italic>Abcb1a/b</italic> KO/WT groups). Mice were intravenously administered PFOA as a single dose (dose: 3.13 µmol/kg, injection volume: 10 mL/kg).</p><p>We conducted two kinetic studies. The first study was conducted to observe the time course of plasma PFOA concentrations. In this experiment, blood was collected at 0, 2, 24, and 72 h after PFOA administration. Mice were killed by cervical dislocation under sevoflurane anesthesia after observation for 72 h. At sacrifice, tissue samples and blood were collected. Blood samples were centrifuged to separate plasma at 800 × <italic>g</italic>. The remaining carcass was flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.</p><p>In the second experiment, we determined renal clearance. Urine was collected at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h after PFOA administration, and blood and bile were collected at the sacrifice. To calculate renal clearance of PFOA (CL<sub>r</sub>), we used the following equation:</p><disp-formula id="e"><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="m1" display="block" overflow="scroll"><mml:mtable columnalign="right left right left right left right left right left right left" rowspacing="3pt" columnspacing="0em 2em 0em 2em 0em 2em 0em 2em 0em 2em 0em" displaystyle="true"><mml:mtr><mml:mtd/><mml:mtd><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mtext mathvariant="italic">CL</mml:mtext><mml:mi>r</mml:mi></mml:msub><mml:mtext mathvariant="italic"> of PFOA (mL/observed duration)</mml:mtext></mml:mrow><mml:mo>=</mml:mo></mml:mtd></mml:mtr><mml:mtr><mml:mtd/><mml:mtd><mml:mspace width="1em"/><mml:mfrac><mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mtext mathvariant="italic">cumulative amount of PFOA in urine (</mml:mtext><mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mi>μ</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:mrow><mml:mtext mathvariant="italic">g/observed duration)</mml:mtext></mml:mrow></mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mtext mathvariant="italic">PFOA concentration in plasma at 72</mml:mtext><mml:mrow><mml:mspace width="thinmathspace"/></mml:mrow><mml:mtext mathvariant="italic">h (</mml:mtext><mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mi>μ</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:mrow><mml:mtext mathvariant="italic">g/mL)</mml:mtext></mml:mrow></mml:mrow></mml:mfrac></mml:mtd></mml:mtr></mml:mtable></mml:math></disp-formula></sec><sec id="sec10" disp-level="2"><title>Determination of PFOA concentrations in biological samples</title><p>In a polypropylene tube, diluted plasma, urine, or homogenized kidney was combined with 10 µL of a 1 µg/mL solution of <sup>13</sup>C<sub>2</sub>-PFOA as an internal standard (donated by the Environmental Protection Agency of the USA, originally synthesized by Perkin Elmer, Boston, MA, USA). A volume of 1 mL of tetrabutylammonium hydrogen sulfate was added to 2 mL of 0.5 M sodium carbonate buffer solution (pH adjusted to 10) and vortexed, and then 2 mL of methyl <italic>tert</italic>-butyl ether was added and vortexed [<xref rid="r13" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>]. The tube was centrifuged to separate the aqueous and organic phases, and the methyl <italic>tert</italic>-butyl ether layer was transferred to a glass tube and evaporated to dryness under a gentle stream of dry nitrogen. The residue was then re-dissolved in 100 µL of 100 mM benzyl bromide acetone and was heated for 1 hour at 60 °C in an autosampler vial. Derivatized extracts were analyzed using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (Agilent 6890GC/5973MSD; Agilent Technologies Japan, Tokyo, Japan) in the electron impact ionization mode [<xref rid="r31" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>]. Benzylated PFOA was separated on a DB-5MS column (30 m length, 0.25 mm inner diameter, 1 µm film thickness) with helium carrier gas. Splitless injections (2 µL) were performed with the injector set at 220 °C, and the split was opened after 1.5 min. The initial oven temperature was 70 °C for 1.5 min, increased by 15 °C min<sup>−1</sup> to 100 °C, and then increased by 40 °C min<sup>−1</sup> to 240 °C. The ion fragment (m/z 504, [M]<sup>+</sup>) was monitored and used as quantification ions. The detection limit was 2 ng/g of sample. The mean recovery rate of <sup>13</sup>C<sub>2</sub> PFOA from samples was 93.1% ± 8.3%.</p></sec><sec id="sec11" disp-level="2"><title>Microarray analysis of gene expression</title><p>Kidneys were collected from <italic>Abcb4</italic> null mice and wild-type mice (3 mice aged 8–10 weeks in each group). Approximately 30 mg of kidney was diced on ice, and then total RNA was extracted using an RNeasy Lipid Tissue Mini Kit (Qiagen, Tokyo, Japan). The concentration and purity of total RNA were measured using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies Japan), respectively [<xref rid="r32" ref-type="bibr">32</xref>]. Extracted RNA was stored at −80 °C until analysis. Cyanine-labeled cDNA was synthesized from the total RNA samples and hybridized to oligo DNA microarray slides (Whole Mouse Genome 4x44k; Agilent Technologies Japan). The slides were scanned with an Agilent GeneArray Scanner (Agilent Technologies Japan). Information from probe features was obtained using Feature Extraction software version 9.5 (Agilent Technologies Japan). Raw and processed data and the experimental design have been submitted to the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus according to the MIAME code, and are available at <ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/" ext-link-type="uri">http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/</ext-link> (GEO accession number: <ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE30084" ext-link-type="uri">GSE30084</ext-link>).</p></sec><sec id="sec12" disp-level="2"><title>Sequence homology analysis</title><p>The following protein sequences were analyzed: human ABCB1 (NCBI: <ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/NP_000918.2" ext-link-type="uri">NP_000918.2</ext-link>), rhesus monkey ABCB1 (<ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/AAN07779.1" ext-link-type="uri">AAN07779.1</ext-link>), rat Abcb1b (<ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/NP_036755.3" ext-link-type="uri">NP_036755.3</ext-link>), and mouse Abcb1b (<ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/NP_035205.1" ext-link-type="uri">NP_035205.1</ext-link>). Identities of sequences were evaluated using BLAST (ver. 2.14.0) (<ext-link xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" xlink:href="https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/" ext-link-type="uri">https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/</ext-link>). Sequence alignment was performed using ClustalW (ver. 2.1) [<xref rid="r33" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>] and Jalview (ver. 2.11.2.0) [<xref rid="r34" ref-type="bibr">34</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="sec13" disp-level="2"><title>Statistical analysis</title><p>Three to five animals were used in each group. All results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the unpaired Student <italic>t</italic>-test. A probability of &lt;0.05 was considered statistically significant. The analyses were performed using JMP Pro Statistical Software, version 16 (SAS institute, Cary, NC, USA).</p></sec></sec><sec id="sec14" disp-level="1"><title>Results and discussion</title><sec id="sec15" disp-level="2"><title>Kinetics of PFOA uptake and excretion in wild-type and <italic>Abcb4</italic> null mice</title><p>There was no significant difference in plasma PFOA concentrations between wild-type and <italic>Abcb4</italic> null mice during 72 h after PFOA administration (Fig. <xref rid="fig01" ref-type="fig">1</xref>A). PFOA concentrations in plasma did not significantly change from 2 to 72 h (Fig. <xref rid="fig01" ref-type="fig">1</xref>A). Therefore, for subsequent analyses, we used plasma PFOA concentrations at 72 h.</p><fig id="fig01" position="float"><?disp-level 3?><label>Fig. 1</label><caption><p>Excretion and distribution of perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) in wild-type and <italic>Abcb4</italic> null mice.</p><p>(A) Plasma concentrations of PFOA in mice at 2, 24, and 72 h after intravenous administration. (B) PFOA concentrations in bile at 72 h. (C) Cumulative CL<sub>r</sub> of PFOA (mL/24–72 h) at 24, 48, and 72 h after intravenous administration. (D) PFOA concentrations in the kidney at 72 h. Filled bars and triangles indicate wild-type (WT) mice and open ones <italic>Abcb4</italic> null (Abcb4KO) mice. Results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. This finding suggests that species differences in the <italic>ABCB1</italic> sequence could be associated with substrate affinity. Therefore, we evaluated the sequence conservation of ABCB1 between these species. Mice and rats have two <italic>Abcb1</italic> paralogs <italic>Abcb1a</italic> and <italic>Abcb1b</italic>, while monkeys and humans do not have two paralog genes. In this study, the sequence of mouse/rat <italic>Abcb1b</italic> and human/monkey <italic>ABCB1</italic> were analyzed because we speculated that <italic>Abcb1b</italic> contributes to renal secretion of PFOA in mice.</p><p>Protein sequence identities of monkey ABCB1, rat ABCB1b, and mouse ABCB1b were 96%, 81% and 81%, respectively, compared with human ABCB1 (Table <xref rid="sm01" ref-type="sec">S3</xref>). We searched amino acids that are conserved between the monkey, rat, and mouse, but not in humans, by sequence homology analysis. Nine amino acids (E24, V635, A759, F851, I1003, M1027, G1038, R1103, and K1168) were identified in human ABCB1 (Table <xref rid="sm01" ref-type="sec">S4</xref> and Fig. <xref rid="fig05" ref-type="fig">5</xref>A). As a result, among the nine non-conserved amino acids, A759 and F851 are present in the transmembrane domain, and G1038, R1103, and K1168 are present in the ATP-binding domain (Fig. <xref rid="fig05" ref-type="fig">5</xref>B). The lack of conservation of these amino acids may affect human ABCB1 function. ABCB1 has a wide range recognition of substrates and it differs among species [<xref rid="r38" ref-type="bibr">38</xref>]. These non-conserved amino acids might reduce the PFOA-secreting function of human ABCB1 and <italic>in vitro</italic> site-directed mutagenesis experiments will elucidate the mechanisms in substrate recognition.</p><fig id="fig05" position="float"><?disp-level 3?><label>Fig. 5</label><caption><p>Non-conserved amino acids in human ABCB1 compared with monkey ABCB1 and rat/mouse Abcb1b.</p><p>(A) Sequence alignment among four species (human, monkey, rat and mouse). Intriguingly, we found that <italic>Abcb1b</italic> transported PFOA into urine in mice. The contribution of <italic>Abcb1b</italic> to the renal excretion of PFOA under physiological conditions was estimated to be approximately 30%. ABCB1b is localized at the apical membrane in renal tubules where it transports various drugs to the lumen [<xref rid="r39" ref-type="bibr">39</xref>]. To the best of our knowledge, <italic>Abcb1b</italic> is the first transporter of PFOA that has been located to the apical membrane.</p><p>The biological half-life of PFOA in humans (3.8 years) [<xref rid="r18" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>] can be explained by a low excretion rate in the kidney [<xref rid="r22" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>] and enterohepatic circulation of PFOA [<xref rid="r23" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>, <xref rid="r24" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>]. The low urinary excretion rate may be explained by the lower substrate specificity of human ABCB1 to PFOA than that in other species. In contrast, the excretion rate of PFOA into bile is 30- to 60-fold larger than the urinary excretion rate [<xref rid="r23" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>, <xref rid="r24" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>]. Therefore, enhancing fecal elimination by decreasing the reabsorption rate in the enterohepatic circulation of PFOA may shorten the biological half-life in humans. Several drugs have been developed to enhance the elimination of bile into feces [<xref rid="r40" ref-type="bibr">40</xref>, <xref rid="r41" ref-type="bibr">41</xref>], providing a pharmacological approach to enhance the elimination of PFOA and other PFASs from the body. In fact, cholestyramine, bile acid sequestrant resin, is known to enhance fecal excretion of PFAS through increase of bile acid in feces [<xref rid="r42" ref-type="bibr">42</xref>].</p></sec><sec id="sec21" disp-level="2"><title>Limitations in this study</title><p>Renal clearance of PFOA in <italic>Abcb1a/b</italic>-null mice was decreased compared with WT mice. Ablations of <italic>Abcb1a/b</italic> genes may cause off-target effects in gene expression in kidney as observed in <italic>Abcb4</italic> ablation while there have been no reports on significant compensation effects of <italic>Abcb1a/b</italic> deletion. If the potentially induced off-target gene may affect the renal excretion change more than 30%, contribution of ABCB1 may decline. Confirmation of gene expression in the tissue in mice and <italic>in vitro</italic> experiments of transporting activity of ABCB1 for PFOA will support this study results.</p><p>As we investigated before, solute carrier transporters are involved in PFOA kinetics [<xref rid="r25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>]. Further, reported biological half-lives of PFOA significantly differ from humans [<xref rid="r11" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>]. Protein bindings of PFOA to serum proteins may further modify the kinetics. The relative contribution to ABC transporters should be evaluated in experimental animals and humans in future.</p></sec></sec><sec id="sec22" disp-level="1"><title>Conclusion</title><p>This study shows that ABCB4 is one of the major transporters of PFOA in the bile; and ABCB1 is one of the major transporters of PFOA in kidney in the mouse. The human ABCB1 system may not have substrate affinity to PFOA as Abcb1a/1b does in the rodent, which could explain the much lower renal clearance of PFOA in humans than in rodents. In contrast, the human <italic>ABCB4</italic> system, the synteny of mouse <italic>Abcb4</italic> in rodents, is involved in excretion into bile in humans and rodents [<xref rid="r24" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>]. Further studies on transporters are required to establish not only rational risk assessment of PFAS, but also to develop pharmacological interventions to enhance the elimination of PFASs from the body.</p></sec><sec id="sec23" disp-level="1"><title>Abbreviations</title><def-list><def-item><term>ABC</term><def><p>ATP-binding cassette</p></def></def-item><def-item><term>PCR</term><def><p>polymerase chain reaction</p></def></def-item><def-item><term>PFOA</term><def><p>Perfluorooctanoic acid</p></def></def-item><def-item><term>PFASs</term><def><p>Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances</p></def></def-item><def-item><term>PLCO trial</term><def><p>Prostate, Liver, Colorectal, Ovarian (PLCO) trial</p></def></def-item></def-list></sec><sec id="sm01" disp-level="1"><title>Supplementary information</title><supplementary-material id="sm01_1" position="float"><media xmlns:xlink="http://www.w3.org/1999/xlink" id="d66e894" xlink:href="ehpm-29-021-s001.pdf" mimetype="application" mime-subtype="pdf"><?cloudpmc-path d945/10965415/9049f767d537/ehpm-29-021-s001.pdf?><?cloudpmc-bucket app?><?size 1966207?><caption><p><bold>Additional file 1: Supplemental description of methods.</bold>
