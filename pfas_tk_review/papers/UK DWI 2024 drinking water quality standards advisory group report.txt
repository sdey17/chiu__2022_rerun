Executive summary
The function of the Drinking Water Inspectorate (the Inspectorate) is to protect public
health and maintain public confidence in drinking water in England and Wales. The
Inspectorate holds water companies to account to ensure they supply safe and clean
drinking water, now and for future generations.
The Chief Inspector of Drinking Water has an advisory duty to the Secretary of State under
Section 86(2)(a)(ii) of the Water Industry Act 1991. In his letters to the Parliamentary
Under Secretary of State for the Environment and the Minister for Climate Change, the
Chief Inspector recommended a review of the list of the water quality regulations to
address emerging risks and other contaminants. Over time, additional risks to drinking
water quality have emerged due to the use of industrial chemicals and plastics, and the
impact of climate change. Legacy issues, such as lead in older properties, remain.
As part of the European Union (EU) Exit project a governance gap in drinking water policy
and legislation was identified. Policy options were drawn up for consideration within Defra
and the Inspectorate. Drinking water policy is devolved, and Welsh government officials
were involved in the development of this process. An appropriate mechanism for
reviewing drinking water quality standards, to ensure standards remain relevant and
protective of public health, was identified, to support the Chief Inspector in his advisory
duty to the Secretary of State.
The Inspectorate convened a group of experts to advise on drinking water standards. The
expert advisory group considered the scientific evidence, including toxicology and
potential occurrence in drinking water supplies, of a defined list of 15 parameters, which
included lead and per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS). The review considered the
addition, removal, tightening and relaxation of parameters to protect public health.
International regulatory approaches were considered when evaluating the most
appropriate options.
This report summarises the advisory group’s findings and recommendations together with
the options considered and evidence base. The findings are being submitted to the Chief
Inspector to consider and to make recommendations to the relevant ministers for
legislative updates. Although the recommendations are based in the interest of public
health, adopting certain recommendations, such as lead, microcystin-LR and PFAS, may
support with or be supported by wider efforts to achieve the Secretary of State’s key
priorities to ‘clean up our rivers, lakes and seas’.
There is a need to periodically update the list of regulatory parameters to protect the
public from new and emerging contaminants, or to reflect scientific advancements. This
report is therefore considered to be the first phase of work, with additional parameters to
be considered in subsequent reviews. It is recommended that a requirement is introduced
into legislation to mandate the regular periodic review of drinking water standards in
England and Wales for the ongoing protection of public health. This would provide
equivalence to Article 20 of the EU Drinking Water Directive, and ensure the standards are
appropriate, and promote public confidence in drinking water.

Background to the Regulations
Drinking water quality regulation is based upon recommendations provided by the World
Health Organisation (WHO). Through the Guidelines for Drinking Water Quality, the WHO
provide a set of international guideline values which should not be exceeded in drinking
water. The Guidelines are kept up to date through rolling editions based on expert reviews
of key issues, with the most recent edition published in 2022. The WHO recommend that
the Guidelines are adapted to ensure applicability to the local setting, and countries are
advised to adopt more stringent health-based standards where appropriate and
achievable.
Drinking water quality legislation in England and Wales was transposed from the EU
Drinking Water Directive 98/83/EC (the Directive). Monitoring requirements are laid out in
the following regulations (henceforth referred to as the Regulations):
   •   Water Supply (Water Quality) Regulations 2016 (as amended);
   •   Water Supply (Water Quality) Regulations Wales 2018;
   •   Private Water Supplies (England) Regulations 2016 and;
   •   Private Water Supplies (Wales) Regulations 2017.
These rigorous monitoring standards include a wide range of parameters, including
microbiological organisms, chemical parameters, pesticides and aesthetic quality
indicators of taste and odour. Since transposition of the Directive, the monitoring
requirements have remained largely unchanged. Over recent years, scientific
understanding around the toxicology of some existing parameters listed in the Regulations,
and new parameters not included in the Regulations has evolved. Therefore, a review of
certain parameters is required.
In 2017, the WHO regional office for Europe published a report to the European
Commission in advance of a revision to the Drinking Water Directive. This report provided
recommendations for regulatory revisions to improve the level of health protection from
drinking water.
Following EU Exit, a mechanism to drive ongoing revisions of the Regulations is required to
ensure the continuous provision of world-leading drinking water quality to protect
consumers and maintain public confidence in drinking water. Since EU Exit, a recast
Drinking Water Directive (the recast Directive) has come into force. The Inspectorate were
key contributors to the recast Directive prior to EU Exit. There have been no amendments
to the Regulations since EU Exit or the publication of the recast Directive.
Under Better Regulation, amendments to each Statutory Instrument will require an
associated regulatory impact assessment. These will be carried out by Defra and Welsh
Government for each Statutory Instrument.

Methodology and scope
Assembly of the advisory group
The advisory group was appointed during 2023 through open competition. The
recruitment process followed Defra rules on terms of appointment, declaration of conflicts
of interest, and remuneration. Advisory group members were selected based on their
expertise and background to ensure a representative breadth of experience and
knowledge on subjects including toxicology, water treatment, water supply management
and small water supplies. The experts represented a breadth of perspectives including
from research and academia, consultancy, water industry, regulatory and international
governance.
The UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) was represented on the group of experts.
The Inspectorate provided a chair and secretariat function for the advisory group and
participated in the discussions.


Advisory group scope of work
The advisory group was asked to consider statutory limits for specific parameters in
drinking water, to protect public health and maintain public confidence in drinking water.
For this reason, the recommendations are applicable to both public and private water
supplies, although the advisory group acknowledged the additional challenges and support
required to achieve compliance in private water supplies. For both public and private
water supplies, inclusion of a standard in the Regulations provides a basis for funding and
risk assessment by water companies or local authorities respectively.
In its report to the European Commission in 2017, the WHO included several other
recommended amendments to the Directive. These included aspects such as strengthening
regulatory risk assessment and introducing legionella control in buildings. These were out
of scope of the review of the advisory group’s work.


Identification of parameters
The parameters selected for review were identified from a gap analysis of the Regulations
and the recommendations included in the report published by the WHO in 2017 to the
European Commission. The standards listed in the report as for consideration are provided
in Table 1.

Four of the parameters listed in Table 1, to consider removal, were excluded from the

   • Benzene was listed as one of the top ten hazards in the risk assessment risk index

   • Cyanide can be a by-product of chloramination. Following a compliance breach, the

   • The risk of contamination from polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons continues to be
     monitored by the Inspectorate due to the large number of events reported each

In addition to the standards listed in Table 1, bisphenol A and microplastics were included
in the review. In its report to the European Commission, the WHO stated that should
countries wish to provide reassurance to consumers regarding endocrine disruption
chemicals in drinking water, precautionary, arbitrary benchmark values could be
considered. Bisphenol A has a high political and media profile and was therefore included
in the review. Microplastics were added to the scope of the review due to its significant
media attention and wider concern from the Welsh emerging threats group. This was
deemed an appropriate opportunity to review the risks relating to microplastics in drinking
water.


Decision making process
For each parameter, the Inspectorate collated evidence from several sources and compiled
into individual parameter reports. The evidence included toxicology data, WHO
publications including WHO guideline values where available, international standards,
aggregate water company and private water supply data, research published as part of the


                                                                                             9 of 87
Inspectorate's research programme, treatment options, analytical capabilities, and
deliverability. The Committee on Toxicity of Chemicals in Food, Consumer Products and
the Environment (COT) is the UK’s independent scientific committee which provide advice
to government departments on matters concerning the toxicity of chemicals. Publications
by the COT provided a key source of toxicity information. Data held by the Environment
Agency and Natural Resources Wales, on prevalence of contaminants in the environment,
were reviewed to assess prevalence of risk to drinking water sources.
Individual parameter reports were disseminated to the advisory group. Each advisory
group member provided individual feedback and an initial opinion on their recommended
option(s) in advance of a meeting to discuss each parameter. The Inspectorate collated
and circulated the feedback prior to each meeting. Five meetings were held to discuss the
feedback and to assess the most appropriate options to recommend to the Chief
Inspector. Further information was circulated if required. For most parameters, consensus
was met by the advisory group, but any divergences of opinions are outlined in the report.
The following decision criteria were considered to assess the inclusion, removal, relaxation
or tightening of each parameter:
   • Is there sufficient and robust supporting toxicology information to demonstrate a
     risk to public health or aesthetic water quality?
   • Does this recommendation support public confidence in drinking water?
   • Is there evidence of occurrence in public or private water supplies?
   • Have there been any regulatory interventions, such as notifiable events or
     enforcement action taken by the Inspectorate, associated with the parameter?
   • Is this recommendation in alignment with or more stringent than WHO guidelines?
   • Does this recommendation provide equivalent protection to other international
     regulators?
   • Is this recommendation deliverable?
This final report was prepared by the Inspectorate. The advisory group were invited to
submit comments and peer review the report.

Supporting evidence and option evaluation
1,2-dichloroethane
CAS registration number 107-06-2
Consideration: Remove the existing standard.
Recommendation: Retain the current standard of 3 µg/L.
Justification: There is a demonstrated risk of occurrence of 1,2-dichloroethane in private
water supplies, and an unquantified environmental risk, which could impact public
supplies.


Existing regulatory position and international standards
The Regulations state a prescribed concentration for 1,2-dichloroethane of 3 µg/L. In 2017,
the WHO recommended in its report to the European Commission, that Member States
could consider removing this standard from regulations due to its low risk of occurrence in
drinking water sources. The WHO stated that elevated concentrations in drinking water
tend to be caused by acute pollution incidents from localised sources and that current
groundwater management strategies should ensure that levels in drinking water are not of
concern to public health. The toxicity data remain unchanged. The European Commission
and Scotland retained the existing standard of 3 µg/L (Table 3).

WHO guidance and toxicity derivations
The WHO introduced a guideline value for 1,2-dichloroethane of 30 µg/L in 2003 (WHO,
2003a). This guideline value is based on a cancer, haemangiosarcomas, observed in rats.
As a non-threshold chemical, there is no identified limit of ingestion of 1,2-dichloroethane
below which harm does not occur. Therefore, exposure should be as low as reasonably
practicable. For non-threshold chemicals, including 1,2-dichloroethane, the WHO guideline
values were derived using an upper 95% confidence interval value associated with a risk of


                                                                                       12 of 87
one additional cancer in a population of 100,000 (WHO, 2022). This is based on a
consumption of two litres of water per day over 70 years. The current regulatory standard
of 3 µg/L was derived by the European Commission which used a precautionary risk of one
additional cancer in a population of 1,000,000 resulting in a standard which is tenfold
lower than the WHO guideline value.


Evidence for risk to health and consumer acceptability
1,2-dichloroethane is classified as possibly carcinogenic to humans (Group 2B) and listed as
a substance of very high concern (HSE, n.d.; IARC, 1999). Effects of acute poisoning from
1,2-dichloroethane include headaches, dizziness, sensitive skin, nausea, vomiting,
constricting cardiac pain, abdominal pain, diarrhoea, unconsciousness and adverse
neurological effects (UKWIR/WRc, 2023). Exposure to 1,2-dichloroethane in laboratory
animal tests has shown to affect the liver, kidney and immune system and to be potentially
mutagenic (Health Canada, 2015; WHO, 2003a).
1,2-dichloroethane can lead to a ‘pleasant’, ‘chloroform’ odour at a threshold of 7000 µg/L
and can create a ‘sweet’ taste in drinking water, threshold not stated (UKWIR/WRc, 2023;
WHO, 2003a).


Sources and pathways to drinking water
1,2-dichloroethane is an industrial chemical used in the manufacture of vinyl chloride. It is
a constituent component in items such as paints, varnishes, metal degreasers and
cosmetics (UKWIR/WRc, 2023; WHO Europe, 2017). It can enter the environment and
drinking water sources through discharges of industrial effluents or waste disposal. Surface
waters, especially near industrialised areas, and groundwaters near waste disposal sites
are at highest risk of contamination from 1,2-dichloroethane (WHO, 2003a). The risk of
occurrence of 1,2-dichloroethane in drinking water is very low (WHO Europe, 2017). The
rare exceedances which do occur are generally acute and localised incidents and therefore
1,2-dichloroethane can be managed effectively on a localised basis (WHO Europe, 2017).


Occurrence in drinking water in England and Wales
Public water supplies (compliance and risk data): Since 2009, water companies have
achieved 100% compliance with the current standard. All samples submitted to the
Inspectorate since 2009 were below 1.5 µg/L and 99.8% were below the limit of detection.
The Inspectorate holds a large volume of risk assessment data, assessed by water
companies, regarding 1,2-dichloroethane. Many of the risks are assessed as controlled, but
equally many control measures require validation or are under investigation.
Public water supplies (source water data): Over 77,000 source water samples have been
submitted to the Inspectorate since 2009. Of these samples, 99% were below the limit of
detection.
Private water supplies: Since 2013, local authorities have submitted over 12,000 treated
water samples for 1,2-dichloroethane to the Inspectorate. Of these samples, 95% were
below the limit of detection. Three samples were reported with concentrations of 3 µg/L,

                                                                                    13 of 87
from across two supplies. In 2021, there were two breaches of the standard (47.33 µg/L
and 3.77 µg/L) from two different supplies.
Environmental monitoring data: Environmental monitoring for 1,2-dichloroethane is
included as part of the Environmental Quality Standards Directive, the Emissions Inventory
and the Water Framework Directive. Monitoring is being conducted increasingly using a
risk-based approach. A review of environmental water quality data held by the
Environment Agency since 2014 indicate that concentrations of 1,2-dichloroethane in the
environment are generally low (EA, 2024). However, concentrations up to 69.7 µg/L have
been recorded from trade and wastewater effluents and one result of 498,000 µg/L was
recorded from a river/running surface water during a pollution incident in 2017. Data held
by Natural Resources Wales indicate that concentrations in the environment are generally
below the limit of detection with all detections less than 1.5 µg/L (NRW, 2024).


Treatment options and control
1,2-dichloroethane can be effectively treated and removed from drinking water using
granular activated carbon, which can achieve concentrations as low as 0.1 µg/L, or by
aeration (UKWIR/WRc, 2023; WHO, 2003a; WHO, 2022). These treatment processes are
widely used by water companies in England and Wales but are not so widely used in
private water supplies.


Analytical capabilities
The Standing Committee of Analysts published a method (number 265) for the analysis of
volatile organic compounds, including 1,2-dichloroethane, in 2019. Analysis of 1,2-
dichloroethane is generally performed by headspace gas chromatography/mass
spectrometry (GC-MS) in conjunction with a suite of volatile organic compounds, including
benzene and often trihalomethanes. As an existing compliance parameter, laboratories
hold accreditation for sampling and analysis of 1,2-dichloroethane.


Other options considered
Remove the standard for 1,2-dichloroethane: The WHO recommend that, based on its
physico-chemical characteristics and likely low occurrence in drinking water, Member
States could consider the removal of 1,2-dichloroethane from regulations (WHO Europe,
2017). The WHO promote risk-based monitoring to focus resources on highest risks to
drinking water quality. Although compliance with the standard in public supplies is 100%,
this option was discounted due to concerns of a residual risk in the environment,
demonstrated risks to private water supplies and due to the very minimal benefits that
could be redirected from the removal of the standard.
The advisory group noted the importance of removing standards from the Regulations
where there is sufficient evidence of lack of occurrence in drinking water and subsequently
no risk to public health. Developing an accepted process for assessing risks to support the
removal of standards from the Regulations may be helpful in the future.

Antimony
CAS registration number 7440-36-0
Consideration: Relax the existing standard.
Recommendation: Retain the current standard of 5 µg/l subject to advice from the COT.
Justification: The critical study used to derive the health-based guidance value for
antimony is one by Poon et al. (1998). This study determined a no observed adverse effect
level (NOAEL) of 0.06 mg/kg body weight/day based on subtle histopathological changes in
thyroids of male rats. A subsequent publication by Lynch et al. (1999), reassessed the data
presented by Poon et al. (1998). This study considered that the thyroid effects were
adaptive and not adverse, highly variable and did not show a dose response. Moreover,
effects seen were not replicated in a study by the National Toxicology Program (Dieter,
1991). Therefore, Lynch et al. (1999) recommended the use of a less conservative NOAEL
of 6 mg/kg body weight/day based on decreased body weight gain and reduced food and
water intake.
The WHO used the NOAEL derived by Lynch et al. (1999) as the basis of the health-based
guidance value leading to a guideline value of 20 µg/L, while Health Canada have retained
the use of the NOAEL derived in the Poon et al. (1998) study. Using the NOAEL derived by
Poon et al. (1998), Health Canada derived a health-based guidance value of 3 µg/L and
have adopted a maximum acceptable concentration of 6 µg/L due to practical
achievability. Due to the differing interpretations of the Poon et al. (1998) study and
NOAELs used by authoritative organisations, including the WHO, Health Canada and ATSDR
in the USA, the advisory group recommended that advice should be sought from the COT
on the most suitable NOAEL and health-based guidance value to use to derive a regulatory
standard.
The COT have started to consider the evidence to support the advisory group with their
recommendation (COT, 2024a). In their October meeting, the Committee raised several
concerns over the study by Poon et al. (1998), highlighting significant issues with the
pathology assessment (COT, 2024b). Such concerns were in alignment with those noted by
Lynch et al. (1999) and Members considered that Lynch et al. (1999) provided a reasonable
assessment of the interpretation of the study findings by Poon et al (1998). Overall, based
on the data presented, Members were unable to reach a conclusion about the relevant
point of departure and requested additional information. Further discussions are
anticipated in early 2025. Once the COT has provided its advice, the recommendation will
be reviewed and the Chief Inspector updated with the outcome.
Public water supply compliance with the current standard is 100% and since 2010, 99.52%
of samples collected from private water supplies have complied with the current standard.
Antimony could be used as a replacement for lead fittings (WHO, 2003b). Therefore, the
increased drive towards removal of lead fittings from water supplies could be associated
with increased antimony used in fittings. Relaxation of the standard, if safe for public
health, could be a useful enabling step for lead reduction policies relating to lead in water
fittings.

WHO guidance and toxicity derivations
In 2003, the WHO re-evaluated the guideline value for antimony, from 5 µg/L to 20 µg/L, in
response to new scientific evidence that resulted in the use of the less conservative NOAEL
derived by Lynch et al. (1999) and an uncertainty factor of 1,000 (WHO Europe, 2017). The
revised guideline value was based on a NOAEL of 6 mg/kg body weight/day for decreased
body-weight gain and reduced food and water intake. An uncertainty factor of 1000 was
applied to the NOAEL to account for intra- and inter-species variation and the use of a
subchronic study (exposure over a period of 28 to 90 days) to give a tolerable daily intake
(TDI) of 6 µg/kg body weight/day. Allocating 10% of the TDI to drinking water provides a
guideline value of 20 µg/La (WHO, 2003b).
The previous guideline value was calculated using a lowest observed adverse effect level
(LOAEL) of 0.43 mg/kg body weight/day. This was based on decreased longevity, decreased
blood glucose levels and increased cholesterol levels in rats. Applying an uncertainty factor
of 500, a TDI of 0.86 µg/kg body weight/day was calculated (WHO, 1996). Allocating 10% of
the TDI to drinking water provided a guideline value of 3 µg/L. As this value was below the
practical limit of analysis, a guideline value of 5 µg/L was adopted (WHO, 1996).

Evidence for risk to health and consumer acceptability
The health risks associated with antimony ingestion vary depending on its oxidation state
(WHO, 2003b; WHO Europe, 2017). The more toxic form, antimony trioxide (III), is
classified as probably carcinogenic to humans (Group 2A), however this is only if inhaled
not ingested (IARC, n.d.; IARC, 2023a; WHO, 2003b; WHO Europe, 2017). Antimony (V) is
more commonly found in drinking water (WHO Europe, 2017).
The oxidation states generally detected in the environment, antimony (III) and antimony
(V), are generally odourless and tasteless (UKWIR/WRc, 2021a).


Sources and pathways to drinking water
Antimony can occur naturally in the environment although occurrence in source waters
used for drinking water is unlikely to be at significant concentrations unless risks relating to
acid mine drainage are identified in the catchment (UKWIR/WRc, 2021a). The most
common source of antimony in drinking water is through the dissolution of metals from
fittings used in domestic plumbing (WHO, 2003b; WHO Europe, 2017). Trace amounts can
be derived from brass tap fittings and solders. Antimony forms very hard alloys with
copper, lead and tin, and may be considered a replacement for lead solder (WHO, 2003b).

Treatment options and control
Antimony can be removed from drinking water through coagulation, reverse osmosis,
adsorption and ion exchange (Li et al, 2018; UKWIR/WRc, 2021a). Coagulation, adsorption
and ion exchange are widely used by water companies in England and Wales but are not so
widely used in private water supply systems. Removal efficiency of antimony through
coagulation is dependent on the coagulant used, its dose, the pH of the water and the
oxidation state, with removal most effective using ferric chloride as a coagulant
(UKWIR/WRc, 2021a). Data provided to the Inspectorate by water companies show a
reduction in antimony concentrations between source waters and the point of compliance
(consumer taps) indicating that conventional treatment is effective at reducing antimony
concentrations. As the wider concern is in distribution and plumbing systems, control of
antimony through product specification is more appropriate (WHO Europe, 2017).


Analytical capabilities
The Standing Committee of Analysts published a method (number 262) for the analysis of
metals, including antimony, in 2018. Analysis of antimony is performed by inductively
coupled plasma spectrometry (ICP-MS), as per ISO standard 17294-2:2023. Water
company data provided to the Inspectorate indicate a limit of quantification of 0.01 µg/L.
As an existing compliance parameter, laboratories hold accreditation for sampling and
analysis of antimony.

WHO guidance and toxicity derivations
In 2009, the WHO relaxed the provisional guideline value for boron from 0.5 mg/L to 2.4
mg/L (WHO, 2022). This was to reflect a considerable body of research, from the UK and
USA, which found that intake from sources other than drinking water is low, allowing the
allocation attributed to drinking water to be significantly increased (WHO, 2009). The
revised guideline value of 2.4 mg/L was calculated using a lower 5% confidence limit of the
benchmark dose (BMDL05) of 10.3 mg/kg body weight/day, based on decreased foetal
body weight in rats in two studies combined (Price et al., 1994, 1996 and Heindel et al.,
1992), and an uncertainty factor of 60 to give a TDI of 0.17 mg/kg body weight/day
(rounded to 0.2 mg/kg body weight/day)e (WHO, 2009). The BMDL05 was similar to the
NOAEL of 9.6 mg/kg body weight/day, based on decreased foetal body weight in rats in the
studies by Price et al., 1994, 1996. The BMDL05 was selected as it was derived from similar
studies carried out in the same laboratory, and all dose response data were used in the
derivation of the BMDL05, increasing confidence in the estimation. Allocating 40% of the
TDI to drinking water gives the guideline value of 2.4 mg/l. The previous boron guideline
value allocated 10% of the TDI to drinking water (WHO, 2003c). For young children, the
WHO assumes a body weight of 10 kg and a consumption of 1 litre of drinking water per
day which can be used to calculate a guideline value of 0.8 mg/L, rounded to 1.0 mg/L.
The WHO raised concerns that a standard of 1.0 mg/L is excessively cautious and may be a
barrier to introducing desalination in locations with limited choices for new sources of
drinking water (WHO Europe, 2017).

Evidence for risk to health and consumer acceptability
Boron is of relatively low toxicity to humans. There are no data that show adverse health
effects in humans occur following environmental exposure from boron (WHO Europe,
2017). Boron is found in food and is an essential element required by many plants (WHO
Europe, 2017). Symptoms of boron poisoning include vomiting, abdominal pain and
diarrhoea however single acute ingestion is unlikely to cause serious poisoning
(UKWIR/WRc, 2020b). Infants who consume high concentrations of boron, approximately
12-120 grams of borax, for intermediate durations of four to 12 weeks can exhibit ‘seizure
disorders’ (UKWIR/WRc, 2020b). In laboratory animals, there is evidence that boron can
impact male reproduction, decrease foetal body weight, decrease haemoglobin levels,
increase body weight and increase brain and thyroid weights (UKWIR/WRc, 2020b; WHO
Europe, 2017).
Boron and boric acid are odourless and there are no reported taste thresholds
(UKWIR/WRc, 2020b).


Sources and pathways to drinking water
Boron is a naturally occurring element in groundwater. It is widespread in the environment
at low concentrations and in higher concentrations in areas with boron-rich rocks
(UKWIR/WRc, 2020b; WHO Europe, 2017). Boron can enter drinking water catchments
from industrial processes include glass manufacture, soaps and detergents, flame
retardants, neutron absorbers for nuclear installations, cosmetics, pharmaceuticals,
pesticides and agricultural fertilisers (UKWIR/WRc, 2020b). Previously boron was found in
surface waters and treated wastewater effluents from detergents, however this is no
longer considered an issue in the environment (WHO Europe, 2017).
Seawater contains high concentrations of boron. Desalination technologies alone, such as
reverse osmosis, do not provide significant removal however many sites include boron
removal resins and have operating practices to reduce or remove boron concentrations.
Increased consideration of seawater, brackish waters or water reuse schemes as drinking
water sources are anticipated in the future to meet demand (Southern Water, 2024; WHO
Europe, 2017).


Occurrence in drinking water in England and Wales
In England and Wales, only Thames Water and South West and Bournemouth Water (Isles
of Scilly) operate desalination plants. To date, all operations have provided treated
drinking water compliant with the boron standard. Water supplying these works abstract
from brackish surface waters or coastal boreholes however and are therefore expected to
have lower boron concentrations than waters abstracted directly from the sea.
Approximately 40% of drinking water supplied on the Isles of Scilly is from desalinated
seawater abstracted from coastal boreholes (South West Water, 2022), where source

Blending with low-concentration boron supplies is the most economical method to reduce

reverse osmosis is required or single pass reverse osmosis with appropriate treatment
such as boron removal resins. This is associated with high capital and significant
operational costs and is a very energy intensive process bearing a high carbon footprint
(Jersey Water, 2024; WHO Europe, 2017).

Due to the small scale of the distribution networks and fewer sources, blending is more
challenging to achieve in private water supplies. Reverse osmosis is installed at few water
treatment works.

Sources and pathways to drinking water
Chlorate is formed during decomposition of sodium hypochlorite solutions and is a
breakdown product of chlorine dioxide. These are chemicals used widely in water
treatment as oxidants and disinfectants (Cranfield University, 2019; WHO Europe, 2017):
Sodium hypochlorite: Chlorate is a degradation product from sodium or calcium
hypochlorite. Decomposition of solid calcium hypochlorite is much slower than sodium
hypochlorite and therefore chlorate contamination is less likely to be significant where
calcium hypochlorite is used (WHO, 2016). The rate of decomposition, and subsequent
increase in chlorate concentration, increases with the age, concentration and temperature
of the hypochlorite solution (BSI, 2013; WHO Europe, 2017). As the available chlorine in
the hypochlorite decreases, more product is needed to achieve the desired concentration
for disinfection, which leads to an increase in the amount of chlorate added to the water.
Onsite electrolytic chlorination (OSEC): Hypochlorite solutions used for OSEC generation
are weak (1-2% sodium hypochlorite) and therefore do not decay significantly over short
periods of time. Estimated compliance with a chlorate standard of 0.25 mg/L is
approximately 97.15% indicating that chlorate generation is still of concern in supplies
where OSEC is used (Cranfield University, 2019).
Chlorine dioxide: Sodium chlorate is used in the preparation of chlorine dioxide and
therefore chlorate can be a byproduct of chlorine dioxide disinfection. If chlorine dioxide is
dosed prior to ozone treatment the chlorite formed from the chlorine dioxide is converted
to chlorate and can occur at concentrations approaching and exceeding the guideline value
(WHO Europe, 2017). Although the use of chlorine dioxide for disinfection of drinking
water is much less widespread than sodium hypochlorite, use across Europe is increasing
especially for legionella control in buildings (WHO Europe, 2017).
Sodium chlorate was used as a pesticide until it was banned by the EU in 2009. Old stocks
may still be in use and therefore some sources may enter drinking water from the
environment (PHE, 2019).


Occurrence in drinking water in England and Wales
The use of sodium hypochlorite and OSEC in drinking water treatment has increased,

To date, the considerable body of research regarding the potential public health risks
associated with disinfection by-products has been focussed on trihalomethanes. The
toxicity of haloacetic acids is less well understood than trihalomethanes, and the volume
of evidence regarding associated health risks posed by individual haloacetic acids varies
with each substance.
Some haloacetic acids are known to create odour issues such as dibromoacetic acid which
can cause a pungent odour at a threshold of 230 µg/L (UKWIR/WRc, 2019b).


Sources and pathways to drinking water
Haloacetic acids are a group of disinfection by-products generated when chlorine reacts
with natural organic matter and/or bromine in source waters (WHO Europe, 2017; WHO,
2022). There are nine brominated and/or chlorinated haloacetic acids in total (Cranfield
University, 2011). Precursors for the different haloacetic acids vary depending on the
nature and concentration of the organic material, the chlorine dose and chlorination
conditions, such as pH and chlorine contact time (Cranfield University, 2011; DWI, 2009).
Formation can be seasonal with the highest concentrations seen in the autumn (DWI,
2009; WHO, 2022). Previous research indicated that the formation of haloacetic acids is
lowest in groundwater systems, higher in lowland surface waters and highest in upland
surface waters (DWI, 2009). Subsequent research found that disinfection by-products
associated with lowland water sources have greater propensity to form brominated
haloacetic acids (UKWIR, 2020).
Dichloroacetic acid, trichloroacetic acid and monochloroacetic acid may be present in
catchments due to their previous or current uses as pesticides or herbicides and in
industrial processes (UKWIR/WRc, 2019b; UKWIR/WRc, 2021b; UKWIR/WRc, 2024a).

submission for haloacetic acids since 2022. All risks have been self-assessed by the
companies to indicate that risk mitigation is achieved, verified and maintained. Private

Research commissioned by the Inspectorate: The Inspectorate has commissioned three
research projects to improve understanding of the formation, occurrence and possible

In the second project, a representative survey was carried out of 20 sites across England

(maximum concentration of 28.9 µg/L for HAA9). Concentrations of haloacetic acids in

from brominated haloacetic acids and therefore greater emphasis needs to be placed on
these (UKWIR, 2020). The research recommended monitoring of HAA9, in preference to
HAA5, to provide a more complete picture of the potential hazard.


Treatment options and control
Haloacetic acids form due to the reaction between the compounds naturally present in
source waters and chlorine. Therefore, management is through preventing their formation
by controlling and optimising chlorine doses and removing the precursors of formation.
Natural organic material, pH and bromide ions should be managed in the catchment. Ion
exchange, activated carbon and dense membranes can provide enhanced removal of
organic matter precursors of haloacetic acids. These processes are used widely by water
companies in England and Wales but less widely in private water supply systems. Pre-
treatment processes, such as coagulation and pH control during chlorination, should be
optimised to reduce the risk of haloacetic acid formation (WHO Europe, 2017; WHO,
2022). However, disinfection should not be compromised in an effort to control
disinfection by-products (WHO Europe, 2017).

The health risks associated with ingestion of lead are well documented. Lead can cause
both acute and chronic toxic effects. In the context of drinking water, exposure is likely to
be relatively low but over longer periods of time, and therefore chronic exposure is of
greater concern than acute exposure.

can cause cardiovascular disease (measured by hypertension), chronic renal disease

in which residents where exposed to elevated levels of lead in drinking water, included
skin rash/irritation, hair loss, muscle aches and behavioural changes such as reduced sleep,
aggressiveness, difficulty concentrating, depression and anxiety/stress (UKWIR/WRc,
2019c).

enforcement of the use of lead-based fittings is limited. As part of the Green Economic
Recovery, two water companies were provided with finances by Ofwat to carry out lead
replacement pilot trials, one of which was part of their smarter healthier homes initiative.
The trials, in Coventry and Bomere Heath, tested different models for conducting the
work, upskilling workers and developing communications strategies with the public. The
unit costs for lead replacements have reduced in Coventry, and it is reasonable to expect
further unit cost reductions as experience grows. The learning from these trials continues
to be shared with the wider industry and regulators. Trials are ongoing to disengage
orthophosphate dosing. Tightening of the lead standard could be a catalyst for further
innovation of lead removal.
As part of the price review 2024 (PR24) process, the Inspectorate set out long-term
planning guidance to water companies. One of the eleven expectations was to reduce the
risk of lead in drinking water (DWI, 2022b). Companies submitted lead reduction strategies
as part of their PR24 business plans. Most water companies set a target for supplies to
contain no lead pipe by 2050, however estimated dates to achieve this by were calculated
to be 100s years, with a maximum completion time of 1,355 years (DWI, 2024b).
Public water supplies (compliance data and risk data): Compliance with the current

Treatment options and control
Lead in source waters can be treated through conventional surface water treatment
processes and activated carbon (UKWIR/WRc, 2019c). In pipework, orthophosphoric acid
forms an effective microscopic barrier between the lead pipe and the passing water to
reduce plumbosolvency. It is considered to successfully lower lead concentrations to 10
µg/L however reducing the concentrations of lead to 5 µg/L will require removal of lead
pipes and fittings from water supply systems.
Conventional surface water treatment processes of activated carbon and dosing of
orthophosphoric acid are used widely by water companies in England and Wales, but less
widely in private water supply systems.


Analytical capabilities
The Standing Committee of Analysts Bluebook published a method (number 262) for the
analysis of metals, including lead, in 2018. Analysis of lead is performed by ICP-MS. As an
existing compliance parameter, water company and commercial laboratories hold
accreditation for sampling and analysis of lead. The data reported to the Inspectorate from
water companies indicate that the current limit of quantifications that water companies
analyse lead to is around 0.02 µg/L. Therefore, existing analytical methods are fit for
purpose and there is no need to further develop existing analytical methods.


Other options considered
Retain the current standard of 10 µg/L: This option was considered by the advisory group
as the WHO recommend that policy is developed in advance of tightening a standard. A
standard in isolation is not sufficiently health protective and the advisory group were keen
to stress that associated policy is required. Retaining the current standard was discounted
as the group advised that as there is no discernible threshold of lead in drinking water, and
tightening the standard would further safeguard consumers against lower concentrations
of lead in drinking water. Tightening the standard would promote a direction of travel to
address the lead issue.
Tighten the standard to a prescribed concentration of 0 µg/L: This standard is not
analytically feasible at present due to restrictions regarding limit of quantification and due


                                                                                      49 of 87
to trace concentrations of lead being present in drinking water. Furthermore, this option
could pose difficulties with explaining risk to consumers where trace concentrations are
identified, causing a negative impact to public confidence in drinking water. The WHO
guideline value for lead is based upon practical achievability and at this stage a standard of
0 µg/L is considered unachievable. The risks from lead should be addressed using a risk-
based approach, with greater risks being addressed first. This standard could drive
attention away from supplies with high concentrations of lead being addressed first.
Increase the sampling frequency for lead: The current compliance sampling frequency for
lead does not provide a representative distribution of the risk, for example eight samples
must be collected from a zone which supplies between 5,000 and 100,000 properties.
There was some support from the advisory group to increase the compliance sampling
frequency for lead and this could be considered as a policy option. Water companies are
conducting significant operational sampling programmes to understand lead risks and
there may be other measures to better understand the risk profile from lead. The locations
of compliance samples collected from consumer taps must therefore be random so as not
to bias risks, as per the Regulations. For lead, targeted monitoring, informed by risk
assessments, may be an effective measure to protect public health.

Microcystin-LR
Consideration: Introduce a new standard.
Recommendation: Develop regulatory guidance on risk reduction of algal management.
The guidance should support water companies with their current algal management
strategies as well as long-term mitigation measures. Guidance should include information
on triggers for investigations and expected actions to take, verification monitoring
requirements, including microcystin-LR, catchment risk assessments, preventative work to
reduce risks from algal blooms and remediation and treatment of algal toxins. The WHO
have recently published information for regulators and water suppliers on the
management of cyanobacteria in drinking water supplies, which could be used to support
with drafting of any regulatory guidance by the Inspectorate (WHO, 2024).
Justification: To address the wider issues and risks associated with algae in drinking water
supplies. Guidance would include the requirement for sampling a range of cyanotoxins,
including microcystin-LR, and trigger points for sampling. Defining an algal bloom is
complex, with additional guidance required to drive an effective response. Therefore,
companies may require further guidance, for example in Wales (WHP, 2022) and Australia
(WQRA, 2010), to effectively address risks from algal blooms. There are significant
geographic and seasonal variations in bloom formation, with different algal toxins present
in different blooms. A microcystin-LR standard would not safeguard against the risks posed
by other cyanotoxins. Analysis of total microcystins is more cumbersome than for
microcystin-LR. The advisory group recommended that a more holistic approach to algal
management is more effective than introducing a standard for microcystin-LR which is not
present in many water bodies.

There is not currently a standard prescribed for microcystin-LR in the Regulations. In 2017,
the WHO recommended in its report to the European Commission, that Member States
should consider introducing a standard with a prescribed concentration of 1 µg/L but that
every effort should be made to minimise the risk of cyanobacterial blooms in source
waters. The European Commission and Scotland introduced a standard to reflect this

WHO guidance and toxicity derivations
The provisional WHO health-based lifetime guideline value for total microcystin is 1 µg/L
(WHO, 2022). This is designated as provisional due to uncertainties in the database and
because it is based on data only for microcystin-LR due to insufficient toxicological data for
other microcystins. The guideline value was derived using a NOAEL of 40 µg/kg body
weight/day, based on slight liver toxicity, and an uncertainty factor of 1000p to calculate a
TDI of 0.04 µg/kg body weight/day (WHO, 2022). The value is intended to be protective of
public health over extended periods but presence in water sources is expected to be short
term (WHO Europe, 2017). The WHO short-term drinking water guideline value, intended
for periods of up to two weeks, is 12 µg/L (WHO, 2020).
The WHO have derived guideline values for cylindrospermopsins (0.7 µg/L, 3 µg/l for short
term exposure) and saxitoxins (3 µg/L for acute exposure) (WHO, 2022). A guideline value
has not been derived for anatoxins due to insufficient data, however a reference value of
30 µg/L is suggested for short term exposure (WHO, 2022).


Evidence for risk to health and consumer acceptability
Microcystins are highly toxic to the liver, some are neurotoxic and microcystin-LR has been
listed as possibly carcinogenic to humans (IARC, n.d.; UKWIR/WRc, 2022; WHO Europe,
2017; WHO, 2022).

Sources and pathways to drinking water
Cyanobacteria are photosynthetic bacteria which occur widely in lakes, reservoirs, ponds
and slow-flowing rivers (WHO, 2022). During certain conditions, such as when nutrient
contents are high and warm weather periods, cyanobacteria can increase substantially in
biomass concentration, known as an algal bloom. These blooms often occur seasonally and
vary geographically. Cyanobacteria produce cyanotoxins which generally occur in low
concentrations unless a bloom is present (WHO Europe, 2017). The four main groups of
cyanotoxin which account for most, although not all, of the toxicity to humans are
anatoxin-a variants, cylindrospermopsins, saxitoxins and microcystins (WHO, 2022).
Microcystins are the best studied and understood of the cyanotoxins. Microcystin-LR
(leucine (L), arginine (R)) is the most frequently occurring and the most toxic microcystin
(UKWIR/WRc, 2022; WHO, 2022). Risks from microcystins are anticipated to increase as
temperatures increase due to climate change and accelerated eutrophication caused by
human activities such as farming and treated wastewater discharges (Fera, 2024).


Occurrence in drinking water in England and Wales
Wider issues relating to algae in source waters have resulted in the Inspectorate issuing
one notice and investigating several taste and odour events (DWI, 2021; DWI, 2023b; DWI,
2023c).
Public water supplies (compliance data): The Inspectorate hold no data from water
companies on microcystins in treated waters.
Public water supplies (source water and risk data): Since 2009, four water companies in
England have submitted data on microcystins in source waters to the Inspectorate. Of the
1490 samples submitted against the parameter code ‘microcystin’ during this period, 96%
(1424) were below the limit of detection. Two water companies have submitted a small
volume of risk assessment data to the Inspectorate for microcystin as part of their risk
assessment submissions. All lines were assessed as risk mitigated.
Private water supplies: Smaller supplies are unlikely to abstract from lakes or slow-moving
surface water bodies however some may abstract from small surface water bodies.
Therefore, the impact on private water supplies is unknown.
Research commissioned by the Inspectorate: The Inspectorate commissioned research to
investigate the occurrence of cyanotoxins in surface waters across England, Scotland and
Wales (Fera, 2024). Sampling carried out by the researchers detected microcystin-LR in
surface waters with concentrations of less than 0.2 µg/L in the winter and 0.5 µg/L in the
summer or autumn. A review of data provided by eleven water companies highlighted two
microcystin-LR detections in surface water bodies from one company that were above the



                                                                                   53 of 87
WHO guideline value of 4.5 µg/L and 16 µg/L. These samples were collected between May
and October. No cyanotoxins were detected in the treated waters.
The research highlighted that there is no single accepted definition for an algal bloom and
attempts to define a bloom are extremely complex. Of the companies surveyed as part of
the research, five out of eleven did not define a bloom. One company used WHO guideline
values for safe recreational waters as a surrogate as to whether a hazardous bloom is likely
(WHO, 2003d) but a baseline of algal conditions of individual water bodies must be
established to understand changes to be identified and monitored. The researchers
concluded that stating sampling should only be carried out in the event of potential
blooms may not be immediately beneficial to water companies when trying to assess
whether monitoring is legally required.


Treatment options and control
The most effective way to manage microcystin-LR is through preventing the formation of
blooms of cyanobacteria through catchment and source water management (WHO
Europe, 2017; WHO, 2022). These can include reducing the nutrient content, changing
reservoir stratification or mixing or adjusting the abstraction depth if blooms cannot be
prevented (WHO Europe, 2017). There may be additional unrecognised toxins within the
blooms and controlling the blooms can also control these (WHO, 2022).
Conventional surface water treatment processes can remove or degrade cyanotoxins
(WHO, 2022). Cyanotoxins can be removed through filtration where cells are intact,
including riverbank filtration, flocculation, granular or powdered activated carbon, or
oxidation using chlorine and ozone (UKWIR/WRc, 2022; WHO Europe, 2017; WHO, 2022).


Analytical capabilities
The Standing Committee of Analysts published a method (number 280) for the
determination of microcystins in 2022. Analysis of microcystin-LR is validated through ISO
standard 22104:2021. This standard is a method which uses liquid chromatography and
tandem mass spectrometry. These methods are capable of detecting microcystins to a
limit of quantification of 0.1 µg/L.


Other options considered
Introduce a standard for microcystin-LR with prescribed concentration 1.0 µg/L: The
WHO consider that routine monitoring for microcystin in drinking water is not the best
way of managing the risks from algal toxins which are only present intermittently during
algal blooms (WHO Europe, 2017). The recent research project commissioned by the
Inspectorate indicates that no microcystin-LR were detected in treated waters and
therefore the expert group raised concerns that introducing a standard would mask wider
issues relating to algal toxins. Introduction of this standard would not safeguard locations
where other cyanotoxins, such as cylindrospermopsins, are of concern. For this reason, the
advisory group recommended that regulatory guidance, to include monitoring of

method of managing the risks posed by algae.

Microplastics
Consideration: Introduce a new standard.
Recommendation: Establish a list of parameters of interest for continued evidence
gathering to ensure regulations are revised within effective timeframes should sufficient
evidence become available which highlights a risk to drinking water quality and public
health. Include microplastics on such a list.
Justification: To reflect WHO recommendation that microplastics need not be monitored
in drinking water at this time. A numerical standard for microplastics would be difficult to
derive due to analytical constraints and insufficient oral toxicological data to assess an
appropriate value. The Inspectorate is not aware of any other regulators having introduced
a standard for microplastics. The need for a numerical standard should be kept under
review as further evidence emerges.


Existing regulatory position
There is not currently a standard prescribed for microplastics in the Regulations. The
Inspectorate is not aware of any countries who have introduced regulatory standards for
microplastics in drinking water. The EU have introduced a ‘watching brief mechanism’ to
enable more dynamic, flexible regulation of emerging risks such a microplastics in drinking
water. The WHO does not currently recommend routine monitoring for microplastics as
there is no evidence to indicate a human health concern and to ensure the most effective
use of resources (WHO, 2019).


WHO guidance and toxicity derivations
The WHO have not established a guideline value for microplastics. There are very limited
data from experimental studies to assess the potential oral toxicity of microplastics, which
prevents firm conclusions on the risk from low level ingestion (Bevan, 2019; Cranfield
University, 2022).


Evidence for risk to health and consumer acceptability
Only particles smaller than 20 µm in size are thought to be able to reach organs following
ingestion with uptake in the gastrointestinal tract being restricted to those less than 150
µm (Bevan, 2019; Cranfield University, 2022). Microplastics in drinking water can pose a
potential risk to health via three mechanisms (WHO, 2019):
o Toxicity of the microplastic particle itself (WHO, 2019). Due to limited studies, many of
  which are of questionable reliability and relevance, firm conclusions regarding toxicity
  cannot be determined yet (WHO, 2019). Particle toxicity may be associated with
  localised inflammation, altered gut microbiome and altered lipid metabolism (Bevan,
  2019).



                                                                                    56 of 87
o Chemicals which have known adverse health effects, such as from unbound monomers,
  additives and sorbed chemicals from the environment, attaching onto microplastics
  (WHO, 2019). This is considered of low concern in terms of risk to human health
  (Bevan, 2019; WHO, 2019),
o Microorganisms attaching to and forming biofilms on microplastics. This is considered
  of low concern in terms of risk to human health (Bevan, 2019).
Microplastics in drinking water attract intermittent media attention which can influence
public confidence in the safety of drinking water.


Sources and pathways to drinking water
Microplastics are generally described as solid-phase, non-degradable, polymeric materials
which are less than 5 mm in diameter and are insoluble in water (UKWIR/WRc, 2018;
WHO, 2019). There is no single, scientifically agreed definition of a microplastic.
Microplastics are ubiquitous in surface waters and in urban areas due to domestic and
industrial pollution sources, and of lower risk in groundwaters which are better protected
from particulate contamination (Cranfield University, 2022; WHO, 2019). They can enter
water courses from surface run-off, wastewater effluents, combined sewer overflows,
industrial effluent, degraded plastics and atmospheric deposition. They are found
predominantly in urban areas where domestic effluents and industrial activities were
suggested as main sources (Cranfield University, 2022). Some microplastics found in
drinking water may come from treatment and distribution systems (WHO, 2019).


Occurrence in drinking water in England and Wales
The Inspectorate holds no sampling data in relation to microplastics in public or private
water supplies. Water companies are not currently risk assessing microplastics as part of
their regulatory risk assessment process. Private water supplies tend to be located in rural
settings and abstract from groundwater sources. Therefore, risks from microplastics in
private water supplies is considered to be lower than for public supplies.
The Inspectorate has commissioned two research projects to understand i) the potential
exposure and adverse health effects from nanoparticles and microplastics (Bevan, 2019)
and ii) the possible removal of microplastics in drinking water treatment (Cranfield
University, 2022). These projects recommended that further and more detailed
microplastics investigations are required but that optimisation of conventional surface
water treatment processes, such as clarification and filtration, should reduce the risk from
microplastics in drinking water.
Several gaps in the research remain, such as the understanding of risks to public health,
and an agreed analytical methodology is yet to be established.




                                                                                    57 of 87
Treatment options and control
Physical removal processes of coagulation, flocculation, sedimentation/flotation and
filtration, when optimised to produce low turbidity waters, are considered highly effective
in removing particles with similar characteristics to microplastics, such as size, density and
surface charge (Cranfield University, 2022; UKWIR/WRc, 2018; WHO, 2019). Research
commissioned by the Inspectorate found that more than 99.99% of microplastic particles
are removed through these conventional treatment processes (UKWIR/DWI, 2019).
Advanced treatment, such as nanofiltration and ultrafiltration, can remove smaller
particles larger than 0.001 µm and 0.01 µm respectively (UKWIR/WRc, 2018; WHO, 2019).
As microplastics can be effectively managed through optimizing water treatment
processes, efforts should be made to focus on ensuring existing processes are operated
effectively and efficiently (WHO, 2019).


Analytical capabilities
Significant further work is required to develop robust analytical methods for microplastics.
Standard methods for the identification, quantification and analysis of microplastics in
foods and biological specimens are not available, making comparison of study findings
difficult (Bevan, 2019).
Samples can be collected using trawl nets across the surface of water, or from solutions,
for later analysis (WHO, 2019). Approaches to determine the chemical composition and/or
particles size are spectroscopic, thermoanalytical, and chemical (WHO, 2019). The ISO
TC147 'Water Quality' committee is in the process of drafting ISO standards for sampling
and analytical techniques (spectroscopy and thermoanalytical) for microplastics. There are
constraints with the size obtained and limit of detections; the smallest particle size
detected is 1 µm in drinking water, with mean concentration values ranging from 10-3 to
103 particles/litre (UKWIR/WRc, 2018; WHO, 2019).


Other options considered
Introduce a numerical standard for microplastics: The limited evidence available that exist
for microplastics in drinking water do not indicate a risk to public health, however further
studies are required. The WHO does not currently recommend routinely sampling for
microplastics in drinking water and the European Commission, Canada, Australia and USA
have not introduced statutory standards. There are gaps in the evidence which prevent the
introduction of a standard and which are necessary to define an appropriate guideline
value.

Consideration: Introduce a new standard.
Recommendation: Introduce a standard with maximum prescribed concentration of 0.1
µg/L for the sum of a defined list of named PFAS. The PFAS included in the sum of
calculation should be based on the risk assessment for the supply, and in line with the
Inspectorate’s guidance. The most recent guidance published by the Inspectorate, in

Significant further work is required to prevent the introduction of PFAS into the
environment so that they can be managed at source and prevented from entering drinking
water supplies at source. This would reduce the need for costly monitoring and reduce the
environmental impact of additional water treatment processes to mitigate risks through
drinking water as well as safeguarding public health from PFAS in drinking water.
Establishing a regulatory review mechanism for new and emerging contaminants to
protect public health is required, equivalent to Article 20 of the recast Drinking Water
Directive.
Justification: The advisory group discussed the sufficiency and further emergence of
evidence and whether there was adequate data to introduce a standard at this time. Due
to the persistence of PFAS in the environment, and the potential for some PFAS to
accumulate in the body and be toxic at low concentrations, the advisory group
recommended to introduce a standard to protect public health. In addition, many
regulators have introduced standards for PFAS and the advisory group raised concerns that
not introducing a standard would pose a risk to public confidence in the safety of their
drinking water due to the high profile and volume of media attention surrounding PFAS. A
regulatory standard will support the Environment Agency in its designation of drinking
water protected areas and is therefore deemed a positive step to support efforts to reduce
exposure at source.
The Inspectorate’s current guidance was considered by the advisory group to be
precautionary and health-protective based upon the existing toxicology evidence. The
recommended prescribed concentration is based on concerns with the toxicity of PFAS and
that some may bioaccumulate in humans over time. Basing the PFAS on the water
companies’ risk assessment and the Inspectorate’s guidance allows for a risk based and
flexible approach which can be adapted should a new PFAS of concern emerge.
There was careful consideration for more stringent standards to be introduced for certain
PFAS where toxicological data are available. One advisory group member was very keen to
advocate for tighter standards for some individual PFAS to align with other regulatory
approaches. For certain PFAS, such as perfluorooctane sulfonic acid (PFOS),
perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) and perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA), there are some robust
toxicological data which may support introducing of tighter standards for some specific
PFAS. However, there are many different opinions internationally regarding which critical
effect and health-based guidance values are most appropriate. In England and Wales,

Evidence for risk to health and consumer acceptability
Studies on laboratory animals have reported various effects from PFAS ingestion, such as
adverse effects to the liver, thyroid, kidney and the immune system, as well as causing
developmental, neurodevelopment and reproductive toxicity (COT, 2022a; EFSA, 2020;
WHO Europe, 2017). In humans, several adverse health effects have been reported
including effects on the immune system, increased cholesterol and an increase in the
serum level of the liver enzyme alanine aminotransferase and reduced birthweight (WHO
Europe, 2017). The International Agency for Research on Cancer has classified PFOS as
possibly carcinogenic to humans (Group 2B) and PFOA is classified as carcinogenic to
humans (Group 1) (IARC, n.d.; IARC, 2023b). The EFSA concluded that there is insufficient
evidence that PFAS can cause cancer in humans (EFSA, 2020).
PFAS are not associated with taste or odour concerns. Concerns relating to PFAS in the
environment and drinking water attract significant media attention.


Sources and pathways to drinking water
PFAS are a large group of thousands of synthetic organic chemicals, including PFOS and
PFOA. They are identified by their strong carbon-fluorine bond. Once in the environment
PFAS are very persistent, remaining for many years and often being referred to as ‘forever
chemicals’. Being mobile they can have widespread occurrence in the environment. The
most detected and understood PFAS are PFOS, PFOA, PFNA and PFHxS. Classified as
persistent organic pollutants under the Stockholm Convention, the manufacture, sale and
use of products containing these three PFAS is banned except in some specific cases
(UNEP, n.d.). Several countries submitted a dossier under REACH to restrict the use of PFAS
in the EU due to their persistence, bioaccumulation and mobility (ECHA, 2024). This dossier
is being reviewed by the European Chemicals Agency.
PFAS are used in household products, including food packaging, cookware, clothing and
household furnishings, as well as firefighting foams and for industrial purposes. The
properties of PFAS, such as their resistance to very high heat and providing protection
from water, have led to their widespread use and application in everyday products. They
can enter the environment, and subsequently drinking water, from industrial and domestic
effluents. Potential PFAS sources identified in drinking water catchments include airports,
fire stations, wastewater discharges, trade effluents, industry, landfill, biosolids and sludge
applied to land (DWI, 2024e).


Occurrence in drinking water in England and Wales
The use of PFOS, PFOA and PFHxS are banned in England and Wales, however they
continue to persist in the environment. UK REACH have started preparing a restriction



                                                                                      63 of 87
dossier on the use of PFAS in fire-fighting foams and is considering further restrictions on
the use of PFAS (Defra, 2024).
Public water supplies: Since 2021, the Inspectorate has required water companies to
submit sampling results for concentrations of 47 PFAS in both source and treated waters.
Over 1 million analyses have been submitted to the Inspectorate since October 2021. Most
treated water samples, and source water samples where there is no PFAS treatment in
place, are below tier 3 (greater than or equal to 0.1 µg/L). In 2022, two treated water
samples were reported to be in tier 3, and in 2023, one sample was reported in tier 3. All
three of these samples were blended in the distribution network to below tier 1
concentrations so there was no risk to the consumer (DWI, 2024b). The understanding of
risk will evolve as further data are collected. Water companies are required to consider
risks from PFAS as part of their risk assessment process and to ensure systematic reduction
of risk in the short, medium and long terms.
Private water supplies: Sources of PFAS can be in rural and urban locations and therefore
private water supplies may be at risk from PFAS contamination. Sampling and analysis of
PFAS is complex and costly. Specific consideration for the practical application of a PFAS
standard by local authorities and private water suppliers is required. There is currently
minimal monitoring of PFAS in private water supplies. The Inspectorate encourages local
authorities to submit PFAS data in their annual data submissions. To date very little data
have been submitted. In 2023, 623 private water supplies in England and three in Wales
included PFAS in their regulatory risk assessments (DWI, 2024b). The Environment
Agency’s open dataset contain PFAS data for groundwater monitoring sites. When sources,
or nearby sources, are sampled and found to contain notable concentrations of PFAS, the
Agency informs private water supply owners and the relevant local authority.
Water treatment processes used by water companies to reduce concentrations of PFAS,
such as granular activated carbon or blending, are not widely used in private water
supplies. Where high concentrations of PFAS are detected, management strategies will
require specific consideration.
Research commissioned by the Inspectorate: The Inspectorate has carried out several
research projects to support policy development to date. Research has included an initial
understanding of prevalence and hazards, and to identify an analytical method for
monitoring PFAS (DWI, 2022c). A research project in which laboratory tests are being
conducted to review the removal efficacy of PFAS in certain water treatment processes is
in progress and is due to be published in 2025.


Treatment options and control
The preferred method of managing PFAS in drinking water is by controlling at source and
reducing their use and input into the environment (WHO Europe, 2017). Due to their
widespread use, occurrence and persistence in the environment however, removal
through drinking water treatment processes will be required.
The most widely accepted and commonly used treatment process for removing PFAS from
drinking water is granular activated carbon. This process is used widely by water


                                                                                     64 of 87
companies in England and Wales but less widely in private water supply systems. Carefully
considered operational procedures, for example carbon exhaustion rates, adsorption
capacity and bed volume ratios, are required to ensure performance is not affected by the
presence of natural organic matter in the source water (WHO Europe, 2017). Companies
should regularly test adsorbers to ensure that the carbon remains active for PFAS removal.
The resistance of PFAS to heat has led to difficulties with regeneration of the carbon
media.
Other treatment methods which may be effective for PFAS removal include membrane
filtration (reverse osmosis or nanofiltration), anion exchange and surface-active foam
flotation. Blending of water with higher concentrations of PFAS with water with lower
concentrations of PFAS is also accepted as a method for managing PFAS in drinking water.


Analytical capabilities
The Standing Committee of Analysts has not yet published a method for the analysis of
PFAS. There are currently various analytical methods, such as ISO standard 21675:2019 or
EN 17892:2024, available to measure certain PFAS. Both of these methods use LC-MS(MS)
but neither enable analysis of the 48 named PFAS listed in the Inspectorate’s current
guidance. Increasingly, water company laboratories across England and Wales are gaining
UKAS accreditation for the analysis for various PFAS, including the 20 required in the recast
Directive, the 47 as per the previous Inspectorate guidance or the 48 as per the current
guidance.


Other options considered
Do not introduce a statutory standard: PFAS are currently being managed by the
Inspectorate through guidance to the industry. The toxicity data are still under review by
the COT and the WHO, and the Inspectorate are awaiting the findings of both reviews.
Some experts considered that it may be too soon to establish appropriate regulatory
standards and that until the outcomes of these reviews are published, a flexible approach
to regulation through guidance may be more appropriate. On balance however, the expert
group considered that both reviews are anticipated to take considerable time, probably
years, to complete and a standard is required in the interim to promote public confidence
in drinking water safety.
Introduce a standard with prescribed concentration of 0.01 µg/L for 48 individually
named PFAS: This option was discounted by the group due to lack of toxicology data to
support such a standard being proportionate to safeguard public health.
Introduce a standard for specific named PFAS, such as PFOS and PFOA in addition to the
sum of 48 named PFAS: This option was considered by the group with a few members
advocating for tighter standards for specific PFAS where the evidence exists. TDIs have not
yet been derived by the COT for specific PFAS, however the Environment Agency has used
the EFSA tolerable weekly intake to derive an interim value for contaminated land. The

COT had reservations over the derivation of the tolerable weekly intake derived by EFSA
and therefore it was decided to await the outcome of the evaluation by the COT prior to
introducing standards for specific PFAS.
Introduce a standard with prescribed concentration of 0.1 µg/L for 48 individually named
PFAS and 0.5 µg/L for sum of PFAS: The UKHSA endorsed the introduction of a sum of
PFAS standard as health protective due to the potential for the combined effects arising
from co-exposure to similar PFAS that may cause similar effects acting via the same mode
of action. The revised Inspectorate guidance introduced a sum of value of 0.1 µg/L, in line
with the approach used by the EU and Scotland, and therefore introducing a standard
which was more lenient than the guidance value was disregarded by the advisory group.

of analytical methodology and treatment options to adhere to such a standard at this time.

Evidence for risk to health and consumer acceptability

Sources and pathways to drinking water

WHO guidance and toxicity derivations
In 2008, the WHO published a provisional guideline value for uranium of 15 µg/L (WHO,
2008). This was designated as provisional due to outstanding uncertainties with the
toxicology and epidemiology data, and concerns regarding its achievability for small
supplies. The guideline value was calculated using a LOAEL of 60 µg/kg body weight/day
based on kidney toxicity in animal studies, and an uncertainty factor of 100 to calculate a
TDI of 0.6 µg/kg body weight/day. This TDI was endorsed by the COT (COT, 2006).
Allocating 80% of the TDI to drinking water provided a guideline value of 15 µg/Ls (WHO,
2005).
Following publication of new epidemiology studies on populations exposed to high
uranium concentrations, the WHO relaxed this guideline value to a provisional value of 30
µg/L (WHO, 2022). The revised guideline value was derived using the lower 95%
confidence limit on the 95th percentile exposure distribution of 637 µg/day, which is
considered as the NOAEL, and an uncertainty factor of 10 for intraspecies variation, giving
a TDI of 60 µg/day (rounded) (WHO, 2012). The human epidemiological studies indicated
that there is no clear evidence for effects on the kidney, the most sensitive adverse effect,
if the concentration of uranium in water is below a drinking water concentration of 30 µg/L
(WHO, 2022).


Evidence for risk to health and consumer acceptability
Uranium is a chemically toxic radionuclide. The primary health concern from uranium in
drinking water is its chemical and not radiological toxicity (UKWIR and WRc, 2019e; WHO,
2022). Ingestion of uranium through drinking water can cause nephritis (inflammation of
the kidneys), and changes in bone structure, although the dose required to cause such
effects is largely unknown (BGS, 2006; UKWIR and WRc, 2019e; WHO, 2005). There is little
information available on the chronic health effects of uranium consumption in humans and
to date no clear no-effect concentration has emerged from studies on humans up to a
concentration of approximately 30 µg/L (UKWIR and WRc, 2019e; WHO, 2022).
There are no known aesthetic concerns related to uranium in drinking water (UKWIR/WRc,
2019e).


Sources and pathways to drinking water
Uranium is a naturally occurring contaminant in some source waters. It can enter drinking
water through leaching from natural deposits and from anthropogenic sources such as
release from mill tailings, emissions from the nuclear industry, combustion of coal and
other fuels and the use of uranium-containing phosphate fertilizers (BGS, 2006; WHO,
2022). As a list II substance under the European Commission Groundwater Directive,
inputs of uranium to the environment must be limited (BGS, 2006). Uranium

Operational standards
Operational monitoring consists of proactive activities which demonstrate the
effectiveness of control measures to ensure the safety of a water supply (WHO Europe,
2017). It can complement verification, or compliance monitoring and is an essential
component of water safety planning. In their report to the European Commission, the
WHO recommended the inclusion of certain parameters in operational monitoring to
further strengthen risk-based management (WHO Europe, 2017). This review included
somatic coliphages and turbidity.
The advisory group considered the concept of introducing ‘operational standards’ and how
this would work in practice by both the regulator and the regulated. Legal advice would be
required to understand the implications and enforcement of an exceedance of an
‘operational standard’, as well as its monitoring and reporting requirements, and
mechanisms for achieving funding to resolve where issues are identified. The introduction
of any operational standards needs to be considered in the context of Better Regulation to
ensure proportionate regulation, and that there is consideration for the additional
resource and financial implications that the introduction of such standards are associated
with, such as the need for additional analytical methods and accreditation of sampling
methods.

indicator and monitoring regime. Establish a regulatory list of parameters of interest to
ensure continued evidence gathering and add somatic coliphages, or other appropriate
viral indicators to such list. Review the Inspectorate’s guidance provided to the industry
and local authorities regarding expectations to assess the risk from viruses in source and
treated waters.
Justification: There was consensus within the advisory group that greater consideration
and risk assessment for viruses in drinking water is needed, but at this time evidence of the
benefits of somatic coliphage monitoring is lacking. There was divergence in opinion
regarding the most effective approach to safeguard the risks for the protection of public
health. Companies are already required, under regulation 26, to have site specific
disinfection policies which ensure the inactivation of all pathogens to maintain water
microbial safety under all operating conditions.
As with other microbial indicator organisms listed in the Regulations, the presence of
coliphages themselves are not of direct health concern to humans. They are indicative of
viruses of faecal origin, regardless of the host animal, which can help to identify potential
sources of faecal contamination in source waters. Somatic coliphages can also be used to
demonstrate removal of different and much smaller microorganisms than bacteria in


                                                                                      75 of 87
drinking water treatment. The advisory group identified the benefits of introducing an
operational standard for somatic coliphages would be i) to provide an indicator of the
potential presence of viruses in groundwater, and removal by water treatment, and ii) to
align with the European Commission and Scotland.
Conversely, the advisory group identified concerns around the practicality and suitability of
somatic coliphages as a viral indicator for assessing water treatment performance, and the
adequacy of evidence of the benefits of monitoring at this time. An effective standard
which would be representative of risk, compatible with the internationally accepted
health-based target of 1 micro DALY (disability adjusted life year) for microbiologically safe
drinking water, would require very large volume sampling, potentially in the order of
millions of litres. Small sample volumes would result in low chance of detections, especially
in treated waters due to somatic coliphages being highly sensitive to chlorination. Such
large volume sampling is not practically feasible for routine application in water company
laboratories. The advisory group were concerned that, although carried out in emergencies
or to monitor for Cryptosporidium, large volume sampling for an indicator organism at this
time is not proportionate to the risk to public health and benefits which may be seen.
On balance, it was decided that further consideration for viral protection of drinking water
supplies is required to ensure the most effective approach is followed.


Background
Coliphages are viruses which infect E. coli and closely related bacteria. They are classified
as either somatic or F-specific coliphages, depending on their mode of attachment to the
host cell. Coliphages can indicate the likely presence of faecal contamination, from humans
or animals, with a possibility that harmful viruses may also be present. Drinking water
quality legislation in England and Wales include no requirements for monitoring viruses at
present.
The WHO have not introduced a guideline value for somatic coliphages. In its report to the
European Commission, the WHO recommended periodic monitoring of somatic coliphages,
as an indicator of the presence of viruses, in groundwater and, as an indicator of treatment
efficacy, in treated water at a concentration of 0 PFU/100 ml (WHO Europe, 2017). This is
to complement existing microbial monitoring including E. coli and Clostridium perfringens.
The European Commission introduced an operational standard of 50 PFU/100ml to be
adopted for source water, with a requirement to demonstrate that their numbers in
treated drinking water do not exceed 100 PFU/100 ml (Table 17).

Evidence for risk to health and consumer acceptability
Somatic coliphages are viruses which in themselves do not pose a direct risk to human
health. They can be an indicator of the occurrence and survival of viruses in water
environments and their fate and behaviour through the treatment process (WHO Europe,
2017).
The drinking water quality regulations include the requirement to monitor for faecal
indicator bacteria of E. coli and Enterococci. Indicator bacteria were adopted to determine
the potential of faecal contamination and, therefore, the likely presence of enteric
pathogens of all types. Latterly, it was recognised that other indicators, such as coliphages,
may be a better indicator for the occurrence and fate and behaviour of viruses. The WHO
recommended that somatic coliphages should be monitored, and not exceed a value of 0
PFU/100 ml, in groundwaters to ensure protection of enteric viruses in these sources, and
post treatment (0 PFU/100 ml) to check sufficient elimination of enteric viruses from
drinking water (WHO Europe, 2017).

Occurrence in drinking water
Somatic coliphages are excreted by humans and animals. They occur in sewage in the
order of 106 to 108 somatic coliphages per litre and have been detected in lake and river
water at up to 105 per litre (WHO Europe, 2017). A research project commissioned by the
Inspectorate examined the occurrence of human enteric viruses across 13 water supplies
in England (UKWIR, 2024b). The research found low numbers in most source waters with
more detections in surface waters compared to groundwaters. The research highlighted
the need for more monitoring of viruses across different water sources. Consequently, the
Inspectorate has commissioned further research to better understand the presence of
somatic coliphages in different water sources.


Analytical capabilities
There is currently no method approved by the Standing Committee of Analysts to analyse
somatic coliphages. ISO standard 10705-2:2000 provides the detection and enumeration of
bacteriophages of which part two includes the enumeration of somatic coliphages. Water
company laboratories would need to develop an analytical method, and gain UKAS
accreditation for analysis for somatic coliphages. Currently, no water company laboratories
in England and Wales hold such accreditation. Scottish Water has gained accreditation.
Once set up, analysis of somatic coliphages is relatively simple and inexpensive, and can
provide results within 24 hours (WHO Europe, 2017). No specialist equipment is required,
above that which is already used in microbiology laboratories however additional training
on new techniques may be required.
Large volume sampling, which may be more effective for assessment of risk, are more
resource and time intensive than examining conventional volumes of 100 ml.

Turbidity
Consideration: Introduce a new operational standard.
Recommendation: Introduce operational turbidity monitoring, for supplies which require
filtration as a water treatment process, to complement existing compliance requirements.
Currently, turbidity must be less than 1 NTU at the point of disinfection, as per regulation
26(b)(ii), and must be less than 1 NTU upon leaving the water treatment works. Note,
these requirements are less stringent in the Private Water Supply Regulations. This would
require monitoring at the outlet of each individual filter. Turbidity should be kept as low as
reasonably achievable and should be less than 0.3 NTU for 95% of the time and not exceed
0.5 NTU for 15 consecutive minutes (WHO Europe, 2017).
Justification: The WHO recommended the inclusion of turbidity as an operational
parameter to verify adequate performance of filtration processes (WHO Europe, 2017).
There was consensus amongst the advisory group that this should be adopted in England
and Wales to verify filter performance. This could support recommendations made in
relation to other parameters in this document and public health concerns, such as
breakthrough of microplastics and parasitic protozoa.
