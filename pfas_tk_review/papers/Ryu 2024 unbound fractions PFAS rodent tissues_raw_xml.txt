However, data on PFAS binding in human tissues are limited. Accumulation of PFAS in human tissues has been linked to interactions with specific proteins and lipids in target organs. Additional data on PFAS binding and unbound fractions (<italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub>) in whole human tissues are urgently needed. Here we address this gap by using rapid equilibrium dialysis to measure the binding and <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> of 16 PFAS with 3 to 13 perfluorinated carbon atoms (<italic toggle="yes">η</italic><sub>pfc</sub> = 3–13) and several functional head-groups in human liver, lung, kidney, heart, and brain tissue. We compare results to mouse (C57BL/6 and CD-1) and rat tissues. Results show that <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> decreases with increasing fluorinated carbon chain length and hydrophobicity. Among human tissues, PFAS binding was generally greatest in brain&gt;liver≈kidneys≈heart&gt;lungs. A correlation analysis among human and rodent tissues identified rat liver as suitable surrogate for predicting <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> for PFAS in human tissues (R<sup>2</sup> ≥0.98). The <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> data resulting from this work and the rat liver prediction method offer input parameters and tools for toxicokinetic models for legacy and emerging PFAS.</p></abstract><abstract id="ABS2" abstract-type="graphical"><title>Graphical Abstract</title><p id="P2">

id="S1"><label>1.</label><title>Introduction</title><p id="P3">Per- and polyfluorinated alkyl substances (PFAS) are a diverse class of anthropogenic chemicals with a wide range of commercial and industrial applications.<sup><xref rid="R1" ref-type="bibr">1</xref></sup> Their extensive use has resulted in detectable concentrations in drinking water, ambient air, animals, and in virtually all humans.<sup><xref rid="R1" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>–<xref rid="R13" ref-type="bibr">13</xref></sup> PFAS exposures are associated with adverse health outcomes, such as thyroid hormone disruption, decreased response to vaccination,<sup><xref rid="R14" ref-type="bibr">14</xref></sup> dyslipidemia,<sup><xref rid="R15" ref-type="bibr">15</xref></sup> and hepatotoxicity.<sup><xref rid="R16" ref-type="bibr">16</xref></sup> Slow elimination and strong tissue accumulation of PFAS are typically associated with increased toxicity.<sup><xref rid="R17" ref-type="bibr">17</xref></sup> While hundreds of PFAS have been detected in the environment, tissue-specific accumulation has been tested only for a handful of PFAS.<sup><xref rid="R18" ref-type="bibr">18</xref></sup> Accumulation and slow elimination of PFAS in humans have been associated with binding to isolated biomolecules;<sup><xref rid="R19" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>–<xref rid="R21" ref-type="bibr">21</xref></sup> however, they have not yet been linked to PFAS binding to whole tissues. Data and predictive tools for characterizing PFAS binding in whole human tissues are needed and are the focus of this work.</p><p id="P4">PFAS with long fluorinated carbon chains (<italic toggle="yes">η</italic><sub>pfc</sub> ≥7), such as perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA, <italic toggle="yes">η</italic><sub>pfc</sub> = 7) and perfluorooctane sulfonic acid (PFOS, <italic toggle="yes">η</italic><sub>pfc</sub> = 8), show strong accumulation in humans and have elimination half-lives that range between 3–6 years.<sup><xref rid="R22" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>–<xref rid="R24" ref-type="bibr">24</xref></sup> In contrast, short-chain PFAS like perfluorobutanoic acid (PFBS, <italic toggle="yes">η</italic><sub>pfc</sub> = 3) are eliminated by humans in weeks.<sup><xref rid="R25" ref-type="bibr">25</xref></sup> After absorption in the gastrointestinal tract, PFAS pass through liver and enter the circulatory system.<sup><xref rid="R21" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>,<xref rid="R26" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>–<xref rid="R28" ref-type="bibr">28</xref></sup> For PFAS with <italic toggle="yes">η</italic><sub>pfc</sub> ≥7, binding to proteins and phospholipids are thought to determine elimination half-lives.<sup><xref rid="R29" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>–<xref rid="R31" ref-type="bibr">31</xref></sup> For PFAS with <italic toggle="yes">η</italic><sub>pfc</sub>&lt;6, membrane transporters, enterohepatic recirculation, and renal tubular reabsorption may play a more important role in tissue distribution.<sup><xref rid="R31" ref-type="bibr">31</xref>,<xref rid="R32" ref-type="bibr">32</xref></sup> Binding of PFAS to tissues is driven by their physicochemical properties (e.g., <italic toggle="yes">η</italic><sub>pfc</sub>, hydrophobicity, p<italic toggle="yes">K</italic>a), the macromolecular composition of the tissue (e.g., structural proteins, phospholipids), and abundantly expressed functional proteins (e.g., fatty acid binding proteins and albumin).<sup><xref rid="R33" ref-type="bibr">33</xref>,<xref rid="R34" ref-type="bibr">34</xref></sup> An increasing number of structurally diverse PFAS means there is a need to systematically characterize PFAS binding to human tissues and identify the physicochemical properties that drive tissue accumulation. However, PFAS binding to human tissues beyond plasma have not yet been measured.</p><p id="P5">The unbound fraction of chemicals (<italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub>) in tissues is typically the amount that is available for inter-tissue transport and interacts with molecular targets that elicit toxic effects.<sup><xref rid="R35" ref-type="bibr">35</xref></sup>
<italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> is inversely correlated to the tissue binding affinity, which can be quantified by the tissue-water partition coefficient (<italic toggle="yes">K</italic><sub>tissue/w</sub>).<sup><xref rid="R31" ref-type="bibr">31</xref></sup> PFAS that are bound to tissue components (e.g., proteins, phospholipids) are considered unavailable for renal and hepatic clearance until being desorbed as unbound PFAS are eliminated,<sup><xref rid="R36" ref-type="bibr">36</xref>–<xref rid="R38" ref-type="bibr">38</xref></sup> thereby prolonging elimination half-lives. At steady-state, unbound concentrations (<italic toggle="yes">C</italic><sub>unbound</sub>) are expected to be similar among tissues, assuming negligible influence of membrane transporter-mediated flux.<sup><xref rid="R39" ref-type="bibr">39</xref></sup>
<italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> and <italic toggle="yes">K</italic><sub>tissue/w</sub> can be used to predict tissue-specific accumulation and elimination half-lives. Comprehensive studies on experimental <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> and <italic toggle="yes">K</italic><sub>tissue/w</sub> in whole human tissues are urgently needed to assess PFAS accumulation and tissue distribution in humans.</p><p id="P6">Previous studies measured PFAS binding to isolated macromolecules, such as proteins (bovine serum albumin [BSA]<sup><xref rid="R40" ref-type="bibr">40</xref>,<xref rid="R41" ref-type="bibr">41</xref></sup>, human serum albumin [HSA]<sup><xref rid="R42" ref-type="bibr">42</xref></sup>, fatty-acid binding protein [FABP]<sup><xref rid="R43" ref-type="bibr">43</xref></sup>) and phospholipids.<sup><xref rid="R44" ref-type="bibr">44</xref></sup> Partition coefficients derived from such studies have been used to parameterize physiologically-based toxicokinetic (PBTK) models and simulate <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> and the tissue distribution of PFAS.<sup><xref rid="R19" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>,<xref rid="R20" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>,<xref rid="R29" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>,<xref rid="R31" ref-type="bibr">31</xref></sup> Machine-learning and molecular dynamics simulations are <italic toggle="yes">in silico</italic> substitutes to experimental derivation of <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> and tissue binding.<sup><xref rid="R45" ref-type="bibr">45</xref></sup> While these simulations provide mechanistic insights into the interaction of PFAS with single biomolecules,<sup><xref rid="R38" ref-type="bibr">38</xref></sup> they have rarely been evaluated against experimental <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> in whole tissues. Rodent tissues have been used to generate data on <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> for diverse pharmaceuticals that were useful for predicting <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> in human tissues.<sup><xref rid="R46" ref-type="bibr">46</xref>–<xref rid="R49" ref-type="bibr">49</xref></sup> Extending tissue partitioning measurements for PFAS in rodents to humans might similarly be useful.</p><p id="P7">The main objective of this work was to evaluate the suitability of <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> and <italic toggle="yes">K</italic><sub>tissue/w</sub> measured in rodent model tissues for predicting PFAS binding in human tissues. Furthermore, this is the first study to report unbound fractions in various human tissues. This study leverages a well-established framework for pharmaceuticals and a high-throughput equilibrium dialysis method<sup><xref rid="R50" ref-type="bibr">50</xref>–<xref rid="R52" ref-type="bibr">52</xref></sup> to measure <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> and <italic toggle="yes">K</italic><sub>tissue/w</sub> of 16 PFAS with <italic toggle="yes">η</italic><sub>pfc</sub> 3–13 and different functional head groups (carboxylic acids, sulfonic acids, and sulfonamides) in brain, heart, kidney, liver, and lung tissues of humans and rodents (mouse, rat). These tissues were selected because they have been shown to accumulate PFAS in humans.<sup><xref rid="R19" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>,<xref rid="R21" ref-type="bibr">21</xref></sup> Inter-species and -tissue correlation analyses were used to evaluate how well various rodent tissues predicted <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> in human tissues. Regression modeling was applied to assess the relationship between physicochemical properties of PFAS and their <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> in various tissues. Using our data, we discuss potential mechanisms driving PFAS toxicokinetics in humans and the suitability of rodent surrogate tissues as a fast and straightforward tool for predicting human PFAS tissue distribution.</p></sec><sec id="S2"><label>2.</label><title>Materials and Methods</title><sec id="S3"><label>2.1</label><title>Chemicals and Reagents.</title><p id="P8">The study PFAS included 11 perfluoroalkyl carboxylates, 3 perfluoroalkyl sulfonates, 1 perfluoroalkyl sulfonamide, and 1 fluorotelomer sulfonate. Physicochemical properties of PFAS ranged from <italic toggle="yes">η</italic><sub>pfc</sub> from 3 to 13, molecular weight (M<sub>w</sub>) from 214.04 to 714.11 g/mol, p<italic toggle="yes">K</italic>a from −0.21 to 6.25,<sup><xref rid="R53" ref-type="bibr">53</xref></sup> and octanol-water distribution coefficients (log<italic toggle="yes">D</italic>, pH: 7.0) from 2.23 to 10.11 (<xref rid="SD1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Tables S1</xref> and <xref rid="SD1" ref-type="supplementary-material">S2</xref>). Standards for perfluorobutanoic acid (PFBA), perfluoropentanoic acid (PFPA), perfluorohexanoic acid (PFHxA), perfluoroheptanoic acid (PFHpA), perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA), perfluorodecanoic acid (PFDA), perfluoroundecanoic acid (PFUDA), perfluorododecanoic acid (PFDoDA), perfluorotridecanoic acid (PFTriDA), perfluorotetradecanoic acid (PFTeDA), perfluorohexane sulfonic acid (PFHxS), perfluorooctanesulfonamide (PFOSA), and 6:2 fluorotelomer sulfonate (6:2 FtS) were purchased from AccuStandard. Standards for perfluorobutane sulfonic acid (PFBS) and perfluorooctane sulfonic acid (PFOS) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Brain, heart, kidney, liver, and lung from commonly used rat and mouse strains (Wistar Han rat, CD-1 and C57BL/6J mouse) were obtained from BioIVT and the Pfizer Drug and Safety Research &amp; Development (DSRD) tissue bank and pooled by sex. All mouse and rat work was performed according to Pfizer Global Research policies and procedures. Human heart, kidney, liver, and lung were purchased from BioIVT, and human brain was obtained from the NIH NeuroBioBank<sup><xref rid="R54" ref-type="bibr">54</xref></sup> as a pool of 2 male and 2 female donors. All human tissues were processed following University of Rhode Island and Pfizer Institutional Review Board approved protocols. The equilibrium dialysis device (HTD96) and cellulose membranes with a molecular weight cutoff of 12–14 kDa were obtained from HTDialysis, LLC. All other reagents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich unless otherwise specified.</p></sec><sec id="S4"><label>2.2</label><title>Tissue homogenization and preparation</title><p id="P9">Tissues were processed according to previously described methods.<sup><xref rid="R47" ref-type="bibr">47</xref></sup> Briefly, frozen tissues were completely thawed in a 37°C water bath. Small incisions were made to the tissues to minimize blood contamination. Thawed tissues were soaked in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS, pH: 7.4, ThermoFisher) for five minutes. The pH in the suspension was measured for all experiments. Tissue samples were subsequently dried with paper towel, weighed, and diluted to either 1:5 or 1:10 (brain) using DPBS. Tissues were homogenized using a TH tissue homogenizer (Omni). In a second step, the tissues were homogenized further using a glass dounce homogenizer (Wheaton).</p></sec><sec id="S5"><label>2.3</label><title>Equilibrium Dialysis</title><p id="P10">The equilibrium dialysis methods used an HTD 96 micro equilibrium device for binding studies that was similar to previously reported techniques.<sup><xref rid="R50" ref-type="bibr">50</xref>–<xref rid="R52" ref-type="bibr">52</xref></sup> Briefly, DBPS buffer solution containing PFAS was prepared. The receiver concentration was ~five-fold the estimated <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> concentration based on preliminary data using the same test setup or was assumed to be 0.001 (unitless) if no prior data were available. Porous membranes of the dialysis apparatus were pre-saturated twice with 250 μL of PFAS-spiked DBPS buffer for one hour and overnight (~18 h), respectively. Stock solutions of single PFAS in dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) were prepared and added to tissue homogenates yielding final concentrations of 5 μM (1% DMSO). This concentration was chosen to optimize the LC-MS/MS signal while maintaining a PFAS-to-tissue homogenate ratio that avoids saturation effects observed for proteins like serum albumin.<sup><xref rid="R37" ref-type="bibr">37</xref>,<xref rid="R55" ref-type="bibr">55</xref></sup> 150 μL of PFAS-free DBPS were added to the receiver and 150 μL of PFAS-spiked tissue homogenates were added to the donor side. Replicates <italic toggle="yes">(n</italic>=4) of PFAS:tissue combinations were tested. The HTD was sealed with a gas-permeable membrane (Sigma) and shaken at 200 rpm at 5% CO<sub>2</sub>/air, 75% relative humidity, and 37 °C for 18 h. For protein and tissue precipitation, 15 μL of buffer and 45 μL of tissue homogenate were transferred into a 96-deep well plate. To ensure there were no interferences between wells during the LC-MS/MS run, an additional 45 μL of blank matrix was added to the buffer side and 15 μL of blank buffer to the matrix side in the sample plate to create matrix-matched samples. The samples were quenched with 200 μL of cold acetonitrile containing tolbutamide (30 nM) and carbamazepine (12 nM) that were used as internal standards to monitor the performance of the LC-MS/MS. The plates were sealed, vortexed for 1 min, and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min. We used <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> in the liver, brain, heart, kidney, and lung tissue to develop regression models. PLSR is a multivariate approach that allows many responses (i.e., <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> in various tissues) to be incorporated in one regression model. PLSR performs a descriptor dimension reduction procedure and constructs a set of latent components that maximize the total descriptors variance in the dataset to generate the model with the best predictive power for the target responses.<sup><xref rid="R62" ref-type="bibr">62</xref></sup> Using the PLSR approach, we developed four models based on the experimental <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> results obtained from the four organisms: C57, human, mouse, and rat. A total of 20 physicochemical properties were calculated from Molecular Operation Environment (MOE) software version 2018.01 (Chemical Computing Group ULC) and Dragon software version 6.0 (Talete srl). In the LOOCV procedure, a PFAS was considered as a test set for model evaluation and the remaining 15 PFAS were used as training set for developing the model. This process was repeated 16 times, such that each PFAS was used as a test set once. Parameters for building the model (i.e., number of components) were tuned using a grid search in the cross-validation process. Higher molecular weight (M<sub>w</sub>) PFAS with longer perfluorinated carbon chains and higher log<italic toggle="yes">D</italic> have high tissue binding affinity, resulting in lower <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> than the shorter-chain, low M<sub>w</sub> PFAS with low log<italic toggle="yes">D</italic>.</p><p id="P16">We observed a strong relationship between M<sub>w</sub> and <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub>. For example, PFTeDA (M<sub>w</sub>: 714) exhibited a low <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> in human liver tissue (6.8 × 10<sup>−6</sup>), in contrast to PFBA (M<sub>w</sub>: 214), which showed six orders of magnitude higher <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> of 0.3. Both PFAS are carboxylic acids, but the increase in fluorinated carbon chain length and lipophilicity resulted in a 45,000x stronger binding of PFTeDA (<italic toggle="yes">η</italic><sub>pfc</sub> = 14; log<italic toggle="yes">D</italic>: 10.11) than PFBA (<italic toggle="yes">η</italic><sub>pfc</sub> = 3; log<italic toggle="yes">D</italic>: 2.2). A similar trend for binding to serum albumin and globulins was recently reported, where carboxylates showed increased binding as M<sub>w</sub> increased up until a plateau at a Mw ≈ 500–600 g/mol.<sup><xref rid="R29" ref-type="bibr">29</xref></sup> Interestingly, this plateau was not observed in this study (<xref rid="F1" ref-type="fig">Fig. 1</xref>). Interestingly, liver/tissue concentration ratios were similar between binding experiments and in humans for the long-chain PFNA and PFOS (<italic toggle="yes">η</italic><sub>pfc</sub> = 8), whereas PFHxS (<italic toggle="yes">η</italic><sub>pfc</sub> = 6) and PFOA (<italic toggle="yes">η</italic><sub>pfc</sub> = 7) were overestimated in the liver as compared to kidney and lung tissue (<xref rid="SD1" ref-type="supplementary-material">Fig. S7</xref>). Results show only small differences in tissue binding across biological species. We find rat liver provides a suitable surrogate tissue for predicting binding of PFAS in various human tissues (kidney, brain, lung, heart) when used in combination with appropriate scaling factors. Considering the large number and structural heterogeneity of PFAS, rat liver provides a useful surrogate for screening and categorizing the internal distribution of PFAS in humans. To reduce animal use, future studies should explore alternative surrogate materials, such as polymers known to bind PFAS, to predict human tissue binding of these compounds. Data on <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> and <italic toggle="yes">K</italic><sub>tissue/w</sub> reported in this study can help parameterize physiologically-based toxicokinetic (PBTK) models that are useful for quantifying the elimination and distribution of PFAS. However, it is important to consider that other toxicokinetic processes, which may be more species-dependent than tissue binding, such as membrane transporter expression, could introduce inaccuracies when applying the predicted <italic toggle="yes">f</italic><sub>unbound</sub> in human PBTK models.
