For commercial re-use, please contact journals.permissions@oup.comAbstractPer- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are a diverse class of anthropogenic chemicals; many are persistent, bioaccumulative, and mobile in the environment. Worldwide, PFAS bioaccumulation causes serious adverse health impacts, yet the physiochemical determinants of bioaccumulation and toxicity for most PFAS are not well understood, largely due to experimental data deficiencies. As most PFAS are proteinophilic, protein binding is a critical parameter for predicting PFAS bioaccumulation and toxicity. Among these proteins, human serum albumin (HSA) is the predominant blood transport protein for many PFAS. We previously demonstrated the utility of an in vitro differential scanning fluorimetry assay for determining relative HSA binding affinities for 24 PFAS. Here, we report HSA affinities for 65 structurally diverse PFAS from 20 chemical classes. We leverage these experimental data, and chemical/molecular descriptors of PFAS, to build 7 machine learning classifier algorithms and 9 regression algorithms, and evaluate their performance to identify the best predictive binding models. Evaluation of model accuracy revealed that the top-performing classifier model, logistic regression, had an AUROC (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve) statistic of 0.936. The top-performing regression model, support vector regression, had an R2 of 0.854. These top-performing models were then used to predict HSA–PFAS binding for chemicals in the EPAPFASINV list of 430 PFAS. These developed in vitro and in silico methodologies represent a high-throughput framework for predicting protein–PFAS binding based on empirical data, and generate directly comparable binding data of potential use in predictive modeling of PFAS bioaccumulation and other toxicokinetic endpoints.Keywords: binding affinity, bioaccumulation, computational toxicology, PFAS, protein bindingstatusreleaseddisplay-pdfyesis-olfnois-manuscriptnois-preprintnois-journal-matternois-scannednois-retractednoCollection date 2025 Jan.Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are a diverse class of synthetic organofluorine chemicals that are used widely in commercial and industrial applications. Owing to the strength of their carbon-fluorine bonds, PFAS and their terminal breakdown products are resistant to enzymatic and thermal degradation, and thus accumulate in the environment. The persistence, mobility, and worldwide use of PFAS have resulted in the ubiquitous detection of these chemicals in environmental matrices, and in humans, plants, and wildlife (Sunderland et al. 2019; De Silva et al. 2021). The toxicity of some PFAS targets several organ systems (hepatic, renal, immune, endocrine, etc) and has caused escalating public and regulatory concern regarding the adverse health impacts resulting from exposure (Fenton et al. 2021; Carlson et al. 2022; Radke et al. 2022; Crawford et al. 2023). Although PFAS comprise a large heterogenous class of thousands of chemicals, research has predominantly focused on a small subset of perfluoroalkyl acid congeners, whereas the fundamental physiochemical properties of several thousand PFAS remain undefined (Radke et al. 2022).Due to major physiochemical differences between the highly fluorinated PFAS and other persistent organic pollutants, which tend to be lipophilic, data and computational models describing the transport and fate of other organic pollutants do not adequately inform PFAS toxicokinetics (Conder et al. 2008; Cheng and Ng 2017). As amphiphilic compounds, many PFAS are bound by proteins and accumulate in highly perfused protein-rich tissue compartments, including the blood, liver, and kidneys (Han et al. 2003; Bischel et al. 2011; Jian et al. 2018; Sheng et al. 2018). Protein–PFAS interactions are thus a critical variable for computationally determining the transport, bioaccumulation, and half-life of PFAS (Ng and Hungerbühler 2013; Ng and Hungerbühler 2014; Cheng and Ng 2017, 2018; Dassuncao et al. 2019; Zhao et al. 2023; Fischer et al. 2024).Human serum albumin (HSA) is the primary transport protein in human blood for many endogenous and exogenous ligands, including many PFAS (Peters 1995; Fasano et al. 2005; Forsthuber et al. 2020; Jackson et al. 2021). Increased HSA–PFAS binding affinities are associated with increased circulatory half-life, delayed elimination, and decreased plasma-free fraction of PFAS in the body (Han et al. 2003; Cheng and Ng 2017; Fischer et al. 2024). However, while an increased HSA–PFAS binding affinity leads to increased bioaccumulative potential of the PFAS, the unbound or free fraction of PFAS in the plasma is the more bioavailable fraction, which is often associated with greater likelihood of tissue transport, reactivity, and toxicity (Cheng and Ng 2017; Watanabe et al. 2018; Gao et al. 2019; Sheng et al. 2020; Qin et al. 2023; Smeltz et al. 2023; Fischer et al. 2024). Accurate measures of albumin–PFAS binding are needed to improve the accuracy of pharmacologically-based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) models, and other computational models aimed at elucidating PFAS toxicokinetics, which remain poorly understood (Ng and Hungerbühler 2014; Cheng and Ng 2017; Jackson et al. 2021; Alesio et al. 2022; Fischer et al. 2024). Yet, experimentally determined HSA binding affinity estimates exist for only a small number of PFAS congeners.Although several advanced computational approaches hold great potential for predicting PFAS–protein interactions, as well as PFAS bioactivity and toxicity, the efficacy of these models is limited by this lack of basic physiochemical data (Cheng and Ng 2017, 2018, 2019). The majority of calculated HSA–PFAS binding affinities are not directly comparable across different studies and experimental approaches, and binding affinity estimates for a single congener can range across orders of magnitude (Chen and Guo 2009; Bischel et al. 2010; MacManus-Spencer et al. 2010; Beesoon and Martin 2015; Chi et al. 2018; Jackson et al. 2021; Li et al. 2021; Maso et al. 2021). One contributing factor for the paucity of PFAS binding data is that several of the most common approaches for measuring albumin–PFAS binding, including equilibrium dialysis and isothermal titration calorimetry, are expensive, time-consuming, and/or require large volumes of protein, making them insufficient for the large-scale determination of binding affinities for thousands of PFAS congeners (Vuignier et al. 2010; Liu et al. 2019; Jackson et al. 2021).To address the need for directly comparable data describing albumin binding of PFAS, our lab has developed and used an in vitro differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) assay to determine relative binding affinities of HSA to structurally informative sets of different PFAS. The DSF assay measures concentration-dependent changes in the melting temperature (TM) of HSA with increasing concentrations of a PFAS, and those data are then used to estimate binding affinity (Ka) values (Vivoli et al. 2014; Jackson et al. 2021). Using this DSF assay, we have determined relative PFAS binding affinities in serum albumin from humans, as well as laboratory and livestock animals (Jackson et al. 2021; Starnes et al. 2024). However, due to the vast number of PFAS in production and use, with a least 14,000 PFAS chemical structures currently identified, there is a critical need for high-throughput methodologies to facilitate the rapid evaluation of protein–PFAS interactions (Ng and Hungerbuehler 2015). Machine learning approaches are uniquely positioned to address these data gaps, as machine learning is a powerful, artificial intelligence-based tool used to automate analytical model building, and allow for robust pattern identification in complex datasets (Sedkaoui 2018; Green et al. 2021; Andersen and Reading 2024).To address the lack of experimental binding data for thousands of PFAS congeners, we developed a stepwise, high-throughput approach in which (i) DSF is used to calculate HSA binding affinities for diverse PFAS, (ii) DSF binding data is then used to train and optimize machine learning algorithms to build: (iii) optimized classification models to predict binary binding (yes/no) of PFAS, and (iv) optimized regression models to predict HSA–PFAS binding affinities. In this study, we first used DSF to determine HSA binding affinities for a curated binding affinity dataset of 65 PFAS from 20 chemical classes. DSF binding affinity data for 3 fatty acids, representing non-fluorinated structural analogs of some perfluoroalkyl carboxylic acids (PFCAs), were also included in the training data for machine learning models. These fatty acids were included to further assess the impact of fluorination on HSA binding of PFAS. We trained 7 machine learning models to make binary binding predictions (yes/no binding), and 9 regression-based models were trained to make quantitative binding affinity (Ka) predictions. Model performance was evaluated using 3-by-3-fold cross-validation, and the metrics of Cohens kappa statistic, area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), and root mean squared error (RMSE). The optimally performing binary classifier and quantitative regression models were ultimately used to generate binding predictions for hundreds of PFAS in the EPAPFASINV chemical inventory list, allowing the direct comparison of HSA binding across hundreds of diverse PFAS congeners.Materials and methodsChemicals and reagentsAqueous solutions were prepared with sterile Milli-Q A10 water (18 Ω; 3 ppb total oxidizable organics). GloMelt (λEx = 468, λEm = 507 nm) and carboxyrhodamine (ROX; λEx=117,588, λEm = 608 nm) dyes (CAT No. 33022-1) were purchased from Biotium (Fremont, CA, United States). HSA (purity ≥95%, fraction V, CAS 70024-90-7, CAT No. 126658) was from Millipore Sigma (Burlington, MA, United States). Methanol (meOH, purity ≥99.9%, CAT No. A454-4), dimethyl sulfoxide (purity ≥99.9%, CAT No. 136-1, Lot 147002), Na2HPO4 (purity ≥99.2%, CAT No. S374-3, Lot 056560), NaCl (purity ≥100%, CAT No. S271-10, Lot 134874), and 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES, purity ≥99.0%, CAT No. BP310-1, Lot 052975) were from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, United States). Details on chemical class, structure, purity, and purchasing information for all fatty acids and PFAS congeners analyzed in this study are detailed in Tables S1 and S2.Albumin and chemical preparationFatty acid and PFAS stock solutions (5, 10, or 20 mM) were prepared in aqueous HEPES-buffered saline (HBS; final concentrations were 140 mM NaCl, 50 mM HEPES, 0.38 mM Na2HPO4, pH 7.4) and appropriate solvent when necessary (Jackson et al. 2021; Table S3). Stocks of HSA (1 mM) were prepared by reconstituting lyophilized stocks in HBS. Octanoic acid stocks were prepared at 10 mM concentration in HBS with 30% meOH. PFAS and fatty acid stock solutions were serially diluted to achieve necessary working stock concentrations for DSF concentration-response analysis.Differential scanning fluorimetryDSF experiments were performed in sealed optical 96-well reaction plates (MicroAmp Fast, Applied Biosystems), with a final volume of 20 μl reaction mix as previously described (Jackson et al. 2021; Starnes et al. 2024). A minimum of 2 independent plates were run for each protein–ligand combination, with 3 replicates per sample/concentration on each plate. Controls on each plate included a no protein control, solvent-matched vehicle control (no ligand), and 3 concentrations of octanoic acid (1, 2, 3 mM) and/or PFHxA (0.3, 1, 3 mM) as positive controls. Each well contained a final concentration of 0.13 mM HSA, which was the protein concentration yielding optimal signal-to-noise ratio (Jackson et al. 2021).DSF data analysisRaw data from the DSF assay, reported in relative fluorescent light units, were exported as Microsoft Excel files and Applied Biosystems Real-Time PCR experiment files. The Protein Thermal Shift software (v1.4, Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA) was used to analyze raw data, and TM values were determined using the Boltzmann method (Vivoli et al. 2014). Statistical analyses were performed using Prism (v.10, GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA). Albumin TM is defined as the temperature at which the maximum change in fluorescence was observed (Jackson et al. 2021). Concentration–response curves for all test chemicals were generated in Prism and smoothed using the Savitzky and Golay method (Savitzky and Golay 1964). As previously described, a single-site ligand binding model was used to calculate dissociation constants (Vivoli et al. 2014; Jackson et al. 2021).Machine learning model developmentMachine learning models were trained using DSF-calculated association constant values (Ka) for each PFAS congener, as well as 1D, 2D, and 3D chemical descriptors (Cherkasov et al. 2014). Saagar, ToxPrints, and OPEn structure–activity/property Relationship App (OPERA) chemical descriptors were used to comprehensively represent the chemical structures of all analyzed fatty acids and PFAS (Yang et al. 2015; Mansouri et al. 2018; Sedykh et al. 2021). Structural information (SMILES strings), ToxPrints, and OPERA features for each chemical were retrieved from the EPA’s CompTox Chemistry Dashboard, utilizing CAS registry numbers for chemical identification (Williams et al. 2017). Models were trained using an initial training set of 57 chemicals, including PFAS (n = 54) and fatty acids (n = 3). One trifluoro-crotonic acid PFAS congener (CAS 71027-02-6) from our experimental dataset was removed from the machine learning training set, due to a lack of available molecular descriptor data for the compound. HSA–PFAS binding predictions were calculated using a 2-phase machine learning approach (Fig. 1). In phase 1, we utilized python and sklearn within a custom singularity container to predict PFAS–HSA binding using a binary (yes/no) classification approach with all experimental data (Pedregosa et al. 2011; Eibe et al. 2016; Kurtzer et al. 2017). Saagar (n = 834), Toxprints (n = 729), and OPERA (n = 12) chemical descriptors were generated for each of the PFAS congeners (Sedykh et al. 2021). To optimize data dimensionality, invariant features were removed, and to avoid data leakage all features in the training set were min–max scaled between 0 and 1. Min–max scaling was implemented over a robust scaling approach, due to the small size and zero-inflated nature of the dataset. This resulted in 255 features used for training our classification (binary yes/no prediction) and regression (quantitative Ka prediction) algorithms. In the initial phase of modeling, we tested 7 distinct machine learning classification algorithms, each trained through a rigorous 3-by-3-fold cross-validation process: Logistic Regression (LogReg), K Nearest-Neighbors (KNN), XGBoost (XGB), SVM, Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), and Recursive Partitioning (RP). This cross-validation approach was chosen to ensure sufficient representation of each binding class for modeling. The phase one 3-by-3-fold cross-validation was followed by the evaluation of algorithm performance using Cohen’s Kappa and AUROC statistics (Ben-David 2008).Fig. 1.Schematic of machine learning methods development.Following initial model training and evaluation, we constructed binary classification models utilizing the top 3 performing models across both AUROC and Kappa statistic, which included LogReg, MLP, and RF. The performance of these models was then evaluated using data from a 10-chemical test set held out from training. Additionally, we evaluated the performance of 4 consensus models, integrating predictions by LogReg, MLP, and RF models, to determine whether an ensemble approach would improve binary classifier performance. Final predictive models were trained using the algorithm with the highest AUROC and Kappa statistic.As a negative control for our machine learning classification models, we also performed permutation testing to construct 1,000 label-shuffled test datasets for analysis using our LogReg model (Ojala and Garriga 2009). Evaluation of the predictive performance of machine learning algorithms trained on random label-shuffled data is used to confirm that the predictive capability of final models is not likely to be dictated by random chance.Any PFAS chemical predicted to be bound by HSA in the binary classification models was utilized in the second phase of modeling, for quantitative binding predictions with regression models. For the regression modeling, we used a python and sklearn custom singularity container to make quantitative predictions of Ka values associated with HSA binding for each PFAS congener, using Saagar, Toxprints, and OPERA features. For these quantitative models, we utilized 9 regression algorithms to predict Ka values: SVM, RF, Ridge Regression, RP, Gradient Boosted Regression, MLP, Huber Regression, XGB, and Linear Regression (Berthold et al. 2008; Eibe et al. 2016). Regression modeling also used a rigorous 3-by-3-fold cross-validation, and performance was evaluated using RMSE and R2 values. The algorithm with the lowest RMSE was used to train the final predictive models.Outlier detection and determination of applicability domainsWe employed KNN and Isolation Forest methods in a conservative consensus approach to identify outliers in our DSF albumin–PFAS binding dataset and to determine the applicability domains of our models. KNN is a simple yet effective algorithm for outlier detection. It operates by measuring the distance between data points and a specified number, “k,” of their nearest neighbors, set at 10 (Zhang 2016). If a data point (i.e. PFAS congener) has significantly different neighbors compared with the majority, it is considered an outlier. The Isolation Forest algorithm is a decision tree-based approach designed to detect outliers with a contamination level, or proportion of outliers in the dataset, set at 0.025. Isolation Forest constructs a random forest (RF) of isolation trees, in which each tree isolates individual data points through RP of the feature space (Liu et al. 2008). The algorithm measures how quickly a data point can be isolated (i.e. separated from other points) within the forest, and outliers were identified as points that require fewer splits to isolate, indicating significant deviation from the majority of data points. PFAS congeners were deemed outliers and removed from analyses if there was a consensus of outlier identification between KNN and Isolation Forest methods for that congener.Starting with an initial training set of 57 fatty acids and PFAS, KNN analysis identified 7 chemicals as outliers: octanoic acid (CAS 124-07-2), decanoic acid (CAS 334-48-5), hexadecanoic acid (CAS 57-10-3), 3h-heptafluoro-2,2,4,4-tetrahydroxypentane (CAS 77953-71-0), EtFOSA (CAS 4151-50-2), EtFOSE (CAS 1691-99-2), and 4,4,4-trifluoro-3-(trifluoromethyl) crotonic acid (CAS 1763-28-6). Isolation forest analysis identified only 2 chemicals in the training set as outliers, hexadecanoic acid and EtFOSE. Using a consensus between the outlier detection models, hexadecanoic acid and EtFOSE were excluded from the training dataset. The same outlier detection methods were independently applied to our test set of 10 chemicals. In the test set, KNN identified ethyl trifluoromethanesulfonate (CAS 425-75-2) and 1,1,2,3,3,3-hexafluoropropyl 1H,1H-heptafluorobutyl ether (CAS 1184-97-0) as out of domain, but isolation forest identified all chemicals as in domain. Therefore, all chemicals were retained in the test set.Predicting binding of chemicals in the EPAPFASINV listThe EPAPFASINV list was retrieved from the EPA CompTox Dashboard which contains a curated inventory of 430 PFAS structures (https://comptox.epa.gov/dashboard/chemical-lists/epapfasinv). Any chemical from the list that was missing data for any of our 255 features was removed from further evaluation (n = 4). All chemicals in the EPAPFASINV list that were also found in our training and test sets, identified using DTXSID chemical identifiers, were removed (n = 50). The PCA plot shows a comparison of physical and chemical properties between our DSF-derived PFAS–HSA binding dataset (n = 65) and the EPAPFASINV list (n = 426).DiscussionTo the best of our knowledge, the binding affinities published in this study, quantifying relative HSA binding to 65 PFAS from 20 chemical classes, represent the largest experimentally generated HSA–PFAS binding affinity dataset curated under the same experimental conditions to date, and include the first reported protein binding data for many of the PFAS congeners herein. By combining these in vitro binding data with machine learning approaches, we have developed a stepwise analysis pipeline for the prediction of HSA binding affinities, capable of making binding predictions for hundreds of PFAS with no currently published data describing their basic physiochemical properties.Albumin binding is an important factor in understanding the bioaccumulative potential of different PFAS congeners, and these HSA–PFAS binding data fill major data gaps in pharmacokinetic prediction (Hall and Peng 2020; Cheng et al. 2021; Jackson et al. 2021). Although computational methods, such as PBPK modeling, hold the potential to elucidate key characteristics of PFAS toxicokinetics, a paucity of experimental protein binding data for most PFAS congeners remains a challenge in training accurate and robust predictive models (Fàbrega et al. 2014; Cheng and Ng 2017; Chou and Lin 2019, 2021; Cheng et al. 2021; Deepika et al. 2021). The combined experimental and predictive methods described in this study have provided HSA relative binding affinities for 441 different PFAS structures, and represent a promising new framework for protein–PFAS binding analysis.The DSF assay is an in vitro approach with unique advantages for measuring HSA–PFAS interactions, and is capable of making rapid and accurate binding affinity predictions across a wide variety of PFAS chemical structures. In addition to the DSF assay being relatively rapid, inexpensive, and scalable to high-throughput formats, another benefit of this method is that the ΔTm values calculated in the assay provide additional information regarding the thermodynamics of HSA–PFAS binding. Although most binding interactions are stabilizing for the protein, resulting in increases in ΔTm values, some binding interactions can be destabilizing, as was observed in HSA binding of 1,1,2,3,3,3-hexafluoropropyl 1H,1H-heptafluorobutyl ether (CAS 1184-97-0; Cimmperman et al. 2008). This unique destabilizing interaction suggests the potential for a different mode of binding for this PFAS congener, and serves as an example of the various different binding interactions that can occur with diverse PFAS chemical structures. Additionally, the magnitude of the ΔTm value is indicative of the thermodynamics of the binding relationship, in which entropically driven interactions lead to greater ΔTm values, often seen in interactions dominated by hydrophobic interactions, whereas smaller changes in Tm are indicative of enthalpically driven binding (Holdgate and Ward 2005; Niesen et al. 2007). Taken together, all of these benefits make DSF a valuable tool in the analysis of HSA–PFAS binding, by enabling the rapid screening of HSA binding for thousands of diverse PFAS, as well as providing quantitative binding values and additional insights into the thermodynamics of binding.In our previous DSF studies, we found that serum albumin from multiple mammalian species bound all tested PFAS congeners containing a carboxylic or sulfonic headgroup, but did not bind the FTOH congeners, supporting the known importance of a charged functional headgroup in albumin binding (Jackson et al. 2021; Starnes et al. 2024). That observation held true in these analyses, in which all PFAS congeners containing terminal carboxylic (-COOH) or sulfonic acid (-SO3H) moieties were bound by HSA. In addition, PFAS from several chemical classes lacking charged functional groups were not bound by serum albumin, including the FTOH, fluorotelomer acrylate and methacrylate, polyfluoroalkyl tetrol, and hydrofluoroolefin congeners. In contrast, our previous study also revealed that serum albumin does bind to 1,1,1,2,2,3,3-heptafluoro-3-(1,2,2,2-tetrafluoroethoxy)propane, or E1, another congener lacking a charged functional group (Jackson et al. 2021). The binding of HSA to E1 demonstrates that while the charged functional group plays an important role in HSA–PFAS binding, it is just one of many factors influencing HSA–PFAS binding. Further, serum albumin binding can occur at several different sites on the protein, and binding at different albumin sites is likely influenced by different variables (Chen and Guo 2009; Salvalaglio et al. 2010; Jackson et al. 2021; Maso et al. 2021; Moro et al. 2022). Therefore, the binding of HSA to E1 is suggestive of a different mode of binding for this short-chain polyfluoroalkyl ether congener compared with the carboxylic and sulfonic acid containing congeners, and is consistent with the nonpolar binding mechanism of the fluorinated ether anesthetic compound, enflurane (Liu and Eckenhoff 2005; Jackson et al. 2021).In the current study, HSA bound to only a fraction of the tested congeners in 7 PFAS chemical classes analyzed: the polyfluoroalkyl amines, perfluoroalkyl amides, n-alkyl FASA and FASEs, per- and polyfluoroalkyl trifluoromethane-sulfonates, per- and polyfluoroalkyl methyl esters, per- and polyfluoroalkyl diols, and trifluoro-crotonic acids and crotonates. The fact that PFAS congeners sharing the same functional headgroup are not always similarly bound by HSA demonstrates the importance of other chemical features (beyond functional group) in HSA–PFAS binding. Thus, we utilized interpretable machine learning methods to interrogate which PFAS chemical features influenced HSA binding.Of the 7 binary (yes/no) classification models evaluated in our study, the LogReg classifier exhibited the best overall performance, considering its AUROC and Kappa statistics demonstrated excellent predictive performance. In addition to evaluating all 7 models individually, we utilized an ensemble approach to evaluate consensus predictions across the 3 diverse machine learning methodologies (LogReg, MLP, and RF) that exhibited the best performance. By integrating the predictions generated by these 3 models, we sought to derive a more robust predictive model, as ensemble approaches have the potential to enhance the reliability of predictions, as well as offer interpretable insights into the underlying mechanisms governing chemical toxicity. Ultimately, however, the combinatoric approaches did not outperform the LogReg approach, and LogReg was deemed the best-performing binary classifier model.Logistic regression is an advantageous modeling approach due to the fact that it is computationally cheap to train, and allows the clear interpretability of results, facilitating investigation into the key molecular and physiochemical features of PFAS that drive HSA binding (Hosmer et al. 2013). In our machine learning models, Saagar descriptors were included to provide a 3D visualization of chemicals, capturing spatial arrangements critical for understanding molecular interactions (Cherkasov et al. 2014; Sedykh et al. 2021). ToxPrints were included to offer a 2D fingerprinting approach, summarizing chemical features indicative of toxicity (Yang et al. 2015). OPERA descriptors encompass a wide range of 1D and 2D features, including structural, electronic, and thermodynamic properties, which are used to capture key aspects of a molecule’s behavior and interactions within biological systems (Mansouri et al. 2018).Several of the important molecular features enhancing or hindering HSA–PFAS binding identified by our LogReg classification model align well with findings from previous studies. Here, the presence of a carboxylic acid group (-COOH), and to a lesser extent the presence of a sulfonic acid group (-SO3H), were found to be among the most important features enhancing PFAS binding. Although studies evaluating protein binding to PFAS with a wide range of functional groups are limited, these findings parallel results reported from previous studies. A study conducted by Li et al. (2021) evaluated HSA interactions with 32 PFAS in aqueous film-forming foams, and calculated pseudo-bioconcentration factors (BCFpseudo) as a readout of the relative bioaccumulative potentials of the congeners. These researchers found that PFAS containing carboxylic or sulfonic acid headgroups had the highest BCFpseudo values, indicating increased bioaccumulative potential, consistent with our modeling results (Li et al. 2021). Another study conducted by Smeltz et al. (2023) investigated plasma protein binding of 71 PFAS congeners, reporting that PFCAs and perfluoroalkanoyl chlorides with 6 to 10 carbons had the highest fraction bound in the blood plasma, across 9 different chemical classes of PFAS. Our models also indicate that linear alkanes (-CH2) are features that hinder albumin binding to PFAS. This finding also corresponds with results reported by Smeltz et al. (2023), which found decreased plasma protein binding of the fluorotelomer carboxylic acids, which contain linear alkanes, compared with PFCAs with equivalent carbon chain lengths.The support vector regression exhibited the best performance of the 9 machine learning regression models evaluated for generating quantitative relative binding affinity (Ka) predictions. Predictive accuracy of the final support vector regression model represents a considerable stride in the field, as HSA binding affinity predictions for any single PFAS congener can range several orders of magnitude across studies, as a result of differences in methodologies and experimental conditions (Klevens and Ellenbogen 1954; Han et al. 2003; Chen and Guo 2009; Wu et al. 2009, 2024; Bischel et al. 2010; MacManus-Spencer et al. 2010; Allendorf et al. 2019; Chen et al. 2020; Jackson et al. 2021; Li et al. 2021; Alesio et al. 2022; Alesio and Bothun 2024; Peng et al. 2024). Major differences in binding affinity estimates due to these methodological differences present interpretive challenges preventing the direct comparison of quantitative values across studies. Therefore, our predictive models boast the unique advantage of generating directly comparable HSA–PFAS relative binding affinity values, for hundreds of PFAS congeners completely lacking in physiochemical data, as a result of model training with relative binding affinities generated with the same in vitro DSF approach.In this study, KNN and Isolation Forest were used as complementary approaches to identify PFAS congeners that were out of the applicability domain of our models. KNN is particularly useful when dealing with high-dimensional data, where traditional statistical methods may struggle. However, KNN’s performance depends on choosing an appropriate value for k and defining a suitable distance metric (Zhang 2016). Additionally, KNN assumes that the data distribution is uniform across the feature space. Isolation Forest is an advantageous method because it does not rely on assumptions about data distribution or require extensive parameter tuning. Although KNN excels at local outlier detection, Isolation Forest provides a global perspective by considering the entire dataset’s structure. These methods were used in combination to enhance outlier detection accuracy and improve model robustness.All chemicals in the EPAPFASINV list were identified to be within the applicability domain of our models, highlighting our models’ capacity to make reliable binding predictions across a wide variety of congeners in the PFAS chemical space. To interrogate potential areas of improvement for our models, we investigated the chemicals in which binary binding predictions were close to the threshold cutoff value, an optimized value determined through cross-validation that is used to discriminate between PFAS bound by HSA (yes) or not bound by HSA (no), calculated using the Youden Index (Youden 1950). The most likely explanation for the increased uncertainty in binding predictions for these chemicals is that these structures are either underrepresented (alcohols, terminal alkenes) or not represented (pentameric and phenolic rings, nonfluorine halogens) in the experimental training dataset. Unsurprisingly, a limitation of our modeling lies in the decreased predictive confidence for PFAS congeners most dissimilar from those represented in the training data of the model. Further, the DSF assay is incompatible with particularly large and bulky PFAS congeners due to solubility constraints, which may cause limitations in the modeling of similar congeners. Although our experimental dataset contains a large number of binding affinities compared with other studies in the field, this dataset is still relatively small for utilization in machine learning analysis. However, the structural similarity of the chemicals in our experimental and predictive datasets, in which all chemicals are PFAS, allows for improved performance of the machine learning models despite the relatively small dataset.In this study, we successfully generated directly comparable HSA–PFAS binding data for 441 PFAS chemical structures, encompassing experimentally and computationally derived values. These binding data address a major data gap in the field and can be utilized to improve the predictive capacity of computational modeling approaches, including PBPK modeling, aimed at understanding PFAS behavior, fate, and toxicity in biota. Our machine learning classification model has demonstrated excellent predictive performance, allowing us to confidently categorize PFAS as albumin binders or nonbinders. Although the quantitative regression modeling is not perfect, the final regression model built in this study provides informative estimates of HSA–PFAS binding affinities across structurally diverse PFAS, which will be valuable in the prioritization of PFAS for toxicological studies and risk evaluation. Prioritization should focus on PFAS congeners with particularly high albumin binding affinities, as these are likely to be very bioaccumulative compounds, as well as PFAS with particularly low binding affinities, which may be more bioavailable or reactive in the body. Further, this methodological design is amenable to the study of many other soluble proteins of interest, and represents a rapid and informative alternative framework to in vivo PFAS toxicokinetic experimentation, in line with the National Academy of the Sciences goals for toxicology in the 21st century, and aligned goals of the National Toxicology Program, for establishing new approach methodologies for chemical characterization and the evaluation of chemical safety (National Research Council et al. 2007; ICCVAM 2018).Supplementary Materialkfae124_Supplementary_DataAcknowledgmentsWe thank Dr Jeffrey Yoder for providing the PFMOAA used for assessment in our study.Contributor InformationHannah M Starnes,
