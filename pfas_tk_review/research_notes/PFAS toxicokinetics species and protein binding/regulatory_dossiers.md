## Q1. California OEHHA PFOA/PFOS Public Health Goal — half-life, Vd, clearance, primary studies, and treatment of the short-half-life literature

### Takeaway

OEHHA's 2024 PHG document is the only dossier reviewed here that **abandons the `CL = Vd × ln2/T½` construction entirely for PFOA** and instead regresses measured human intake against measured human serum across eight exposure studies, adopting the regression slope directly as the serum-to-dose clearance factor: **PFOA 2.8 × 10⁻⁴ L/kg-day (0.28 mL/kg-day)** and **PFOS 3.9 × 10⁻⁴ L/kg-day (0.39 mL/kg-day)**. These are ~2.0× and ~4.8× the corresponding US EPA (2016) values. OEHHA's explicit rationale is that Vd is the weakest link in the chain, so a method that bypasses it is preferred. It dismissed the short-half-life literature (Dourson 2019, Zhang 2013b) on methodological grounds, and did **not** engage with Chiu 2022, Campbell 2022, or Dourson 2021 at all.

### Cited Findings — the adopted values

- **Adopted PFOA clearance factor: 2.8 × 10⁻⁴ L/kg-day.** "A human clearance factor of 2.8 × 10⁻⁴ L/kg-day was developed for PFOA and 3.9 × 10⁻⁴ L/kg-day for PFOS. This improves the approach used previously (OEHHA, 2019)." — [OEHHA 2024, *Public Health Goals for PFOA and PFOS in Drinking Water*, Executive Summary p. 9](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **How the PFOA value was derived (Section 4.9, "PFOA CL determination from exposure studies", p. 60–61).** Eight exposure studies with matched intake (ng/kg-day) and serum (ng/mL) were pooled and fitted to a linear regression; "The slope of the regression line in this graph is in fact clearance, which equals 0.28 ml/kg-day or 2.8x10⁻⁴ L/kg-day." The eight studies (Table 4.9.1, p. 60) are Fromme 2007 (Bavarian diet), Trudel 2008 (North America/Europe all routes), Brede 2010 (Arnsberg drinking water, 11.4 ng/kg-day → 32.8 ng/mL), Thompson 2010/Frisbee 2009 (C8 Lubeck 9 → 92.4; C8 Little Hocking 69 → 228), Haug 2011a (Norway all routes), Lorber & Egeghy 2011 (USA all routes), Vestergren 2012 (Swedish diet). **All exposure assessments are assumed to be at steady state.** This is "option A-3" in the summary table. — [OEHHA 2024, Table 4.9.1 p. 60, Figure 4.9.1 p. 61, Table 4.9.3 p. 64](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **How the PFOS value was derived (Section 4.9, p. 62).** The regression approach was *not* usable for PFOS because "other than the scenario of increased intake from drinking water in Ronneby, Sweden (Li et al., 2018c), the rest of the available exposure assessments were at the lower range, rendering the regression approach non-informative." Instead a single-point steady-state calculation: `CL = Cw × 1.4 L/d × fa / (Cserum × BW) = (8,000 × 1.4 × 0.9)/(372 × 70) = 0.39 ml/kg-day = 3.9×10⁻⁴ L/kg-day`, where Cw = 8,000 ng/L PFOS in Ronneby outgoing water (2013), Cserum = 372 ng/mL median in residents exposed ≥ 10 years (N = 1,176), water intake 1.4 L/day, absorption efficiency fa = 0.9, BW = 70 kg. Note this formula "obviat[es] the need for a T½ estimate." — [OEHHA 2024, Section 4.9 p. 62](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **Why the ≥10-year Ronneby subgroup was chosen.** 64.6% of that subgroup were 19–65 years ("the population subgroup likely to be described by the steady state model") and only 24.3% were 66–94 years. The ≥29-year subgroup was rejected because 45.7% were 66–94 years, which "would violate the steady state assumption," with OEHHA attributing the rising serum levels in the elderly to "declining kidney function with age, and as a result, decreased PFAS elimination." — [OEHHA 2024, Section 4.9 p. 62](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **Adopted half-lives: PFOA 2.7 years (Li 2017e; Li 2018c), PFOS 3.4 years (Li 2018c).** For PFOA: "the Li et al. (2017e) study produced a slightly higher T½ value of 2.7 years, which appears to be the best available estimate for PFOA T½ to date... The same robust analytical method applied to PFOA TK data from Ronneby in Sweden, also produced a PFOA T½ estimate of 2.7 years." For PFOS: "Using rigorous analysis and high and discontinued environmental exposure data, Li et al. (2018c) estimated PFOS T½ at 3.4 years. This value is consistent with most recent reports (Table 4.7.2), and is chosen by OEHHA as the best estimate based on available evidence." **Critically, these half-lives are used only for the Vd calculations (options A-4, S-2, S-3), not for the adopted clearance factors** — Table 4.9.3 footnote a: "Used for Vd calculation only." — [OEHHA 2024, Section 4.7 pp. 50 and 52; Table 4.9.3 p. 64](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **Vd values OEHHA derived and the ranking it assigned them (Table 4.9.3, p. 64).** PFOA: option A-1 (US EPA 2016) T½ 2.3 y / Vd 170 mL/kg / CL 1.4 × 10⁻⁴ L/kg-day; option A-2 (OEHHA update of A-1 using Frisbee 2009 serum instead of Emmett 2006) T½ 2.3 y / **Vd 225 mL/kg** / CL 1.9 × 10⁻⁴; option **A-3 (adopted)** no Vd, no T½, CL 2.8 × 10⁻⁴; option A-4 (renal + biliary clearance upper limit) T½ 2.7 y / Vd ≤ 250 mL/kg / CL ≤ 1.75 × 10⁻⁴. PFOS: option S-1 (US EPA 2016d) T½ 5.4 y / Vd 230 mL/kg / CL 0.81 × 10⁻⁴; option **S-2 (adopted)** T½ 3.4 y / Vd 1,160 mL/kg / CL 3.9 × 10⁻⁴; option S-3 (biliary) T½ 3.4 y / Vd ≤ 620 mL/kg / CL ≤ 3.2 × 10⁻⁴. — [OEHHA 2024, Table 4.9.3 p. 64](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **The PFOA Vd = 225 mL/kg recalculation in full (Section 4.8, p. 58).** `Vd = Cw × 1.4 L/d × fa × T½ / (Cserum × ln2 × BW)`. Little Hocking: `(3,550 × 1.4 × 0.91 × 839.5)/(227.58 × ln2 × 71.8) = 335 mL/kg`. Lubeck: `(500 × 1.4 × 0.91 × 839.5)/(92.36 × ln2 × 71.8) = 116 mL/kg`. Average 225 mL/kg. Assumptions: fa = 0.91, T½ = 2.3 y (839.5 d), BW = 71.8 kg, 1.4 L/day water. OEHHA substituted **Frisbee 2009** C8 Science Panel serum (227.58 ng/mL Little Hocking, 92.36 ng/mL Lubeck) for **Emmett 2006** (448 and 68 ng/mL), reasoning that Emmett's household-selection design "may have introduced unaccounted for bias toward higher than average levels in plasma," whereas "the C8 Panel studies... aimed for full population coverage and randomization." — [OEHHA 2024, Section 4.8 pp. 57–58](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **PFOS Vd = 693 mL/kg from Ronneby** by the same steady-state formula (8,000 ng/L, 372 ng/mL, fa = 0.9, T½ = 3.4 y = 1,241 d, BW = 70 kg). OEHHA comments only that the PFOS value being higher than PFOA's "can be due to experimental uncertainty or true chemical differences." — [OEHHA 2024, Section 4.8 p. 59](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **Biliary/enterohepatic alternative (options A-4, S-3).** `CL = CLR + (1 − fb) × (Cbile/Cs) × Bile_flux` and `Vd = (T½/ln2) × CL`. Inputs: bile flux 5 mL/kg-day (Davies & Morris 1993); PFOA bile:serum 0.23 (average of 0.21 Harada 2007b and 0.25 Fujii 2015) → CL ≤ 1.75 × 10⁻⁴ L/kg-day, Vd ≤ 248 mL/kg; PFOS bile:serum 0.6 (N = 4 patients, Harada 2007b) → CL ≤ 3.16 × 10⁻⁴ L/kg-day, Vd ≤ 616 mL/kg. OEHHA notes "The main limitations include the reliability of human CLR and biliary clearance studies." — [OEHHA 2024, Section 4.9 pp. 59–60, 63](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **Human renal clearance geometric means, and the Zhang 2013b exclusion (Table 4.5.1, p. 43).** "The only exception was the PFOA clearance value from the Zhang et al. (2013b) study, which at 0.79 ml/kg-day was higher than the rest of the values, with the next highest value for PFOA clearance at 0.09 ml/kg-day. **Considering it an outlier**, the geometric mean of the remaining six studies for PFOA renal clearance was 0.059 ml/kg-day... For PFOS, the geometric mean of six studies was 0.016 ml/kg-day." All seven CLR studies were conducted in Asia (China, Japan). — [OEHHA 2024, Section 4.5 p. 42–43 and Table 4.5.1 p. 43](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **Why OEHHA rejected the high monkey Vd values (Section 4.9, p. 63).** "PFOA Vd estimates from chronic monkey studies (3,300 ml/kg) are much higher and may not be accurately applied to humans because of experimental limitations or species differences, such as decreased absorption efficiency in chronic gavage experiments in monkeys and inability to account for effects of biliary secretion/enterohepatic circulation of PFOA and extrapolate across species. Utilizing Vd from animal studies would introduce additional uncertainty in the assessment by relying on animal-to-human extrapolation of PK parameters. In this case, OEHHA believes that using human data would be preferable and result in the least uncertainty; in addition, **use of human data allows direct calculation of clearance, bypassing the separate steps of Vd and T½ determination**." — [OEHHA 2024, Section 4.9 p. 63](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **Other exposure parameters feeding the PHG.** Lifetime-weighted average drinking water intake 0.053 L/kg-day (OEHHA's own 2012 peer-reviewed California rates, explicitly retained in preference to US EPA's 2019 updated rates "due to a number of factors such as greater numbers of jobs performed in outdoor settings... and California's hot summer climate"); relative source contribution default 20%. — [OEHHA 2024, Executive Summary pp. 10–11](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **Resulting PHGs** (for context): PFOA 0.007 ppt and PFOS 1 ppt (cancer-based); noncancer health-protective concentrations 3 ppt (PFOA, human liver damage) and 2 ppt (PFOS, total cholesterol in humans). — [OEHHA press release, 2024](https://oehha.ca.gov/water/public-health-goal-press-release-water/california-adopts-health-protective-goals-forever-chemicals-drinking-water)

### Cited Findings — how OEHHA treated the short-half-life literature

- **Dourson et al. 2019 is in the tables with T½ < 0.7 years and is explicitly rejected.** Table 4.7.1 lists "Dourson et al. (2019) | Clinical trial | NA | <0.7 | Assumed steady state at given dose." The reasoning: "Dourson et al. (2019), based on previously available kinetic data from controlled dosing of human cancer patients at relatively high levels, assumed that the steady state for PFOA exposure would be achieved by approximately 36 weeks and estimated plasma concentration at that point. Then, using the steady state assumptions, they determined the resulting T½. In addition to a number of other issues, **this evaluation assumed and did not experimentally determine the time to steady state.** While the underlying kinetic data could be useful for further analysis, OEHHA does not consider the PFOA T½ reported in the Dourson et al. (2019) study to be of sufficient quality or representative of environmental exposure." — [OEHHA 2024, Section 4.7 p. 51 and Table 4.7.1 p. 51](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **Zhang 2013b is treated in two separate ways and criticised in both.** (a) Its PFOA *renal clearance* (0.79 mL/kg-day) is excluded as an outlier from the CLR geometric mean (Table 4.5.1 p. 43). (b) Its *half-life* estimates (PFOA 2.8 y; PFOS 22 y GM) are listed but discounted: "Studies that include considerations of urinary clearance (Zhang et al., 2013b; Fu et al., 2016) are based on the premise of the predominance of renal elimination, and moreover, depend on a chosen Vd value." — [OEHHA 2024, Section 4.7 p. 51; Tables 4.7.1/4.7.2 pp. 51–52](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **Convertino et al. 2018 (the clinical-trial dataset underlying the short-half-life argument) was explicitly excluded.** Peer reviewer comment: "I argue that data/estimates from that [study] ... [should not be used for] estimating PFOA half-life in healthy people." OEHHA response: "Convertino et al. (2018) should not be used for PFOA half-life derivation. The PFOA half-life estimate adopted in this document did not rely on Convertino et al. (2018) data." — [OEHHA 2024, *Responses to Peer Review and Public Comments*, Comment/Response 1, p. ~43 of the peer-review section](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosresponsestocomments040424.pdf)
- **A public commenter raised "Dourson et al. 2020" against OEHHA's single-serum-measurement assumption, and OEHHA rebutted on bias direction rather than on the half-life value.** Comment: "There continues to be considerable controversy regarding the distribution, calculation, and measurement biases associated with the serum elimination half-lives of PFOA in the human population (Dourson et al. 2020)... Clearly, if the serum elimination half-life ranges between 0.5 and 2.0 years, a PFOA measurement taken 8.8 years prior to the diagnosis could be 5+ half-lives." Response 26: "PFOA is not rapidly eliminated and is therefore stable over a substantial period of time. Even more importantly though, since PFOA was measured using the same methods in all of the participants... **the resulting bias from exposure misclassification is most likely towards the null** and not towards the associations seen in these studies. **This is true regardless of whether the half-life is 6-24 months or longer.**" — [OEHHA 2024, Responses to Comments, Response 26, p. 90](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosresponsestocomments040424.pdf)
- **The same argument is used for the choice of a long half-life generally.** Response 26 (first review draft): "all these half-life estimates are long (in the order of years) and choosing one or another would not make much difference in almost any epidemiologic study. Furthermore, if exposures (or serum levels) change over time, any resulting bias would most likely be towards finding no association." — [OEHHA 2024, Responses to Comments, Response 26](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosresponsestocomments040424.pdf)
- **The Seals 2011 long half-lives (8.5–10.1 y) are also discounted, in the opposite direction.** OEHHA argues that because US background PFOA "appeared to decline dramatically starting in the early 2000s," Seals' fixed background subtraction (5 or 15 ng/L) would flatten the regression: "it is impossible to determine how much the higher T½ values obtained for lower initial serum levels in this study were influenced by uncertainty in the level of background adjustment." The same logic is applied to the California Biomonitoring disappearance data (Kim 2020), which imply ~6 years for both PFOA and PFOS. — [OEHHA 2024, Section 4.7 pp. 50–53](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **Health Canada contrast, as OEHHA itself framed it.** A commenter argued OEHHA "significantly underestimates the human clearance rate." OEHHA replied: "To calculate human clearance (hCL) for PFOS, Health Canada (2018) used its estimated half-life and volume a distribution, a method which OEHHA considered and analyzed in great detail... The calculated hCL was 0.07 ml/kg-day, which is 5.5 times lower than the value chosen by OEHHA (3.9×10⁻⁴ L/kg-day). Thus, the Health Canada (2018) value is more conservative than that of OEHHA... The difference... is due to longer half-life and smaller volume of distribution estimates that were chosen for the hCL prior to the availability of the more recent literature used by OEHHA." OEHHA also notes neither it nor Health Canada used a **human** PBPK model, quoting Health Canada: "the human PBPK models that would be used to extrapolate from this serum concentration cannot be fully verified based on existing human pharmacokinetic data." — [OEHHA 2024, Responses to Comments, Response 30, p. 127](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosresponsestocomments040424.pdf)

### Inferences

- OEHHA's hierarchy of evidence for dose reconstruction is, in order: (1) direct human intake↔serum regression, (2) human single-site steady-state calculation, (3) human renal + biliary mass balance, (4) `Vd × ln2/T½` with a human Vd, and last (5) anything requiring an animal Vd. This is the inverse of the EPA approach, which sits at level (4) with a Vd that is itself a back-calculation.
- The PFOS Vd figures are internally inconsistent in the published document: Section 4.8 (p. 59) derives 693 mL/kg from Ronneby, while Table 4.9.3 (p. 64) lists 1,160 mL/kg for the same option S-2. 1,160 mL/kg is what you get by inverting the adopted CL of 3.9 × 10⁻⁴ L/kg-day with T½ = 3.4 y, so Table 4.9.3 appears to report an implied rather than an independently derived Vd. The document does not flag or reconcile the difference. **Report-writers should not cite a single OEHHA PFOS Vd without noting which number they mean.**
- OEHHA's rejection of the short-half-life literature rests on two distinct arguments that are worth separating: a *methodological* one (Dourson 2019 assumed rather than measured time-to-steady-state; Convertino 2018 was a high-dose cancer-patient trial) and an *epidemiological indifference* one (within the range 6–24 months to several years, exposure misclassification biases toward the null, so the choice does not change the dose-response conclusions). The second argument concedes that the half-life might be as short as 6 months without conceding anything about the PHG.

### Gaps

- **OEHHA does not cite Chiu et al. 2022, Campbell et al. 2022, or Dourson et al. 2021.** Grepping both the final PHG (38,696 lines) and the Responses to Comments (13,747 lines) returns no Campbell-authored PFAS PK paper other than Loccisano/Campbell PBPK models, no "Alliance for Risk Assessment"/ARA collaboration, and no Chiu 2022 — the only Chiu citations are Chiu et al. 2018 (high-throughput key characteristics), Smith et al. 2020, and Zahm et al. 2023. The short-half-life position reaches OEHHA only through **Dourson et al. 2019** (cited in the main document) and a commenter's reference to **"Dourson et al. 2020"** (Responses to Comments p. 90). I could not determine from these documents whether OEHHA was aware of the 2021/2022 publications; the final document's literature cut-off would plausibly have allowed them.
- The second public review draft (2022?) was not retrieved; only the 2021 first draft and the 2024 final. Whether the TK chapter changed between the first draft and the final is not established here.

## Q2. US EPA 2024 final PFOA and PFOS drinking water / MCLG toxicity assessments

### Takeaway

EPA retains the classical two-step `CL = Vd × ln2/T½` construction. It **updated the half-lives** (PFOA 3.8 → 2.7 y; PFOS 5.5 → 3.4 y, both from Li et al.) but **deliberately did not update the volumes of distribution**, leaving PFOA at 170 mL/kg and PFOS at 230 mL/kg — producing clearance factors of **0.120 mL/kg-day (PFOA)** and **0.128 mL/kg-day (PFOS)**. Neither assessment cites Chiu et al. 2022.

### Cited Findings

- **PFOA adopted parameters (Table 4-6, p. 4-35):** Volume of distribution 170 mL/kg (footnote b: Thompson et al. 2010a); half-life 2.7 yr (footnote c: Li et al. 2017d); clearance 0.120 mL/kg/d (footnote d: "Calculated from half-life and volume of distribution. Cl = Vd * ln(2)/t½"); cord serum:maternal serum 0.83; milk:serum partition coefficient 0.049. Original Verner et al. (2016) values for comparison: Vd 170, T½ 3.8 y, CL 0.085, cord ratio 0.79, milk:serum 0.058. — [US EPA 2024, *Final Human Health Toxicity Assessment for PFOA*, EPA-815R24006, Table 4-6 p. 4-35](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-05/final-human-health-toxicity-assessment-pfoa.pdf)
- **PFOS adopted parameters (Table 4-6, p. 4-33):** Vd 230 mL/kg (Thompson et al. 2010a); half-life 3.4 yr (Li et al. 2018b); clearance 0.128 mL/kg/d (calculated); cord:maternal 0.40; milk:serum 0.016. Original Verner values: Vd 230, T½ 5.5 y, CL 0.079. — [US EPA 2024, *Final Human Health Toxicity Assessment for PFOS*, EPA-815R24007, Table 4-6 p. 4-33](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-05/final-human-health-toxicity-assessment-pfos.pdf)
- **How the clearance factor is used.** HEDs are computed as the serum POD multiplied by the clearance: the PFOS document repeats, across every endpoint derivation, "the POD multiplied by the selected clearance value (0.128 mL/kg/day; calculated from half-life and volume of distribution; Cl = Vd * ln(2)/t½)"; the PFOA document likewise uses 0.120 mL/kg/day. — [US EPA 2024 PFOS, Section 5 HED derivations](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-05/final-human-health-toxicity-assessment-pfos.pdf); [US EPA 2024 PFOA, Section 5](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-05/final-human-health-toxicity-assessment-pfoa.pdf)
- **Half-life selection criteria, stated verbatim.** "EPA selected a reported half-life value from an exposure to a study population that is demographically representative of the general population, with a clear decrease in exposure at a known time, with a high number of participants and a long follow-up time. Based on these criteria, a half-life of 2.7 years was determined for PFOA as reported in Li et al. (2018c; 2017d). This value comes from a large population (n = 455) who originally had contaminated drinking water for which the study documents the decrease in exposure levels after the installation of filtration with an average final serum sample taken 3.9 years after the beginning of water filtration." — [US EPA 2024 PFOA, p. 4-35](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-05/final-human-health-toxicity-assessment-pfoa.pdf)
- **The reason EPA kept Vd at 170 mL/kg despite changing T½ — an explicit, unusual decision.** "The updated value for human Vd of PFOA, 170 mL/kg, was sourced from Thompson et al. (2010a) who used a one-compartment PK model. This calculation involves several assumptions: that the participants' serum concentrations are at steady-state, their exposure can be estimated from the drinking water concentration in their community, there is 91% bioavailability for exposure from drinking water, and the half-life of PFAS is 2.3 years, which comes from the report of Bartell et al. (2010). **EPA considered updating this parameter to 200 mL/kg, which is the value that would be calculated using the EPA chosen half-life value of 2.7 years. However, the value of 2.3 years was calculated under very similar conditions as the other data in the Thompson et al. (2010a) population and 2.3 years may better reflect the clearance rate in that specific population at that time.**" — [US EPA 2024 PFOA, p. 4-36](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-05/final-human-health-toxicity-assessment-pfoa.pdf)
- **EPA's justification for importing Vd across species.** "Vd is a parameter that is relatively easily obtained from an analysis of PK data from controlled experimental studies, as it is related to the peak concentration observed after dosing and **is expected to be similar between human and nonhuman primates (Mordenti et al., 1991). For comparison, the optimized Vd for PFOA from oral dosing in monkeys was 140 mL/kg (Andersen et al., 2006).**" — [US EPA 2024 PFOA, p. 4-36](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-05/final-human-health-toxicity-assessment-pfoa.pdf)
- **Corroboration EPA accepted for Vd.** "Another group has approached the calculation of Vd by taking the average of reported animal and human values and reported values of 200 mL/kg for PFOA (Gomis et al., 2017)... The resulting average value shows that the value from Thompson et al. (2010a) is reasonable; **EPA selected the Thompson et al. (2010a) result based on the fact that it is the only value derived from human data that EPA considers to be reliable for risk estimation in the general population.**" EPA states the literature range as 170–200 mL/kg ("In humans, the volume of distribution (Vd) for PFOA has been assigned values between 170 and 200 mL/kg"). — [US EPA 2024 PFOA, p. 4-36 and Section 3.3.1](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-05/final-human-health-toxicity-assessment-pfoa.pdf)
- **The PFOS Vd is a derived quantity, not a measured one, and EPA says so in its uncertainty section.** "Because the derivation of the Vd for PFOS relied on the value for PFOA, it is important to consider alternate values for Vd for PFOA. For PFOA, the Vd calculation depended on the half-life. Thompson et al. (2010a) used 2.3 years... **If EPA chosen half-life of 2.7 years was used instead, the Vd for PFOA would be 200 mL/kg, which results in a PFOS value of 271 mL/kg.** EPA did not update the Vd values based on the updated half-life because the value of 2.3 years was calculated based on the same data as the Vd... Gomis et al. (2017) also calculated Vd by taking the average of reported animal and human values and estimated values of 235 mL/kg for PFOS." — [US EPA 2024 PFOS, Section 5.6.2 p. 5-19](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-05/final-human-health-toxicity-assessment-pfos.pdf)
- **EPA's stated two-sided bias concern about high-exposure half-life studies.** "Ongoing exposure will result in a positive bias in observed half-life values if not considered (Russell et al., 2015)... However, a half-life value determined from a population with very high exposure may not be informative of the half-life in typical exposure scenarios **because of non-linearities in PK that may occur due to the saturation of PFAS-protein interactions. This will likely take the form of an under-estimation of the half-life that is relevant to lower levels... due to saturation of renal resorption and increased urinary clearance in the study population.**" — [US EPA 2024 PFOS, Section 5.6.2 p. 5-19](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-05/final-human-health-toxicity-assessment-pfos.pdf)
- **Animal PK parameters in the gestation/lactation model (both documents, Table 4-5).** PFOA: rat T½ 2.23 days (footnote c: "Average of male/female half-lives reported in Dzierlenga et al. (2019a), Kim et al. (2016), and Kemper et al. (2003)"), rat Vd 0.18 L/kg (Kim 2016; Dzierlenga 2019a); mouse T½ 18.5 days and Vd 0.2 L/kg (Fujii et al. 2020). PFOS: rat T½ 40 days, Vd 0.28 L/kg; mouse T½ 36.87 days, Vd 0.26 L/kg. ### Inferences

- EPA's choice to pair a 2.7-year half-life with a Vd that was itself computed assuming 2.3 years is internally inconsistent in a way that **lowers** the clearance factor and therefore **lowers** the HED (more stringent). EPA acknowledges the mismatch and quantifies the alternative (PFOA Vd 200 → PFOS 271 mL/kg), which would raise PFOA CL to ~0.141 mL/kg-day. The decision is defended on population-matching grounds, not on PK grounds.
- Because the PFOS Vd is 1.35 × the PFOA Vd, and the 1.35 factor comes from Andersen et al. (2006) *central-compartment* volumes, the entire EPA PFOS dose reconstruction inherits a parameter from a 20-year-old optimised monkey model. OEHHA attacked exactly this chain (see Q1): "Vdc values determined in this study were not representative of the Vd, but rather of volume of the central compartment."
- The ~2.3× gap between EPA's PFOA clearance (0.120 mL/kg-day) and OEHHA's (0.28 mL/kg-day), and the ~3.0× gap for PFOS (0.128 vs 0.39 mL/kg-day), is almost entirely a **Vd/method** disagreement, not a half-life disagreement — the two agencies agree on PFOA T½ (2.7 y) and PFOS T½ (3.4 y) and cite the same Li et al. studies.

The main documents repeatedly defer to that appendix for the value compilations.

## Q3. EFSA 2020 CONTAM opinion on the four PFAS (PFOA, PFNA, PFHxS, PFOS)

### Takeaway

EFSA did **not** adopt a clearance factor at all. It ran human PBPK models for PFOA and PFOS only — models parameterised with the *older* half-lives of **2.3 y (PFOA)** and **5.4 y (PFOS)** — and handled PFNA and PFHxS by **summing them into PFOA and PFOS respectively and reusing those two models**, on the grounds that PFNA and PFHxS contribute little to total exposure. EFSA named the half-life variability as a stated source of uncertainty in its own uncertainty table.

### Cited Findings

- **The two half-lives hard-wired into the PBPK models.** "The variation in reported half-lives for PFOS and PFOA is a source of uncertainty, since in the respective PBPK models **defined values of 5.4 and 2.3 years for PFOS and PFOA, respectively, were taken into account for developing the models.** Tissue/plasma partition coefficients for PFASs for all tissues in the model were calculated from tissue concentration data derived from ex[perimental studies]." — [EFSA CONTAM Panel 2020, EFSA Journal 18(9):6223, Section 3.6.4.3](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2020.6223)
- **PFNA and PFHxS have no PBK model and were lumped.** "Human PBPK models for PFOA and PFOS were used to estimate exposure to PFOA/PFNA and PFOS/PFHxS for women of 35 years (child-bearing age) leading to a defined serum level in mothers, and to simulate PFAS concentrations in the infant at 1 year of age after 12-months breastfeeding... **Due to the lack of PBPK models for PFHxS and PFNA, the PBPK modelling was based on summing PFOA and PFNA, as well as PFHxS and PFOS and applying the models for PFOA and PFOS, respectively.** Since in practice PFNA and PFHxS contribute little to the overall exposure, this will have a minor effect on the estimations." The median serum ratios used for the lumping were **0.015 for PFHxS/PFOS and 0.03 for PFOA/PFNA**. — [EFSA 2020, Section 3.6.4.3 and Appendix M](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2020.6223)
- **What the PBPK model was used for.** A BMDL₁₀ of 17.5 ng/mL for the sum of the four PFAS in 1-year-old children's serum was converted, "Using a PBPK model, and assuming 12 months of breastfeeding," to a **maternal intake of 0.63 ng/kg bw per day**, from which the **TWI of 4.4 ng/kg bw per week** was set. The 17.5 ng/mL was apportioned as PFOA 8.47, PFNA 0.30, PFHxS 1.06 and PFOS 7.67 ng/mL. **Equal weight-based potency was assumed for the four PFAS** "since derivation of relative potency factors was considered not possible." — [EFSA 2020, Abstract and Section 3.6.4.3](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2020.6223)
- **Model provenance.** The models are the EFSA CONTAM Panel's 2018 modifications of published human PFOA/PFOS PBPK models (Loccisano et al. lineage); "In the previous Opinion... Slight modifications were made by the EFSA CONTAM Panel in 2018." A cross-check against the Minnesota Department of Health (MDH) one-compartment toxicokinetic model gave similar PFOA infant serum predictions at 6 months and 1 year "despite parameter differences between the MDH model and PBPK model (e.g. body weight, volume of distribution (infant, adult), placenta transfer and maternal milk/serum ratio)." — [EFSA 2020, Appendix M, Sections M.2.5.1](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2020.6223)
- **PBPK modelling is flagged in EFSA's own uncertainty table as "+/–"** (i.e. it could bias the assessment in either direction). EFSA's forward-looking recommendation: "PBPK models for PFASs should be further optimised." — [EFSA 2020, Section 3.6.x uncertainty table and Section 4 Recommendations](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2020.6223)
- **EFSA's half-life compilation (Table 15) — human values, with the Panel's own best-estimate judgements.** Selected rows (full set in the CSV):
  - PFOA: 3.8 y (95% CI 3.1–4.4), Olsen et al. 2007, n = 24 M + 2 F; 2.3 y (2.1–2.4), Bartell et al. 2010, n = 200; 2.9 y (2.3–3.8) and 8.5 y (7.1–10.1), Seals et al. 2011 (high- and low-exposure water districts); 1.5 y GM (0.19–5.2) and 1.2 y GM (0.04–14), Zhang et al. 2013a; 1.7 y GM, Fu et al. 2016; 3.9 y, Worley et al. 2017. Total clearance 0.132 mL/kg-day (Harada 2005), 0.150 (Harada 2007 and Olsen 2007), 0.096 (Fujii 2015).
  - PFOS: 5.4 y (3.9–6.9), Olsen et al. 2007; 5.8 y GM (3.2–10) and 18 y GM (1.6–121), Zhang et al. 2013a; 4.7 y (4.2–5.3) M and 4.3 y (4.1–4.5) F, Wong et al. 2014/2015; 1.9 y GM, Fu et al. 2016; 3.3 y, Worley et al. 2017; 4.6 y (3.7–6.1) M and 3.1 y (2.7–3.7) F, Li et al. 2018a.   — [EFSA 2020, Table 15 (Section 3.3.1.2)](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2020.6223)

(2007)... and Li et al. (2018a)... Higher values were reported by Zhang et al. (2013a)... and Worley et al. (2017)... **According to these authors, the half-life estimated by Zhang et al. (2013a) on the basis of renal clearance should be viewed as upper limits due to the possibility that there might be other elimination routes than via the urine.** Other uncertainties in the results provided by Zhang et al. - **Quoted overall ranges.** "estimated half-lives for PFOA and PFOS (expressed as arithmetic means) vary from 2.3 to 8.5 years and from 3.1 to 7.4 years, respectively (EFSA, 2008; ATSDR, 2018; EFSA CONTAM Panel, 2018)." And: "Short-chain PFASs were found to have half-lives ranging from few days (PFBA) to approximately 1 month (PFBS, PFHxA)." — [EFSA 2020, Section 3.3.1.2](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2020.6223)

### Inferences

- EFSA's TWI therefore rests on PBPK models carrying 2.3 y / 5.4 y half-lives — i.e. **neither the current EPA nor the current OEHHA value for either chemical**, and in the PFOS case 1.6× the value both US agencies now use. Because a longer half-life implies more accumulation per unit intake, the EFSA PFOS parameterisation is the *more* conservative direction for a given serum target, whereas the PFOA value (2.3 y, shorter than 2.7 y) is less conservative.
- EFSA is the only dossier here whose dose reconstruction is a genuine **human PBPK simulation** of the maternal–infant system rather than a single multiplicative clearance factor. That buys it lactational and placental transfer realism but at the cost of hiding the half-life assumption inside model code.

## Q5. US EPA animal PFAS pharmacokinetic analysis (CPHEA) — how animal half-lives were fitted, and rat vs mouse

### Takeaway

EPA's CPHEA animal PK work is realised in two places: the **2025 Bayesian hierarchical cross-species analysis** (Dzierlenga et al., *Toxicol Appl Pharmacol*) and, operationally, in the **IRIS reviews for PFBA, PFHxA and PFHxS**, which fit species- and sex-specific clearances and then use the **ratio of human to animal clearance as the dosimetric adjustment factor (DAF)** in place of default BW^0.75 allometry. The explicit finding across both is that **default allometric scaling of clearance fails for PFAS, while BW-scaling of volume of distribution broadly holds.**

### Cited Findings

- **The 2025 Bayesian analysis — method and headline conclusions.** "we implemented a Bayesian inference hierarchical model to estimate PFAS PK parameters for multiple species (mice, rats, and non-human primates) using numerous single-dose animal studies. **Through an alternative parameterization of the one- and two-compartment models, this method improved parameter identifiability and allowed for the use of prior information on PFAS absorption rate, clearance, and volume of distribution.** Using reported time-course concentration data, we estimated sex-specific clearance, volume of distribution, and half-life across mice, rats, and non-human primates using a consistent modeling methodology for eight PFAS: PFHxA, PFHxS, PFNA, PFDA, PFBS, PFBA, PFOA, and PFOS. The resulting comparison to available human data demonstrated that **standard volume of distribution body-mass scaling (BW) for PFAS generally agrees with reported human values while standard assumptions for allometric scaling of clearance (BW) are not appropriate for most of the PFAS investigated in this study.** In addition, we demonstrated that there may be **considerable differences in clearance for PFAS in some species when comparing across different sexes and routes of exposure.**" — According to PubMed, Dzierlenga MW et al. (2025), *Toxicology and Applied Pharmacology*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.taap.2025.117336) (PMID 40210099; PMC12172007)
- **The mechanism the paper invokes for the failure of allometry.** "variations in ADME arise due to differences in **protein binding and renal and hepatobiliary clearance mechanisms.** In particular, **sex- and species-specific differences in active transporter abundance and PFAS binding affinity** challenge body weight-based extrapolation assumptions from animal models to human PK parameters. **Because these protein-dependent changes in ADME do not always scale with species body weight, classic allometric scaling assumptions can fail to account for species-specific transporter-mediated clearance.**" It also flags methodological heterogeneity as a problem in its own right: "study-dependent differences in pharmacokinetic modeling approaches and parameterization techniques can result in large differences among the PK parameters reported in the literature." — According to PubMed, Dzierlenga MW et al. (2025), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.taap.2025.117336)
- **IRIS PFHxA (2023): a hierarchical Bayesian fit on human occupational data, with the Vd borrowed from monkeys.** "The model is analyzed through hierarchical Bayesian analysis, with A and t_inf estimated independently for each individual technician although the technician-level ke is drawn from a population-level distribution. Note blood concentrations were measured only once a month and no other data on exposure is available... The resulting distribution of ke had a mean (90% confidence interval, CI) of **0.00252 (0.00136–0.00477) h⁻¹**. Using an average Vd of **0.7315 L/kg (731.5 mL/kg) for male and female monkeys from Chengelis et al. (2009a)**, the resulting mean for human clearance is CL = Vd·ke = **1.84 mL/kg-h**. Given the expected similarity of Vd across mammalian species, EPA considers the average value estimated for rats (0.33 L/kg) to be a reasonable lower bound for humans and the highest value reported by Chengelis et al. (2009a) for an individual (male) monkey (1.54 L/kg) to be a reasonable upper bound. Combining these with the 90% CI for ke... yields a possible range for human clearance of **0.45–7.35 mL/kg-h, a range of 16-fold**." — [US EPA 2023, *IRIS Toxicological Review of PFHxA and Related Salts*, EPA/635/R-23/027Fa, Section 3.1](https://iris.epa.gov/static/pdfs/0704tr.pdf)
- **IRIS PFHxA Table 5-4 — the sex- and species-specific clearances and DAFs (p. 5-14).** Animal clearance (L/hr-kg): male rat 0.163, female rat 0.383, male mouse 0.0894, female mouse 0.206. Against the preferred human CL of 1.84 × 10⁻³ L/hr-kg, DAFs (CL_H:CL_A) are **male rat 1.1 × 10⁻², female rat 4.8 × 10⁻³, male mouse 2.1 × 10⁻², female mouse 8.9 × 10⁻³**, each with an uncertainty range spanning the 16-fold human CL range. The **alternative** (allometrically scaled) human CL of 0.137 L/hr-kg would give DAFs of 0.84, 0.36, 1.5 and 0.67 respectively — i.e. **~75-fold different**. EPA's stated reason for requiring sex-specific DAFs: "Even though there is no apparent difference in CL between men and women, the sex difference in rats and mice means that the internal dose in male rats at a given applied dose, for example, will be different from the internal dose in female rats given the same dose... The HED for 10 mg/kg-day in male rats is expected to be different from the HED for 10 mg/kg-day in female rats because the internal dose in male rats will be higher than the internal dose in female rats at that dose." — [US EPA 2023, IRIS PFHxA, Table 5-4 p. 5-14](https://iris.epa.gov/static/pdfs/0704tr.pdf)
- **IRIS PFHxA — the "read-across" argument for preferring constant Vd over constant CL across species.** "To evaluate whether it is more reasonable to expect CL or Vd to be similar in humans as in experimental animals, values of CL were examined directly in humans for PFHxS, PFNA, and PFOA by Zhang et al. (2013b) and can be compared to those for experimental animals. **By comparing human and rat clearance for a set of compounds from the same chemical family, for which data are available in both species, a 'read across' can be done to evaluate the most likely case for PFHxA.** Note that PFHxS has the same carbon chain length as PFHxA (C6)..." EPA also notes "Kim et al. (2016b) reported renal PFHxS clearance of 0.76 mL/kg-day in rats while Kim et al. (2016b) and Sundström et al. (2012) reported total clearance of 7–9 mL/kg-day," and acknowledges "Uncertainty in the application of the DAF based on clearance remains, given that neither Vd [nor ...]". — [US EPA 2023, IRIS PFHxA, Section 5.2](https://iris.epa.gov/static/pdfs/0704tr.pdf)
- **IRIS PFBA (2022): the clearest statement anywhere that default allometry fails, with the arithmetic shown.** "using the common assumption of BW^0.75 scaling of clearance and standard species BWs of 0.25 kg in rats and 80 kg in humans, the half-life in humans would be predicted to be 4.2 times greater than rats. Given half-lives of **9.22 and 1.76 hours in male and female rats**, one would then predict half-lives of **38.7 hours in men and 7.4 hours in women. Although the value for men is in the range of results for humans, the value for women is much less than that estimated using the human data available from Chang et al. (2008).**" EPA then cites its own guidance hierarchy — PBPK preferred, then chemical-specific relative clearance, and BW^0.75 only "In lieu of data to support either of these types of approaches (U.S. EPA, 2011)" — concluding "because data are available to support a chemical-specific approach, it is clearly preferred." — [US EPA 2022, *IRIS Toxicological Review of PFBA and Related Salts*, EPA/635/R-22/277Fa, Section 5 p. 5-9](https://iris.epa.gov/static/pdfs/0701tr.pdf)
- **IRIS PFBA Table 5-3 — the divergence quantified (p. 5-10).** Animal CL (mL/kg-h): male rat 21.61, female rat 96.62, male mouse 10.10, female mouse 27.93. Human CL 4.95 mL/kg-h. DAF (CL_H:CL_A) vs DAF (BW^0.75): male rat **0.229 vs 0.236** (nearly identical), female rat **0.051 vs 0.236** (4.6-fold apart), male mouse **0.490 vs 0.139** (3.5-fold), female mouse **0.177 vs 0.139**. The 100-mg/kg mouse group was excluded from the CL average "as it was ~threefold and ~twofold higher for males and females, respectively, than the values reported at 10 or 30 mg/kg. **This could be due to saturation of renal absorption or serum binding.**" The HED formula is given explicitly: `HED_PFBA = POD_NH4-PFB × (MW_PFBA/MW_NH4-PFB) × (CL_human/CL_animal)`, worked as `9.6 mg/kg-d × (214/231) × (4.95/21.61) = 2.04 mg/kg-d`. "linearity in clearance values is only valid up to approximately 30 mg/kg-d and the DAF based [on it] should not be applied to higher PODs." — [US EPA 2022, IRIS PFBA, Table 5-3 p. 5-10](https://iris.epa.gov/static/pdfs/0701tr.pdf)
- **IRIS PFBA human parameters.** Human half-life **67.9 hours** by mixed-effects modelling accounting for clustering (mean 81.8 h, median 67.5 h from the raw 8 subjects / 20 observations), from Chang et al. 2008; **four subjects were excluded** ("both known female subjects (Cordova), one male subject from Cordova, and one subject from Cottage Grove") because "the resulting half-life estimate was then highly sensitive to this assumption" about pre-sampling exposure, plus one below-LLOQ time point. Human Vd **484.5 mL/kg** = "average of male and female Vd values in monkeys, 526 and 443 mL/kg, respectively, Table 4, Chang et al. (2008)." Resulting human CL **4.95 mL/kg-hour = 0.12 L/kg-day**. — [US EPA 2022, IRIS PFBA, Section 5 p. 5-9 and Appendix C](https://iris.epa.gov/static/pdfs/0701tr.pdf)
- **IRIS PFHxS (2025): a weighted geometric mean clearance plus an explicit sex adjustment.** Adopted human clearance **0.041 mL/kg-day** (men and older women), with **0.072 mL/kg-day for women of reproductive age** (= 0.041 × 1.765). "While the range of values in Table 3-4 represent a range of uncertainty of fivefold, given the number of estimates it seems unlikely that the true clearance in humans would be lower than the minimum value of 0.019 mL/kg-d from Zhang et al. (2013b). The weighted geometric mean clearance of 0.041 mL/kg-d is 2.2 times higher than this minimum and based on the overall evidence was considered sound for use in estimating human equivalent doses (HEDs)." The 1.765 factor comes from an NHANES analysis and is cross-checked against menstrual-fluid clearance: "if the relative clearance in women versus men is calculated by adding the rate of menstrual clearance from Verner and Longnecker (2015) [0.033 mL/kg-d] to the average rate estimated for men and older women, then that rate estimate is (0.041 + 0.033)/0.041 = **1.8 times higher in women**, compared with an estimate of **1.765** times higher clearance in women versus men calculated from the NHANES data alone." Crucially: "**this factor of menstrual-associated clearance will not be applied when evaluating dosimetry for nondevelopmental effects, such as thyroid perturbations observed in adult female rats. For those endpoints, the analysis effectively assumes that all women are effectively amenorrheic.**" Variability among women is instead handled by **UF_H,PK = 3**, "which is much greater than the factor of 1.765 being applied for menstrual-associated clearance." — [US EPA 2025, *IRIS Toxicological Review of PFHxS and Related Salts*, EPA/635/R-25/012Fa, Section 3.1 pp. 3-36 to 3-37 and Table 3-4](https://iris.epa.gov/static/pdfs/0705tr.pdf)
- **IRIS PFHxS half-life review, including an age-structured decomposition.** Li et al. 2018: 7.4 y in males (n = 20), 4.7 y in females (n = 30), ages 15–50. Li et al. 2022b: median 5.4 y (5th, 95th percentile 2.34, 9.29), n = 114, with age strata **3.01 y (1–14 y), 5.26 y (15–50 y), 6.41 y (>50 y)**. EPA's interpretation: "**The much lower apparent half-life in the 1–14-year-old group is almost certainly the result of PFHxS dilution into the growing bodies of the youth.** The intermediate half-life for participants 15–50 years of age may be partly attributed to the difference between males (mean 5.39 years) and females (mean 4.48 years) **which may reflect higher clearance due to menstrual fluid loss** for women in that age range. This difference of 17% in half-life is in contrast to minimal differences of less than 2% between males and females aged 1–14 and less than 3.7% between males and females over age 50." Olsen et al. 2007: 8.5 y (cohort of 2 females, 24 males at retirement). EPA also back-calculated Fu et al. 2016: "the paper states that Vd = 230 mL/kg was used for the estimate. Given a reported geometric mean (GM) half-life of 19.9 years in men, the corresponding clearance is 0.022 mL/kg-d." — [US EPA 2025, IRIS PFHxS, Section 3.1 pp. 3-29, 3-30](https://iris.epa.gov/static/pdfs/0705tr.pdf)
- **IRIS PFHxS on background-exposure curvature.** Li et al. 2022b's half-life rose with time since exposure ended (3.85 y using data within 1 y, 4.33 y at 1–2.5 y, 4.62 y at 2.5–4.5 y). "**Positive curvature in a serum time-course plot after a decrease in exposure (for example retirement)... is evidence of background exposure, as can be observed by examining Eq. 2 in (Bartell, 2012).** The differences among half-life values for the periods of evaluation reported by Li et al. (2022b), less than 20%, are not statistically significant, however." — [US EPA 2025, IRIS PFHxS, Section 3.1 p. 3-29](https://iris.epa.gov/static/pdfs/0705tr.pdf)
- **IRIS PFHxS acknowledges that even its own chosen approach is uncertain.** "the absence of a reliable pharmacokinetic model which can account for these factors and the likely differences in accumulation of PFHxS in humans exposed chronically versus in experimental animals during relatively short-term health effects studies creates uncertainty in simpler pharmacokinetic extrapolation based on clearance." Also: "The limited data available for neonates and children indicate that their clearance is higher than adults, so use of the adult value for estimating dosimetry in children should be health-protective of that population." — [US EPA 2025, IRIS PFHxS, Section 3.1 pp. 3-36 to 3-37](https://iris.epa.gov/static/pdfs/0705tr.pdf)

### Inferences

- EPA's IRIS program and EPA's Office of Water have **structurally different dose-reconstruction methods for the same chemical class**. IRIS (PFBA, PFHxA, PFHxS) uses a measured human clearance divided by a measured sex- and species-specific animal clearance to form a DAF, with human Vd typically imported from monkey. The Office of Water (PFOA, PFOS) uses a human clearance back-computed from a human Vd and a human half-life and applies it to human serum PODs. These are not interchangeable, and the two groups make opposite assumptions about where the reliable human anchor lies (IRIS: in CL and in cross-species Vd constancy; OW: in Vd from a steady-state epidemiological back-calculation).
- The PFBA female-rat case (DAF 0.051 vs allometric 0.236) is the cleanest single demonstration in the regulatory literature that PFAS clearance does not scale with BW^0.75, and the reason given — renal transporter and protein-binding differences, not body size — is the same mechanism OEHHA invokes for the human/rodent half-life gap. The IRIS PFHxS document goes further and compares clearance against species-specific GFR "with and without adjustment for serum protein binding."
- The EPA 2025 Bayesian paper's conclusion that **Vd scales but CL does not** is precisely the assumption IRIS PFHxA and PFBA had already adopted operationally (import monkey Vd, measure CL per sex per species). The 2025 paper is therefore best read as retrospective methodological validation of the IRIS practice rather than a new input.

### Gaps

- **I could not retrieve the full text or PDF of Dzierlenga et al. 2025** (PMC interstitial, Europe PMC 403/500, ScienceDirect paywalled; the PubMed MCP tool returned an empty `full_text` field). Only the abstract is in hand. **The per-chemical, per-species, per-sex posterior half-lives, Vd and clearance values from that paper — including whatever it says about rat vs mouse specifically — are therefore NOT captured.** This is the single largest numerical gap in this note and should be flagged to the report-writer as needing another retrieval attempt (possible routes not yet tried: the EPA Science Inventory record at `cfpub.epa.gov/si/si_public_record_report.cfm?dirEntryId=365692&Lab=CPHEA`, HERO, or institutional access to *Toxicol Appl Pharmacol*).
- **No final EPA IRIS review for PFNA was found.** Probing `iris.epa.gov/static/pdfs/0700tr.pdf` through `0715tr.pdf` returned served PDFs only for 0701 (PFBA), 0704 (PFHxA) and 0705 (PFHxS). PFNA values in the CSV therefore come from ATSDR 2021 and EFSA 2020 only.
- **The EPA 2021 PFBS toxicity assessment PDF could not be downloaded** (two candidate URLs returned 404 and 403). PFBS human and animal values were taken from ATSDR Table 1-1 (human 665 h; monkey 8.0–95.2 h; rat 2.1–7.42 h) and EFSA Table 15 (human 27.7 d, Olsen 2009).

## Q6. Agency statements on WHY human half-lives exceed rodent half-lives, and on rat vs mouse / sex differences — and what mechanism is invoked

### Takeaway

Every dossier that addresses the question converges on the same mechanism: **saturable renal tubular reabsorption mediated by organic anion transporters (OAT1/OAT3/OAT4/URAT1/OATP), modulated by serum-protein binding, plus enterohepatic recirculation** — i.e. an excretion-side difference, not an absorption, distribution or metabolism difference. OEHHA puts a number on it: human kidney reabsorbs **99.8%** of filtered PFOA.

### Cited Findings

- **OEHHA's quantitative mechanism statement.** "Han et al. (2012) compared available PFOA renal clearances (CLR) for different species and sexes to their corresponding glomerular filtration rates (GFR)... **Assuming the unbound fraction in blood (fu) as 0.02**, comparison of the GFR × fu (amount passively accessible to tubular secretion) and CLR (amount eventually excreted) values provides insight into whether the compound is actively secreted or reabsorbed during the renal filtration process. This comparison demonstrated dramatic interspecies differences, with **net renal tubular secretion in some systems (rabbit, female rat)** to account for very high clearance values, and a wide range of reabsorption efficiencies with other species. According to this calculation, **human kidney reabsorbed 99.8% of PFOA. This mechanism would explain the exceptionally long human T½ relative to animals.**" — [OEHHA 2024, Section 4.5 p. 43 and Appendix 6 Table A6.4](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **OEHHA's framing of the ADME locus of the species difference.** "**While absorption, distribution and metabolism of PFOA and PFOS demonstrate interspecies similarity, differences in excretion appear to underlie interspecies differences in PFAS kinetics.**" And on the rat sex difference: "Another interesting aspect of interspecies TK differences is the sex difference for the rat PFOA T½ values, with much faster elimination in the female rat... **Other species, including human, do not demonstrate sex differences in PFOA T½ values. The observation of sex differences in PFOA elimination in the rat allowed characterization of the underlying mechanisms of renal reabsorption involving specific membrane transporters.**" Also: "Renal reabsorption was found to be **hormone-dependent** in rats (Ylinen et al., 1989; Vanden Heuvel et al., 1992; Kudo et al., 2002). Multiple receptors at the basolateral and apical membranes of tubular cells have been implicated in this process (Kato et al., 2002; Kudo et al., 2002; Katakura et al., 2007; Yang et al., 2009a; Weaver et al., 2010; Han et al., 2012). **Renal reabsorption has been consistently incorporated into PBPK models of PFAS.**" — [OEHHA 2024, Sections 4.1 p. 38 and 4.5 p. 43](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **OEHHA's magnitude statements.** "Human PFOA T½ is ~200× that of the rat (male) and ~40× that of the mouse. Human PFOS T½ is ~40× that of the rat or mouse. **Such dramatic differences in half-lives indicate that interspecies dose extrapolations have to be done using TK considerations, such as clearance rates or PBPK models.**" — [OEHHA 2024, Section 4.1 p. 38](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **OEHHA on the chain-length gradient and the renal/faecal switch — the rat vs mouse comparison.** "Ohmori et al. (2003) found that in male and female rats injected with a single dose of PFOA or other perfluorocarboxylic acids, **increasing PFAS chain length correlated with longer T½ and lower renal clearance.** Regression of renal clearance versus total clearance for all data points (PFAS/sex combinations) produced good fit (r² = 0.981) and a slope of 0.48, suggesting that renal clearance would account for approximately half of overall excretion." In mice: "urinary clearance was 7.6× higher than fecal clearance with an i.v. dose... and 2.5× higher with a gavage dose (Fujii et al., 2015). **Similar to rats (Ohmori et al., 2003), increasing chain length of perfluoroalkyl carboxylate (C7–C14) resulted in progressively lower urinary clearance and progressively higher fecal clearance**, with approximately similar amounts eliminated via urine or feces for PFDA (C10)." — [OEHHA 2024, Section 4.5 pp. 42–43](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **OEHHA on enterohepatic recirculation as the second mechanism, with numbers.** Harada et al. (2007b) measured biliary clearance in four elderly patients: PFOA 1.06 and PFOS 2.98 mL/kg-day. "**These values are dramatically higher than the urinary clearances, and would also greatly exceed the overall plasma clearances given the kinetic assumptions used in that study. Therefore, the authors concluded that a large fraction of PFOA and PFOS secreted with bile would be reabsorbed via an enterohepatic circulation mechanism, and estimated that the reabsorbed fractions were 0.89 and 0.97 for PFOA and PFOS, respectively.**" In humans, direct faecal measurement failed in three studies (below detection) but "in one study both PFOA and PFOS could be detected in stool samples **following treatment with cholestyramine** in eight individuals with high PFAS body burdens (Genuis et al., 2013), providing evidence for the presence of enterohepatic circulation." In mice, Minata et al. (2010) found bile:blood PFOA concentration ratios of 2.8, 16.7 and 33.9 at low, mid and high doses, which combined with mouse half-lives "would also imply a high level of re-absorption of PFOA although the report did not address this question." — [OEHHA 2024, Section 4.5 pp. 43–44](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)
- **ATSDR's mechanism statement is about toxicodynamics (PPARα), not kinetics, and it says so.** "There is strong evidence that many of the adverse effects observed in laboratory animals involve the activation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-α (PPARα)... **There are species differences in the activation of PPARα; rats and mice are the most sensitive species and guinea pigs, nonhuman primates, and humans are less responsive.** Although humans are less responsive to PPARα agonists, they do have functional PPARα. This may explain some of the species differences in perfluoroalkyl toxicity. However, there is evidence that PPARα-independent mechanisms are also involved in PFOA and PFOS toxicity, including liver and immune toxicity; **it is not known if species differences exist for these mechanisms.**" — [ATSDR 2021, Section 1 p. 5](https://www.atsdr.cdc.gov/ToxProfiles/tp200.pdf)
- **EFSA's mechanism statement — transporter-specific, with Km values.** "Humans have a high percentage of PFOA renal tubular reabsorption due to the high affinity of PFOA for human uptake transport proteins such as **OAT1, OAT3 and URAT1**." Yang et al. 2010 showed "A chain length–dependent inhibition was observed, suggesting that PFCAs in general are substrates of OAT4 and URAT1, and to a lesser extent for OATP1A2... **These data suggest that OAT4 and URAT1 are key transporters in renal reabsorption of PFCAs in humans and, as a result, may contribute to their long half-life.**" On the hepatic side: Zhao et al. 2015 found human NTCP transports PFBS, PFHxS and PFOS (Km = 39.6, 112 and 130 μM); ASBT transports only PFOS; OSTα/β transports all three PFSAs; Zhao et al. 2017 found OATP1B1, OATP1B3 and OATP2B1 transport PFBS, PFHxS, PFOS, PFOA and PFNA, "facilitating the enterohepatic recycling process." Conclusion: "**All these transporters can potentially play a role in the accumulation of PFASs in the liver and contribute to their long elimination half-life in humans.**" — [EFSA 2020, Section 3.3.1.2](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2020.6223)
- **EFSA also ties the mechanism to protein binding and chain length.** "**These data suggest that shorter chain PFCAs, which are less bound to serum protein, are preferentially excreted in urine, whereas PFNA and longer chain PFASs are preferentially eliminated through the bile.**" Urinary clearance "decreased as a function of chain length, from 0.67 mL/kg per day for PFHpA to 0.005 mL/kg per day for PFUnDA"; biliary clearance ran the other way, "from 1.2 mL/kg per day (PFNA) to 11.22 mL/kg per day (PFTeDA)." Binding-affinity orders: albumin — PFHpA < PFOA = PFNA < PFDA > PFUnDA for PFCAs, PFOS < PFHxS for PFSAs (Hebert & MacManus-Spencer 2010); L-FABP — affinity "increased significantly with their perfluorinated carbon number from 4 to 11, and decreased slightly when the number was over 11... PFSAs have stronger L-FABP interactions than PFCAs with the same perfluorinated chain length." EFSA's bottom-line magnitude: human total clearances "are generally **50–100 times smaller than in rodents**." — [EFSA 2020, Section 3.3.1.2](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2020.6223)
- **EFSA on the rat sex difference and its contrast with the mouse.** "**Significant sex differences in the elimination of PFOA are observed in some species such as rats**, for which half-lives may v[ary]... The reasons for such sex differences in tissue distribution are unclear, but could be related to longer half-lives of PFOA in male vs. female rats (Pizzurro et al., 2019). The half-life of PFOA in male and female Sprague-Dawley rats was found to range from **3 to 14 h in females and from 8 to 12 days in males**." For PFHxS the rat sex difference is also large (29 d males vs 1.6 d females, Sundström et al. 2012, two-compartment for males and one-compartment for females) but **in the mouse it essentially vanishes**: "mean serum half-life values were quite similar between male and female mice (approximately 30 vs. 25 days for 1 mg/kg bw and 28 vs. 27 days for 20 mg/kg bw)." For PFNA the mouse sex difference also runs the *other* way from the rat's magnitude: "the rates of PFNA serum elimination were slightly faster in females than males, with estimated serum half-life of 25.7–68.8 days [F] and 34.4–228 days [M], respectively, depending on the dose." For PFOS in the rat, Huang et al. 2019a found "a half-life of about 38 days was calculated in both sexes." — [EFSA 2020, Section 3.3.1.1 and Appendix C Table C.3](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2020.6223)
- **EPA 2025 (CPHEA) names the mechanism directly as the reason allometry fails** — see Q5: "sex- and species-specific differences in active transporter abundance and PFAS binding affinity," and "differences in protein binding and renal and hepatobiliary clearance mechanisms." — According to PubMed, Dzierlenga MW et al. (2025), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.taap.2025.117336)
- **EPA 2024 PFOS invokes saturation of the same mechanism to argue that high-exposure cohorts underestimate the low-dose half-life** — see Q2: "non-linearities in PK that may occur due to the saturation of PFAS-protein interactions... due to saturation of renal resorption and increased urinary clearance in the study population." — [US EPA 2024 PFOS, Section 5.6.2 p. 5-19](https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-05/final-human-health-toxicity-assessment-pfos.pdf)
- **EPA IRIS PFBA invokes dose-dependent saturation in mice** as the reason for excluding the 100 mg/kg group: "This could be due to saturation of renal absorption or serum binding." — [US EPA 2022, IRIS PFBA, Table 5-3 footnote b p. 5-10](https://iris.epa.gov/static/pdfs/0701tr.pdf)
- **A second, non-transporter mechanism appears for growing bodies and for women.** EPA IRIS PFHxS attributes the low paediatric apparent half-life to **growth dilution** ("almost certainly the result of PFHxS dilution into the growing bodies of the youth") and the 15–50 y sex gap to **menstrual fluid loss**. OEHHA formalises the same idea by reproducing the Gomis et al. (2017) multi-term elimination constant, in which for an average 30–40-year-old woman, plasma elimination contributes only **37.3%** of total PFOA elimination, lactation **56.1%** and menstrual blood **6.57%** (PFOS: 54.8%, 32.3%, 12.9%), concluding "contribution of elimination via menstrual blood may not be significant, while PFAS loss through lactation would be sizable." — [US EPA 2025, IRIS PFHxS p. 3-29](https://iris.epa.gov/static/pdfs/0705tr.pdf); [OEHHA 2024, Table 4.5.2 p. 44](https://oehha.ca.gov/sites/default/files/media/2024-10/pfoapfosphgfinaldraft040524.pdf)

### Inferences

- There is near-total agency consensus on the *mechanism* (transporter-mediated renal reabsorption + protein binding + enterohepatic recirculation) and no consensus on the *numbers*. The disagreements between OEHHA, EPA-OW, EPA-IRIS and EFSA are all about which human dataset anchors the dose reconstruction, not about why the species differ.
- **Rat vs mouse is systematically different in the one respect that matters for sex-specific dosimetry:** the large PFOA and PFHxS sex differences are a *rat* phenomenon (driven by hormone-dependent renal transporter expression), largely absent in the mouse (PFHxS ~30 vs ~25 d; PFOS ~38 d in both sexes), and absent in humans. A dossier that averages rat sexes into one half-life (as EPA's PFOA Table 4-5 does, 2.23 days) therefore discards information that EPA's own IRIS program treats as decision-relevant (PFHxA and PFBA use sex-specific DAFs explicitly *because* male and female rats receive different internal doses at the same applied dose).
- The "saturation" argument cuts both ways and both directions are used by agencies for opposite rhetorical ends: EPA uses it to argue high-exposure cohorts *under*estimate the general-population half-life (favouring a longer value); the short-half-life literature uses the same non-linearity to argue the opposite. Neither OEHHA nor EPA resolves this empirically in the documents reviewed.

### Gaps

- No agency document I retrieved offers a **quantitative** interspecies comparison of transporter abundance or Kd values that would let the ~40–200× half-life gap be reconstructed bottom-up. OEHHA's 99.8%-reabsorption figure is the closest, and it is a back-calculation from measured clearance against GFR × fu with fu *assumed* at 0.02, not an independent measurement. The IRIS PFHxS document states it compared clearances to "species-specific glomerular filtration rate (GFR), with and without adjustment for serum protein binding," but I did not extract that comparison table.
- Whether the EPA 2025 Bayesian paper quantifies rat-vs-mouse differences specifically (as opposed to sex and route differences) is **not established** — only the abstract was retrievable. See Q5 Gaps.
